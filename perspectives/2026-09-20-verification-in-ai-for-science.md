# Verification in AI for Science: strong on the checkable, thin on the true

## Thesis

Ask a paper in this corpus how it verifies an AI system's scientific output, and it will almost always answer at one of four distinct levels, and rarely at more than one or two of them at once. There is procedural verification: does the code execute, does a number match a deterministic reference within tolerance, does a proof step follow from its premises. There is judged verification: does a rubric-scored LLM, or a panel of them, rate an output as good. There is epistemic verification: does an independent party, ideally without access to the system's own implementation, reproduce the result from scratch, or does the physical world (a wet-lab assay, a synthesized material, a working experiment) confirm it. And there is institutional verification: disclosure requirements, oversight bodies, publication norms, the social infrastructure that lets a community trust a result without personally re-deriving it.

The corpus's clearest and most consistent finding is that these four levels are not substitutes, and that the field's actual verification effort is badly skewed toward the first and away from the third and fourth, precisely where the underlying scientific stakes are highest. Wherever a deterministic or execution-based check is possible, papers increasingly prefer it and explicitly distrust LLM judgment: TPBench abandons its own automated equivalence judge for algebraic expressions in favor of literal Python execution because, in the authors' words, LLMs are "often unreliable at checking whether two algebraic expressions are actually the same" (slug:25tpbench), and ABench-Physics (arxiv:2507.04766) and CritPt (arxiv:2509.26574) both build grading around numerical tolerance and symbolic equivalence specifically to avoid a judge-reliability class of problem "relevant to open-ended benchmarks reviewed elsewhere in this corpus." But this preference for hard checks is only available where the target itself is bounded and already well-posed. The moment a claim is genuinely open-ended, novel, or research-grade rather than benchmark-grade, the field falls back on LLM judgment, and its own papers repeatedly show that this is exactly where judgment is least trustworthy: TPBench's GPT-4o holistic grader is shown to be specifically unreliable at graduate and research-level difficulty even though it correlates well at easier tiers (slug:25tpbench); PRL-Bench runs its entire frontier-physics evaluation through an unvalidated LLM judge with no human-agreement check reported at all (arxiv:2604.15411); ResearchClawBench cites LLM-judge bias as a known risk in its own related-work section and then relies on a single ungrounded LLM judge for every headline number anyway (arxiv:2606.07591); and PaperGym shows that four LLM judges agreeing with each other establishes only that they share a bias, not that the shared verdict tracks real research-plan quality (arxiv:2608.31119). Verification confidence is therefore highest exactly where the science is least novel and lowest exactly where it is most novel, which is the wrong way around for a field whose central promise is accelerating genuine discovery rather than restating known answers.

## What changed

There is no earlier snapshot on this topic to revise, so the useful comparison is against the flattened, single-axis view of "verification" that casual discussion of AI for Science tends to assume: either an output is checked or it is not, and the check is usually imagined as an LLM grading another LLM. The reviewed corpus displaces that picture in two ways. First, it shows a field that has already worked out a much finer typology than "checked or not." Toward Evaluation Frameworks for Multi-Agent Scientific AI Systems (arxiv:2603.26718) makes this the explicit subject: numerical comparison, step-validity checking via proof tooling, and confirming that a constructed object satisfies its claimed properties are three different kinds of "automatically checkable," each of which fails in a different, specific way, and treating "verifiable" as one uniform property is itself a source of false confidence. AI for Science: Reframing AI's Role in Discovery makes a parallel distinction between a "repository" system, whose learned content needs checking, and an "interface" system, whose retrieval fidelity needs checking, warning that conflating the two modes hides which verification problem is actually in play (slug:26aifsci). Second, and more consequentially, the corpus surfaces verification as a resource-allocation problem, not only a methodological one: the AI in Science survey finds that 89 percent of scientists who report time savings from AI now spend more than a tenth of that saved time auditing, debugging, or fact-checking its output, and 46 percent spend more than a quarter, with the tax reported as highest in the Life Sciences (slug:26aisc). That figure reframes the question "does AI do science right" away from a per-paper methods critique and toward whether the net effect of AI-driven acceleration, once verification labor is priced in, is actually positive, and in which fields.

## Evidence and counterevidence

The strongest local verification in the entire corpus is formal proof-checking, and even it has a documented ceiling that supports the thesis rather than undercutting it. The Lean formalization of quantum field theory reports that "any theorem the Lean kernel accepts as proved is proved, given whatever axioms were assumed," a categorically stronger guarantee than statistical or human peer review can offer (arxiv:2603.15770). But the same paper is explicit that this guarantee covers only formal correctness relative to a formalization, not whether the formal statement captures the intended physical content, the "formalization gap," and it reports a concrete near-miss where a definition "compiled" under the wrong convergence assumption before a team member caught the physical error by hand. The companion Seiberg-Witten formalization goes further and triangulates: an independent numerical oracle with no shared code catches real errors in its own checking apparatus, and adversarial cross-model review catches three statement-level gaps that had already survived 62 numerical checks (arxiv:2607.06379). Yet that paper names its own residual limitation directly: the cross-checking models (Claude, Gemini, Codex) are drawn from the same population of frontier LLMs used to generate the formalization, so independence is achieved across vendors, not across the category of AI-assisted reasoning itself, and a systematic blind spot shared by current frontier models would not be caught by this protocol. Even the best available local check, in other words, still needs a human or an out-of-category check to close the last gap between "logically valid" and "true."

Physics-as-verifier is the corpus's most-cited middle path between purely statistical judgment and full formal proof, and it is genuinely useful but domain-bound. How Do We Build AI to Push the Frontiers of Scientific Discovery? proposes encoding governing laws and symmetries as constraints so a discovery can be checked against what is physically valid before expensive physical prototyping, and explicitly proposes combining this with Lean-style formal verification of the underlying PDEs (slug:26daedsci). MIRA implements a version of this at the computational stage, using intrinsic reaction coordinate validation and DFT as a higher-fidelity adjudicator over cheaper machine-learned potentials (slug:26mira), and the BSM model-building framework verifies its deterministic symbolic backend against textbook results and a published reference paper with genuine, checkable agreement (arxiv:2606.21316). But the same physics-as-verifier essay names the cautionary case that motivates the whole exercise: a widely publicized claim of millions of AI-discovered materials that follow-up experiments substantially failed to validate (slug:26daedsci), and MIRA's own wet-lab closure step, the "ultimate verification" the paper frames the whole system around, is explicitly deferred to a future report rather than demonstrated here. And the BSM paper's local check is narrower than it looks: the deterministic symbolic mathematics is genuinely validated, but the paper reports zero evaluation of the natural-language orchestration layer that is its stated central novelty, so a fully verified computational core sits underneath an entirely unverified translation step from scientific intent to structured input.

Global, epistemic verification exists in the corpus but is rare, small in scale, and often the exception a paper highlights precisely because it is unusual. Denario's one reported independent-reproduction check, a domain expert reimplementing an astrophysics analysis from scratch without access to the system's own code and getting closely matching results, is methodologically sound, but it covers one of the thirteen featured end-to-end papers, and the domain-expert quality scores for the other twelve do not disclose whether scorers had a stake in the system's success (arxiv:2510.26887). Co-Scientist reports genuinely tiered evidence, from a self-judged Elo tournament on the same backbone that generated the hypotheses (n=203) up to real wet-lab dose-response and IC50 assays (slug:26cosci), but the tier that is actually independent of the generating system, blinded human expert preference, runs on only eleven goals. AutoResearchClaw's numeric-registry gate is a real defense against fabrication, but its own authors demonstrate its limit with the T10 case study: the gate can confirm that a set of measurements is not fabricated while remaining unable to tell that the result is scientifically uninformative, because a correctly logged all-zero, indistinguishable-condition output passes the gate cleanly (arxiv:2605.20025). ScientistOne's chain-of-evidence auditing is unusually candid that its own coverage is incomplete, stating plainly that "audit false negatives are unbounded" and that the true failure rate for every system it evaluates, including itself, is likely higher than reported (arxiv:2605.26340). The most direct test of this candor is DualityCert, whose statistical protocol (frozen analysis plan, preregistered replication count, Holm correction, a contamination predicate that quarantines rather than discards failures) is the most rigorous in the corpus, and whose verifier is scoped so precisely that the paper's own "CERTIFIED" label explicitly excludes several named physics obligations from what was actually checked (arxiv:2607.23614). And a mutation-tested rigor auditor built specifically to measure its own blind spots finds a 22 percent detection rate on one class of injected defect against 91 to 100 percent on four others, and reports that fabrication still occurred in reproduction runs even when the verifying agent was masked from the system's reported numbers, concluding that "structured artifacts reduce but do not eliminate hallucinated results" (arxiv:2604.24658).

A recurring, unresolved thread cuts across nearly all of the judged and epistemic layers: the verifier is very often drawn from the same population, sometimes the same specific model, as the system it verifies. ScientistOne's own audit runs the same Gemini model as the generation backbone, the audit judge, and the automated peer reviewer, a triple role the paper does not test with an independent-vendor judge (arxiv:2605.26340). AutoResearchClaw's cross-family judge design is a genuine exception, but its debate personas are all instantiated from one backbone with different prompts, so the diagnosed value of debate reflects prompted role diversity within one model rather than adversarial diversity across independently trained systems (arxiv:2605.20025). GeneBench-Pro is the sharpest counterexample to this pattern: it verifies its own verifier, building a dedicated ablation suite to confirm that plausible-but-wrong analyses are actually scored as wrong, then layering an independent human-expert review on top that catches an implementation error in a reference "correct" method that the automated grading alone had missed (slug:26genebp). That two-layer discipline, verify the grader, then separately verify the ground truth against independent judgment, appears in only a small minority of the corpus.

Institutional verification is the level with the least demonstrated evidence and the most explicit advocacy. Rethinking Science in the Age of Artificial Intelligence proposes mandatory disclosure of AI involvement, IRB-like third-party oversight of autonomous experimentation, and an explicit prohibition on AI systems making final peer-review decisions, but states directly that none of this is newly demonstrated by the paper itself (arxiv:2511.10524). AI Scientists as Engines of Discovery argues for institutional redesign around provenance and human-in-the-loop authorization, but its own capability claim for what AI can already do rests on a single cited demonstration whose selection process is not reported (arxiv:2606.22859). AI for Scientific Discovery is a Social Problem is the paper that best explains why this level matters at all: it attributes AlphaFold's trustworthiness in CASP not only to technical design but to the "social infrastructure" of blind prediction, standardized protocols, and sustained, open community participation, arguing that verification of a field's outputs is partly a property of the institution running the check, not only of the checking algorithm (arxiv:2509.06580). No paper in this corpus reports having built or tested that kind of infrastructure for an AI-for-science system; it exists here only as argument.

Against this reading, the corpus also contains real, if partial, counterevidence to the claim that AI-for-science verification is simply weak. A meaningful fraction of the reviewed papers volunteer their own audit's blind spots in exactly the detail needed to judge them, rather than asserting reliability by fiat: the rigor auditor's 22-versus-91-percent detection gap (arxiv:2604.24658), ScientistOne's "unbounded false negatives" admission (arxiv:2605.26340), DualityCert's explicit list of what its certificate does not cover (arxiv:2607.23614), and the Denario team's decision to devote a dedicated subsection to two detailed negative case studies rather than reporting only successes (arxiv:2510.26887). That level of self-scrutiny is not obviously lower than, and arguably exceeds, the disclosure standard of an average human-authored paper, which suggests the corpus's problem is less that AI-for-science verification is dishonest than that the hardest layer, independent global reproduction at scale, is genuinely expensive and has not yet been built for any system reviewed here, AI-authored or not.

## Tensions and objections

The strongest objection to this synthesis is that the local-versus-global gradient it describes is not specific to AI at all: human peer review is also weakest exactly on the most novel claims, replication is also expensive and rare in ordinary human science, and a bounded, well-posed numeric problem is also easier to grade than an open-ended discovery when the author is a person rather than a model. On this reading, the corpus is not documenting a special AI-verification crisis; it is documenting the same difficulty curve science has always had, now made unusually visible because AI-for-science papers, as a young and self-conscious subfield, report their own audit failure rates and judge-agreement statistics with a candor that human-authored papers in the same domains typically do not. AI for Science: Reframing AI's Role in Discovery's framing of much current practice as a "guess-and-verify loop" (slug:26aifsci) is explicitly presented as a description of standard AI-driven discovery, not a pathology unique to bad actors, which supports this more charitable reading.

A second, more structural objection is that bundling procedural, judged, epistemic, and institutional checks under one word, "verification," may itself be the analytical error, since arxiv:2603.26718's own point is that treating "verifiable" as a single property is what produces false confidence in the first place. On that view, the right response is not to ask whether AI-for-science verification is strong or weak in aggregate, but to insist that every claim in this literature specify which of the four levels backs it, since a claim that is procedurally verified (the code ran, the number matched) and a claim that is epistemically verified (an independent party reproduced it from scratch) are not comparable forms of support even when both are reported under a single "Verification" heading. The paper that comes closest to modeling this discipline in practice, GeneBench-Pro's grader-then-ground-truth double check (slug:26genebp), is also one of the few in the corpus operating on fully simulated, known ground truth rather than an open scientific question, which is itself evidence for the objection: the discipline is easiest to apply exactly where the underlying claim was already the least contested.

## Implications

For anyone building or evaluating an AI-for-science system reviewed in this corpus, the practical takeaway is to ask which of the four levels a headline claim actually rests on before trusting it, since the label "verified" in these papers covers everything from a deterministic execution check to an unvalidated same-family LLM judge. For research direction, the sharpest open gap is not better local checks, several papers here already do that well, but a verifier that is not drawn from the same population of frontier models being verified (the limitation arxiv:2607.06379 names directly), and a global reproduction step that is applied systematically rather than to one illustrative case out of many (the limitation arxiv:2510.26887's single reproduced paper out of thirteen makes concrete). For anyone weighing the economic case for AI-accelerated science, slug:26aisc's verification-tax figures suggest that any claim of net acceleration should be reported net of the auditing burden it creates, broken out by field, rather than as a headline speedup alone.

## Open questions

What fraction of a typical AI-for-science system's total output, not a curated set of highlighted examples, would survive independent, code-blind reproduction if applied systematically rather than to one case in thirteen. Whether a verifier built from a genuinely different paradigm than frontier LLMs (a classical symbolic system, a different training lineage, a human-only blinded panel at scale) would catch failures that current cross-vendor-but-same-category checks miss. Whether the verification tax reported in slug:26aisc actually erases the net time savings AI provides once fully amortized, and why the tax is reported as highest specifically in the Life Sciences. Whether any of the disclosure or oversight mechanisms proposed in arxiv:2511.10524 and arxiv:2606.22859 would change actual outcomes if implemented, since no paper in this corpus has yet tested an institutional verification mechanism rather than only proposing one.
