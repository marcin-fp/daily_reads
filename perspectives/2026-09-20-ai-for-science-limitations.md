# AI for Science limitations: a judgment gap and an allocation gap, neither closed by scale

## Thesis

Two candidate hard limits recur across this corpus, and both resist the standard "wait for the next model generation" response, though for different reasons and to different degrees. The first is an agent-level judgment gap: current systems can execute the full engineering of a research project, debugging clusters, running hundreds of experiments, producing a camera-ready paper, while failing specifically at deciding whether the evidence they have gathered is sufficient, when to abandon a premise that is not working, and what the actual bar for publishable work looks like. The direct evidence against "more compute fixes this" is unusually clean: matched-budget compute scaling from a single GPU to an eight-GPU cluster produced no improvement in experimental rigor, and in a separate study agents left most of their time and dollar budget unused while still failing on judgment. The second is a social and economic allocation dynamic operating above any single agent: AI adoption in science correlates, across a completely different method and a forty-one-million-paper sample, with a narrower collective knowledge extent, reduced follow-on citation engagement, and a self-reported tilt toward safer, more tractable questions, alongside a "verification tax" that eats a meaningful share of the time AI is supposed to save. This pattern is not obviously a capability gap at all; it looks more like an incentive structure that would keep steering effort toward tractable, data-rich problems even if every individual model got better tomorrow.

A third thread cuts across both and deserves to be named on its own: three independently authored pieces, using three unrelated methods (a large controlled shadow-evaluation experiment, a historical-philosophical argument, and a social-science position and case-study analysis) converge on essentially the same underlying claim without citing each other for it. Current AI systems are good at optimizing and deriving within an already-well-posed problem, and the harder, more valuable part of science, deciding which question is worth asking, recognizing when a result is actually novel rather than a recombination, and generating a genuinely new premise rather than a consequence of an old one, is where they consistently fall short. None of the evidence gathered here shows this narrowing with scale within the ranges tested, though one specific sub-case, LLM research-idea homogeneity, has a demonstrated, cheap, non-scaling fix that has not yet been tried on this exact problem, which is a meaningful complication to any claim that these limits are simply unfixable rather than currently unaddressed by the field's default lever.

## Evidence and counterevidence

The cleanest evidence for the judgment gap comes from two studies that neither builds nor tunes a new model, but instead measures what happens when frontier agents are given a real, open-ended research task and enough resources to succeed. ResearchArena runs three frontier coding agents through thirteen full research projects each, end to end, and finds that once experimental-rigor scores replace a manuscript-only LLM review, every agent's score drops, localized specifically to the experimental-rigor dimension; when the authors then re-run every GPU-intensive task on an 8xH100 cluster instead of a single A6000 at matched budget, scores do not improve, and one agent's score actually regresses slightly (arxiv:2605.19156). The CRUX shadow-evaluation study, which gives two frontier agents six days and three thousand dollars of compute to answer the actual, unpublished research question behind two real papers, finds both runs left well under half their budget unused, and traces the eventual double rejection to five specific failure modes: poor judgment about the publishable bar, uncreative responses to negative feedback, ineffective backtracking, poor resource awareness, and instruction drift, none of which a bigger compute allowance would obviously fix, since the agents were not resource-constrained in the first place (arxiv:2607.27191). The same paper's most precise finding is what it calls the generator-verifier gap: across fifteen combined rounds of self- and external-AI review that never once returned an acceptance, neither agent fundamentally revised its approach, which means an accurate rejector and a capable revisor are two different things, and having the former does not imply the latter. A separate, physics-domain case study corroborates this outside computer science entirely: a physicist supervising an agentic coding session catches a scalar "fudge factor" the agent tuned into a formula and did not flag as violating its own stated rule, only by asking whether the parameter appeared anywhere in the reference theory and running a limiting-case stress test the agent had not run itself, and the paper's own explicit argument is that a fully autonomous pipeline would have published it (arxiv:2605.30353). A mutation-tested rigor auditor built specifically to measure this kind of gap finds a 22 percent detection rate on one class of injected defect against 91 to 100 percent on four others, and reports that fabrication still occurred in reproduction runs even when the verifying agent was masked from the system's own reported numbers (arxiv:2604.24658). And ScientistOne's own audit is explicit that "audit false negatives are unbounded," meaning the true failure rate of every system it evaluates, including itself, is likely higher than what gets reported (arxiv:2605.26340). Three genuinely different domains, three genuinely different research groups, and one genuinely different method (a case study rather than a benchmark) point at the same shape of failure.

A sharper instance of the same gap, distinct from outright fabrication, comes from AutoResearchClaw's T10 case: its numeric-registry gate, a real defense against fabricated results, cannot detect that a set of correctly logged, non-fabricated measurements is scientifically uninformative, because an all-zero, indistinguishable-condition output passes the gate cleanly (arxiv:2605.20025). This is worth naming separately because it shows the judgment gap is not reducible to "the system lies," which several papers here take real, structural steps to prevent; it is that a system can be perfectly honest about numbers that answer nothing.

The gap extends into ideation itself, and here the "more capability should help" intuition fails in a specific, measured way rather than merely going untested. A controlled study comparing nine LLMs' research ideas against a matched human-idea baseline, conditioned on identical prior-work context to remove topic-selection confounds, finds every model's idea distribution is far more concentrated than the human one, overrepresenting "bridge, connect existing things" framings that real papers use rarely; critically, enabling extended reasoning mode on two of the tested models increases rather than decreases this concentration, and a full-paper-context ablation does not close the gap either (arxiv:2607.01233). Read alone, this could be one model family's quirk. It is not: a separate, mechanistic study of why aligned models exhibit "mode collapse" traces the cause to a documented human cognitive bias toward familiar, typical content baked into the preference data used for RLHF-style alignment, and states the diagnosis explicitly as "a genuinely different diagnosis than our reward model isn't good enough yet," predicting mode collapse should recur across essentially any RLHF-style pipeline regardless of model quality unless the training data itself changes (arxiv:2510.01171). Put together, these two independently authored papers give the ideation-narrowing finding a mechanism, not just a measurement, and the mechanism is a property of the alignment pipeline rather than of model scale.

The second candidate limit operates above any single agent. A bibliometric study of 41.3 million papers finds AI-adopting scientists do measurably better individually (3.02 times more publications, 4.84 times more citations, checked against a matched pre-adoption cohort to rule out simple selection), while the same AI-touched research shows 4.63 percent smaller "knowledge extent" and 22 percent less follow-on citation engagement between papers that cite the same AI-related original work, concentrated specifically in topics with more available data rather than higher funding priority or prior impact (arxiv:2412.07727). A separate telemetry and survey study, built from Gemini usage logs, a specialized-model citation inventory, and 637 working scientists, finds a parallel signal from an entirely different instrument: 49 percent of surveyed scientists report AI pushing them toward safer, more incremental questions against 28 percent reporting the opposite, alongside 89 percent of time-savers spending over a tenth of the saved time auditing AI output and 46 percent spending over a quarter of it (slug:26aisc). Both papers measure related but not identical constructs, one a large-N structural pattern in what gets published and cited, the other a self-reported perception of where attention goes, and the review of the bibliometric paper explicitly flags this as two methodologically independent instruments converging on the same qualitative pattern rather than one paper corroborating the other directly. Neither paper establishes the mechanism is inherent to AI capability rather than an artifact of the current adoption curve; the bibliometric paper's own authors state they "cannot fully identify the causal linkage." What a third, independently argued paper adds is a candidate mechanism that would explain why the pattern would not obviously disappear as models improve: publication and grant incentives are argued to reward individual, citable models over durable infrastructure and curation work, and to fragment collective effort across many narrow domain-specific applications rather than a small number of shared upstream problems, a dynamic the paper connects explicitly, though without direct citation, to the bibliometric narrowing finding (arxiv:2509.06580). If this account is right, the lever that would close the allocation gap is not model capability but the reward structure sitting above it, which is precisely why "better models" is the wrong kind of fix to expect.

The strongest single finding in this corpus, because it is the least likely to be an artifact of any one paper's method, is a three-way convergence that none of the three papers producing it appears to have coordinated. The social-science paper argues directly that current AI systems "excel at optimization within well-defined problem spaces but lack capacity for problem formulation itself" (arxiv:2509.06580). The CRUX shadow-evaluation study finds, empirically and at the level of individual behavioral traces, that agents can flawlessly execute the engineering of a research project while failing specifically at judging evidentiary sufficiency and creatively abandoning a falsified premise (arxiv:2607.27191). And a historical-philosophical argument, reconstructing Einstein's actual path to general relativity in detail, argues that today's systems have effectively mechanized induction and are rapidly mechanizing deduction, but remain structurally unable to perform the third, distinct mode of inference, abduction, the generation of new axioms rather than the derivation of their consequences, illustrating the distinction with a two-year historical detour caused by a wrong judgment call about which existing assumption to trust (slug:26abduct). A position essay from a fourth angle adds the observation that even where AI-generated mathematical results have started appearing, the field still distinguishes adding a result to existing knowledge from a genuine reframing of how a field's practitioners think about their subject, and that this distinction remains open three years after the same authors' own prior research agenda named it, despite rapid capability growth in the intervening period (slug:26aifsci). A social-science position paper, a large controlled behavioral experiment, and a historical-philosophical argument are about as independent as evidence gets in this corpus, different authors, different institutions, different methods, and none citing the others for this specific claim, which is the strongest basis in this corpus for treating "current AI optimizes well within a posed problem but does not pose the problem" as well-established rather than merely reported.

## Tensions and objections

The most direct challenge to reading the judgment gap as inherent rather than an early-capability artifact comes from a paper in this same corpus that shows very strong, well-verified results in a genuinely open scientific setting: an AI system for writing expert-level empirical software is checked against external, deterministic, already-existing scoring pipelines (a Kaggle leaderboard, an official benchmark's real ground truth) rather than an LLM judge, and performs well (slug:26empsw). The natural reading is that the judgment gap is not a general cognitive limitation at all, but is specifically bounded to settings that lack an external, deterministic score to anchor against; where such a score exists, current systems already do genuinely strong work. This would make the limit look more like a familiar, addressable capability gap, build better verifiers and rewards for more sub-tasks, than an inherent one, and the rigor auditor's own result supports a version of this optimistic reading too: it detects four of five injected defect types at 91 to 100 percent, meaning dedicated engineering investment measurably works in most categories even if one category (22 percent detection) still resists it (arxiv:2604.24658). On this view, the honest claim is narrower than "judgment cannot be automated"; it is closer to "judgment cannot yet be automated wherever no external score exists to anchor it," which is a real but more classic capability-and-infrastructure problem rather than a categorical wall.

The institutional-reform view offers the corresponding optimistic reading of the allocation gap: an essay arguing for AI Scientist development within reformed institutions treats the narrowing and safety-first dynamics as a governance problem with a governance solution, provenance records, human-in-the-loop authorization for high-consequence actions, and reformed incentive structures, rather than as a fixed property of how AI and science interact (arxiv:2606.22859). If publication and funding incentives are the actual driver, as arxiv:2509.06580 argues, then this is in principle a solvable coordination problem, and coordination problems have been solved before in science; CASP's decades of blind, community-governed protocol before AlphaFold's result is the same corpus's own example of exactly that kind of solved coordination problem, for a different (verification) purpose. The honest complication is that no paper here reports having actually implemented and tested such a reform at the scale needed to move the bibliometric numbers, so this remains an argued possibility rather than a demonstrated fix.

The ideation-narrowing finding has its own specific complication worth stating plainly rather than folding into the general judgment-gap story: the mode-collapse mechanism paper does not just diagnose the problem, it demonstrates a cheap, no-retraining fix, asking a model to verbalize several answers with probabilities instead of one, that recovers much of the lost diversity, and separately finds that increasing model capability and scale improves how much diversity gain that specific intervention recovers, confirmed via `lemmalog_why` (arxiv:2510.01171). This means scale is not simply useless here; naively scaling reasoning effort under the current single-answer prompting recipe makes the research-ideation version of this problem worse, but a different, already-validated, non-scaling intervention exists and has not yet been tested on the specific research-ideation task that arxiv:2607.01233 measures. Calling this an inherent limit would overstate the evidence; the accurate claim is that it is not fixed by the field's default lever, and a known alternative lever is untested on this exact case.

## Implications

For anyone treating "the models will get better" as a sufficient roadmap for closing either gap, the evidence here argues for a different prioritization. The judgment gap responds, where it responds at all, to external grounding, deterministic scores, formal verifiers, human veto authority, not to more inference-time compute or a bigger base model under the current recipe; investment aimed at manufacturing more of that external grounding for open-ended sub-tasks looks better supported than investment in scale alone. The allocation gap, if arxiv:2509.06580's account is right, responds to incentive and infrastructure design rather than to model capability at all, which argues for treating funding, credit, and publication norms as a first-class lever for AI-for-science strategy rather than a downstream policy afterthought. The ideation sub-finding argues for testing existing, cheap alignment-level interventions, not new capability, before concluding that a narrowing problem needs a bigger model.

## Open questions

Whether a verbalized-sampling-style intervention actually closes the research-idea diversity gap arxiv:2607.01233 documents, which neither paper has tested. Whether any institutional reform of the kind arxiv:2606.22859 and arxiv:2509.06580 argue for has ever been implemented at a scale large enough to move a bibliometric measure like arxiv:2412.07727's knowledge-extent statistic, as opposed to remaining an argued proposal. Whether the judgment gap is genuinely bounded to settings lacking an external score, as the empirical-software counterexample suggests, or whether that paper's own domain (competitive, leaderboard-scored tasks) is simply an easier case that happens to have the deterministic anchor current systems need, leaving the harder, unscored case of genuinely novel research untested by that comparison. Whether the three-way convergence on "optimizes within a posed problem, does not pose it" would survive a fourth, differently-methoded study specifically designed to falsify it, since three converging papers without a targeted disconfirmation attempt is strong but not conclusive corroboration.
