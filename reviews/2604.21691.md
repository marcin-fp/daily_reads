## What this paper is about

Deep learning works remarkably well but is still mostly practiced by trial and error, without anything resembling a first-principles theory that predicts why or how. A group of deep-learning theorists argues that such a theory is nonetheless emerging, and that it will look less like a piece of pure mathematics and more like a branch of physics — a "mechanics of learning" that describes the coarse, aggregate dynamics of training, much as classical, statistical, and quantum mechanics describe the aggregate behavior of physical systems rather than tracking every particle. The paper is a position piece: it surveys five converging bodies of existing theoretical work as evidence for this claim, argues for the name and research program "learning mechanics," relates it to competing perspectives (statistical learning theory, information theory, mechanistic interpretability), rebuts common objections to the whole project, and lays out ten open problems and advice for newcomers.

## Extended summary

The paper's central argument rests on five "lines of evidence," each illustrated with specific prior results and drawn in explicit analogy to a tool from physics (Table 1): (1) analytically solvable toy settings — deep linear networks (linear in inputs, nonlinear in parameters) and linearized/NTK networks (linear in parameters via Taylor truncation at initialization, nonlinear in inputs) — that reproduce real phenomena like sequential/greedy learning of the largest singular modes first (Saxe et al. 2014) and accurate generalization predictions via kernel ridge regression, though both remain far from realistic, feature-learning networks, motivating an "Open Direction" for toy models that are nonlinear in both data and parameters at once; (2) simplifying limits, principally the infinite-width limit, which bifurcates into a "lazy" (NTK, no feature learning) and a "rich" (feature-learning, achieved by downscaling the final-layer output by 1/width rather than 1/sqrt(width)) regime depending on initialization scaling — a dichotomy the authors generalize into a "Discretization Hypothesis" that finite networks are noisy discretized approximations of well-behaved infinite limits, with width/depth/learning-rate/batch-size playing the role of discretization step sizes; (3) simple empirical macroscopic laws — neural scaling laws (power-law test loss in compute/data/parameters, still unexplained at the level of predicting the exponent a priori), the "edge of stability" phenomenon in full-batch gradient descent (sharpness rises then plateaus near 2/learning-rate, now partly explained via curvature-penalized gradient flow), neural collapse, the "neural feature ansatz," and gradient-flow conservation laws tied to architectural symmetries via a Noether-style argument; (4) disentangling hyperparameters, exemplified by the linear scaling rule for learning rate and batch size (explained via an SDE view of SGD) and the Maximal Update Parameterization (μP), which enables tuning hyperparameters on small proxy models and transferring them to large ones; and (5) universal phenomena across settings — different architectures (CNNs vs. transformers, different diffusion-model backbones) converging to near-identical input-output behavior, shared power-law/Zipfian structure across natural data modalities, and growing similarity of learned internal representations across architectures, objectives, and even data modalities as scale increases (the "Platonic representation" trend), tentatively traced in some simplified cases to the law of large numbers or implicit SGD regularization.

Section 3 situates "learning mechanics" against five other approaches — classical statistical learning theory, information-theoretic/compression views, the older statistical-physics-of-learning tradition (from which this paper's own community descends), neuroscience-inspired approaches, and developmental interpretability/singular learning theory — arguing all are complementary to or would benefit from a mechanics of learning, with an extended discussion of a hoped-for symbiosis with mechanistic interpretability: learning mechanics could formalize interpretability's implicit assumptions (linear representability, locality, sparsity, compositionality) and explain how mechanisms (e.g., induction heads, grokking-associated phase transitions) form during training, while interpretability supplies learning mechanics with concrete, data-grounded phenomena to explain. Section 4 rebuts five stock objections (decades of failed attempts; current theory is too primitive for LLM scale; only high-level behavior matters; we need a theory of data, not of learning; AI will understand itself first) by, respectively, pointing to the recency of large-scale empirical phenomena and cross-disciplinary interest, citing existing useful "local theories" (scaling laws, μP, NTK-based data attribution) that help without explaining a whole model, arguing multiple levels of description (physics/biology/psychology analogy) are all necessary, arguing both data structure and learning dynamics are needed, and arguing near-term human-AI collaboration on theory remains the more likely path and matters for interpretable oversight. Section 5 lists ten open problems (unified nonlinear solvable models; a theory of data structure; a general notion of implicit complexity minimization; formal definitions of "features"; testing the Discretization Hypothesis; eliminating hyperparameters; predicting scaling exponents a priori; the mechanism behind progressive sharpening; principled explanations for why adaptive optimizers work; and precisely characterizing representational universality). Section 6 gives practical advice for newcomers (run frequent experiments; value simplicity and insight over technical complexity; value understanding over state-of-the-art performance; seek human mentorship; try multiple problems early; invest in fundamental tools like statistical physics and random matrix theory). No new experiments, datasets, or models are introduced by the paper itself; every quantitative result cited is attributed to prior published work, and a companion website (learningmechanics.pub) hosts further material.

## Field context

This is explicitly a state-of-the-field position piece from within the "physics of deep learning"/deep-learning-theory community, tracing its own lineage from classical statistical/computational learning theory and the older statistical-physics-of-neural-networks tradition (Hopfield, Amit, Gardner) through to contemporary infinite-width/NTK theory, scaling laws, and hyperparameter-transfer results, and it explicitly positions itself as advocating for closer coordination with, rather than competition against, mechanistic interpretability, information-theoretic, and neuroscience-inspired approaches to understanding deep learning. Two topics the paper treats as open frontiers already have direct empirical counterparts reviewed elsewhere in this corpus: its discussion of neural scaling laws (Section 2.3, Open Direction 7 on predicting scaling exponents a priori) is directly complementary to [reviews/2402.07043.md](2402.07043.md) ("A Tale of Tails: Model Collapse as a Change of Scaling Laws"), which derives a first-principles account of how scaling-law behavior changes when training data is contaminated by synthetic text — exactly the kind of mechanistic, first-principles explanation of scaling-law structure this paper is arguing the field is only beginning to produce; and its discussion of the Discretization Hypothesis and infinite-limit theorizing (Section 2.2, Open Direction 5) is thematically adjacent to [reviews/2509.14786.md](2509.14786.md) ("Pre-training under infinite compute"), which empirically probes the opposite asymptotic regime (a data-limited rather than compute-limited or width/depth-limited system) and finds standard recipes for reusing finite data eventually degrade rather than help — a concrete data point on the boundary conditions of the kind of scaling theory this paper hopes will eventually be derivable from first principles. Beyond these two specific connections, most of the paper's cited evidence base (deep linear networks, NTK, μP, edge of stability, neural collapse) does not otherwise appear as primary subject matter elsewhere in this reviewed corpus, reflecting that this batch of reviews otherwise concentrates on applied LLM capability, evaluation, and agentic-science work rather than foundational learning theory.

## Critical discussion

As a position paper, this piece should be judged on the coherence and honesty of its argument rather than on experimental rigor it does not attempt, and on that standard it does much right: Section 4's engagement with objections is substantive rather than dismissive, the "local theories are still useful without a complete theory" argument is a fair and historically grounded response to the scale-gap objection, and the paper is explicit and repeated in stating what remains unexplained even within its own five lines of evidence (e.g., no framework predicts scaling-law exponents a priori; progressive sharpening lacks a quantitative account for realistic nonlinear networks; the mechanism behind adaptive optimizers' advantage is unknown). The "Seven desiderata" and "tenets for getting started" sections are argued positions rather than empirical claims, and are presented as such.

The chief substantive weakness is a gap between the paper's title-level claim and the evidence marshaled for it: nearly every concrete solved result offered as evidence (deep linear networks, NTK/kernel regression, mean-field infinite-width dynamics, μP hyperparameter transfer) applies to idealized settings — linear or infinite-width networks, Gaussian data, teacher-student setups — that are explicitly and repeatedly acknowledged by the authors themselves to not capture the feature-rich, highly nonlinear, finite-scale transformer-based large language models that motivate the paper's opening framing and its safety/policy stakes in Section 1.2. The paper's own "Open Direction 1" concedes that no unified framework yet handles genuine nonlinearity in both data and parameters simultaneously — which is arguably the single most basic property of the systems ("LLMs") the paper's introduction and title are about. This is not hidden (the paper is unusually candid about what remains unsolved), but the accumulated weight of five partial, mutually-non-unified lines of evidence is used to support a strong, unqualified title-level prediction ("There Will Be...") rather than a more hedged claim like "components of such a theory are accumulating." A reader could reasonably conclude the paper demonstrates sustained, productive activity and real partial successes in a mature sub-community, without that activity yet constituting evidence that a comprehensive theory of the kind claimed is imminent or even assured, since each of the cited successes explains a narrow, often non-representative slice of deep learning practice and the paper offers no worked argument (only an analogy to the history of physics and engineering) for why these slices should be expected to eventually unify into the comprehensive, "seven desiderata"-satisfying theory it argues for. What remains solidly supported: the five described lines of research are real, active, and have produced genuine, verified (elsewhere) quantitative successes in specific idealized regimes; whether they compose into anything resembling a general mechanics of learning for realistic large models is, by the paper's own account of its ten open directions, an open question rather than a settled trajectory.
