Citation: Heng Wang, Jielin Qiu, Wenting Zhao, Cheng Qian, Liangwei Yang, Jiawei Han, Heng Ji, Silvio Savarese, Shelby Heinecke, and Huan Wang. Random Attention: Rethinking KV Cache Eviction for Efficient Reasoning. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03430v1

## What this paper is about

Long chains of thought make a reasoning model's KV cache grow until it becomes the memory bottleneck, so eviction methods keep a fixed budget of cached key-value pairs and discard the rest as decoding proceeds. Every prior eviction method scores each cached token by some estimate of future importance and keeps the top scorers. This paper tests whether that scoring actually matters by replacing it with the simplest possible policy — keep the prompt, then evict uniformly at random within each attention head — and finds it matches the best scored methods while being substantially faster to serve, because it never has to compute a score at all.

## Extended summary

**Method.** Random Attention has two rules: (1) never evict any of the prompt tokens (system prompt, chat template, question); (2) for every other cached position, draw an i.i.d. uniform random score per KV head at each eviction event and keep each head's own top-K independently (Algorithm 1: one `rand` call and one `topk` call per eviction). It is explicitly framed as both a deployable method and a null hypothesis: any signal-based selector that cannot beat it at matched budget and matched prompt protection is not extracting usable information from its score.

**Main results.** Across four models (Qwen3-4B/14B/32B, Phi-4-reasoning) and five reasoning tasks (MATH500, GPQA-Diamond, AIME 2025+2026 pooled, HMMT, LiveCodeBench-v6) at ~4x compression (~3x for LiveCodeBench), Random Attention is compared against SnapKV, R-KV, VaSE, and TriAttention, with full attention as the ceiling. Every reported margin is gated by a paired, problem-clustered percentile bootstrap (95% CI) plus an exact sign test. Random Attention is significantly ahead of a baseline in 31 of 60 baseline cells and significantly behind in exactly one (TriAttention on Qwen3-32B LiveCodeBench, traced to long code prompts consuming a large, variable share of the budget before any selection happens). On math and science tasks (MATH500, GPQA-D, AIME, HMMT) no baseline is significantly ahead of Random Attention on any model; on LiveCodeBench, SnapKV and VaSE fall far behind (SnapKV loses 20-35 points; VaSE collapses to 0.373 on Phi-4-reasoning) while TriAttention roughly ties or slightly leads. A compression sweep (2x-16x, Figure 2) shows Random Attention tracking TriAttention closely at every level while the gap to VaSE widens as the budget tightens.

**Why the signal doesn't matter (two controlled experiments).** First, prompt-protection accounting (Table 2): re-running SnapKV, R-KV, and VaSE with the prompt forcibly protected (rather than left to their own score) closes most of the gap between them — SnapKV, whose default score retains the least of the prompt, gains up to +22.5 points; R-KV, which already retains most of the prompt by score, gains at most +1.9. Once every method protects the prompt, the three baselines land within 2.2 points of each other and, on Qwen3-4B, still trail Random Attention by 4-6 points; a signal-free recency window with the same prompt-protection rule also comes within about two points of the best baseline. Second, a planted-fact probing experiment (a synthetic fact planted 1,536 tokens before a question, with fine control over which KV heads retain it) shows the reasoning trace is doubly redundant: single heads recover a planted fact rarely (best single head retrieves it in 3% of trials), but pooling across 2-8 heads is strongly superadditive (60% at 2 heads, 99% at all 8), and the shape/contiguity of surviving copies barely matters. A separate "stated once, never restated" passcode probe (Table 3) is the one case Random Attention fails outright (0% retrieval, log-probability -18.35, essentially gone), where R-KV's cumulative-attention score recovers it 83.6% of the time — showing precisely what a selection signal is still good for, and that this case is rare in real reasoning traces because models keep restating what they still need.

**Efficiency.** Under vLLM with PagedAttention (one H200, K=2048, 1k-token prompts, 32k-token generations, 128 requests), Random Attention serves 32-43% higher throughput than TriAttention (the only other baseline with a vLLM port) across four models, attributed to skipping the scoring pass entirely (0.30ms per eviction round vs. 1.47-1.64ms for TriAttention) at a point where compressions happen roughly 62k times per workload and each one is a whole-batch synchronization barrier. At matched peak GPU memory, Random Attention also fits the largest concurrent batch of any method tested.

## Learnings

The paper's core methodological move — before comparing eviction/selection methods, first equalize what each protects by rule (the prompt), then measure what remains of the reported gap — is a broadly reusable diagnostic for any comparison across systems that differ in more than one design axis at once; the paper explicitly credits this same confound (comparing methods with different implicit prompt-protection regimes) for inflated "random retention is much worse than scored selection" results reported in other prior work (Yuan et al., Liu et al., cited in §5.1).

The two-level redundancy argument (a value that matters is restated in the text and independently cached by every attention head, so a random per-head draw only needs one head to keep one restated copy) is a specific, testable mechanistic explanation for why simple baselines can match complex ones in exactly this setting (long generation, short prompt) — and the paper is explicit that it inverts for the opposite setting (long input, short output), where the object needing protection is the input rather than its boundary, making the finding scoped rather than a general claim about eviction.

The passcode probe (Table 3) is a clean template for finding the boundary of a "simple baseline suffices" claim: rather than presenting Random Attention as uniformly sufficient, the authors design a specific adversarial case (information stated once, never restated, needed much later) where it is known to fail completely, and show which prior signal (cumulative attention, R-KV's) does recover it — turning a potential weakness into a precise characterization of what remains open (how to recover rare once-stated facts) rather than leaving it as an unexplained residual.

## Verification

Verification is central to this paper's own argument, not incidental: every reported accuracy margin in the main results (Table 1) is gated by a paired, problem-clustered percentile bootstrap (95% CI) plus an exact sign test, with non-significant baseline "wins" explicitly called out as inside the noise (e.g., TriAttention's 0.3-0.6 point GPQA-D leads) rather than reported as a plain ranking. Ground truth for accuracy is exact-match on a final boxed answer (MATH500, GPQA-D, AIME, HMMT) or real test-case execution (LiveCodeBench pass@1), not an LLM judge. The planted-fact and passcode probes use a controlled, synthetic construction with a known ground-truth value, and the recall metric (a log-probability ratio normalized against always-kept and always-deleted controls) is a considered choice for the regime where plain retrieval accuracy saturates at zero and would otherwise hide graded differences. The efficiency claims are backed by two independent protocols (vLLM/PagedAttention and HuggingFace/FlashAttention-2 batched decoding) with a stated caveat that the vLLM comparison to TriAttention uses an unfused re-implementation of its scorer, which the authors flag themselves as likely overstating the memory-footprint gap relative to a fully optimized TriAttention implementation.

## Field context

This paper sits in the KV-cache-eviction-for-reasoning line of work it reviews in §7: H2O (cumulative attention), SnapKV (recent-window attention), R-KV (attention plus a redundancy penalty), VaSE (value-magnitude scoring with stochastic fill), and TriAttention (calibrated positional/trigonometric scoring), all evaluated here as baselines at matched compression budgets. It explicitly distinguishes its decode-time eviction setting (short prompt, long generation, cache grows during decoding) from the separate long-context line of work (long input, short output, cache filled at prefill) where a concurrent paper it cites (Garcia, 2026) makes a structurally similar point — that once prompt-boundary tokens are protected, the choice of score becomes second-order — but for the opposite object (the input boundary rather than the whole, short prompt).

Within this corpus, the closest genuine analogue is "[Sliding-window beats linear attention](2608.28444.md)" (Microsoft ASG, reviewed this batch), which makes the identically shaped argument in a different sub-area of efficient attention: essentially every linear-attention-retrofitting paper compares itself against a weakened variant of the true baseline (sink-free sliding-window attention) rather than the fixed, already-known-strong version (with attention sinks), and once that confound is removed, the simple training-free baseline matches or beats the more elaborate learned method. Both papers share the same diagnostic move — identify an unequalized nuisance variable across a field's comparisons (prompt protection here; sink handling there), equalize it, and show that most of the field's reported gap was actually explained by that nuisance variable rather than by the scored/learned mechanism being compared. No paper currently in this corpus works on KV cache eviction specifically, so this is a new methods-area entry point for the corpus rather than an extension of an existing thread.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claim — that the selection signal in prior KV eviction methods contributes almost nothing once prompt protection is equalized — is supported by a genuinely well-controlled pair of experiments (Table 2's ablate-the-confound design and the planted-fact probe) rather than resting on the main results table alone, and the significance-testing discipline applied to every headline comparison (paired bootstrap plus sign test, with non-significant "wins" explicitly grayed rather than silently reported as leads) is more rigorous than most eviction papers in this line of work, including several of its own baselines as characterized in §7.

The main limitation the authors themselves state clearly is scope: the claim is specific to decode-time eviction on short-prompt, long-generation reasoning traces, and the paper is explicit that it does not claim random selection would work in the long-context, short-output regime studied by concurrent/prior work, nor that a selection signal is worthless in general — the passcode probe (Table 3) is a direct, self-constructed counter-example showing complete failure (0% retrieval) on information stated once and never restated, which the paper reports rather than downplays. This scoping is a strength of the paper's own argument, but it also means the practical takeaway ("Random Attention is a reasonable default") should be read as bounded to workloads that resemble the five reasoning benchmarks tested (math, science, and one code benchmark), all under 32k-token generations and a single tested compression regime per task — a real but narrower evaluation footprint than the paper's framing ("across four models and six reasoning tasks") might suggest at a skim (`narrow-evaluation`). The one place a baseline (TriAttention) does significantly and consistently beat Random Attention — Qwen3-32B LiveCodeBench — is attributed in the text to prompt length rather than the selection signal, which is a plausible mechanistic account given the paper's own prompt-fragility argument, but it is asserted rather than independently isolated by a further controlled ablation the way the math/science prompt-protection experiment was.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the specific, mechanistically-explained claim that most of the reported gap between existing KV eviction methods on reasoning tasks is attributable to differences in implicit prompt protection rather than to the quality of their content-dependent scores, the redundancy-based explanation for why a random draw over the reasoning trace recovers what is needed once the prompt is safe, and the practical serving-throughput advantage (32-43% over the strongest baseline with a public vLLM port) from skipping the scoring pass, which is measured rather than merely argued for.
