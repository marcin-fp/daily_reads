Citation: Nielsen, S., Cetin, E., Schwendeman, P., Sun, Q., Xu, J., Tang, Y. Learning to Orchestrate Agents in Natural Language with the Conductor. ICLR 2026. arXiv:2512.04388.

## What this paper is about

Different LLMs from different providers are each good at different things, but combining several of them into an effective team usually requires a human to hand-design the workflow: who does what, in what order, and who sees whose output. This paper trains a small, 7-billion-parameter language model, called the Conductor, using reinforcement learning to write out that entire coordination plan itself, in plain natural language, for each new problem it sees: which of several much larger "worker" LLMs should tackle which sub-piece of the task, what exact instructions each should receive, and who gets to see whose work. The resulting small orchestrator model, despite being far smaller than any of the frontier models it directs, is shown to push a pool of state-of-the-art LLMs to new best-ever results on hard reasoning benchmarks, at a fraction of the API cost of existing multi-agent coordination approaches.

## Extended summary

The Conductor is trained starting from a Qwen2.5-7B checkpoint using GRPO, the same reinforcement-learning recipe underlying the DeepSeek-R1 reasoning-training paradigm, but repurposed here: rather than rewarding a model for directly solving a problem itself, the Conductor is rewarded for designing an "agentic workflow," a sequence of up to five steps, each specifying a natural-language subtask, which of several available worker LLMs is assigned that subtask, and which previous steps' outputs that worker is allowed to see. The Conductor's own output is never the final answer; instead, its designed workflow is executed by actually calling the specified worker models with their assigned instructions and context, and the Conductor is rewarded based on whether the final output produced by executing that workflow matches the correct answer (reward 1 for a well-formatted, correct final result, 0.5 for a well-formatted but incorrect one, 0 if the output can't even be parsed into the required structure). Training uses 960 curated problems spanning MATH, MMLU, a real-world reasoning benchmark (RLPR), and LiveCodeBench, and a fixed pool of seven worker models spanning both frontier proprietary systems (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4) and open-weight models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, Gemma3-27B, Qwen3-32B), converging in only 200 GRPO iterations with a small batch size and no KL regularization, which the authors attribute to the powerful worker pool sidestepping the exploration difficulties that typically plague RL training of small models from scratch.

Qualitatively, the paper documents the Conductor's strategies evolving over training from sound-but-unsophisticated task splits early on toward more powerful collaboration patterns later, including targeted prompt engineering per worker, explicit verification steps, and having workers share and critique each other's reasoning, all emerging purely from the end-to-end correctness reward rather than being hand-specified. Evaluated against the single best previously-reported results for each individual worker model (drawn from the authors' own re-evaluations, private implementations, and public leaderboards, which the paper notes may reflect undisclosed, proprietary prompting or sampling configurations for those baselines), the 7B Conductor sets new best-recorded results across all seven benchmarks tested, both on held-out questions from its training distribution and on three benchmarks never seen during training (AIME25, BigCodeBench, GPQA-Diamond), with gains on the hardest out-of-domain benchmarks (roughly 3 percentage points on AIME25 and GPQA-Diamond) that the authors compare in scale to the improvement between successive frontier model generations. A more tightly controlled comparison follows, pitting the Conductor against several existing multi-agent coordination baselines (Mixture-of-Agents, MASRouter, RouterDC, Smoothie, and a 5-round self-reflection baseline) all trained and evaluated on the identical seven-model worker pool: the Conductor outperforms every one of these baselines by a wide margin while using on average only about 3 workflow steps (well under its allowed maximum of 5, despite no explicit efficiency regularization), and, with the single exception of RouterDC, at lower inference cost than every other multi-agent baseline tested; a further efficiency comparison against simple 5x self-consistency/reflection scaling of the individual frontier models shows the Conductor achieving both higher raw accuracy and roughly 2x better cost-adjusted performance.

Two additional short finetuning extensions are built on top of the pretrained Conductor. First, finetuning with randomly sampled subsets of the worker pool at each training step produces a Conductor that generalizes to arbitrary user-specified subsets of available models: when restricted to only open-weight workers, this adapted Conductor still outperforms a frontier model (Claude Sonnet 4) by nearly 10%, while performance on closed-model-only subsets remains unchanged from the original pretrained Conductor. Second, allowing the Conductor to recursively invoke itself as one of its own "workers," conditioned on its own prior output and a specified maximum recursion depth, creates a new axis of test-time compute scaling; evaluated on out-of-distribution tasks where the base Conductor and its individual workers still show room for improvement, this recursive variant yields further gains (most notably on BigCodeBench, where the paper traces the improvement to the recursive Conductor detecting that GPT-5, initially over-relied upon, performs poorly on this specific benchmark, and reallocating subsequent recursive calls toward Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.5 Pro instead).

The paper's analysis section reports two forms of scaling and adaptivity. Comparing a 3B and a 7B Conductor trained identically, both converge to selecting the same distribution of worker models, but the larger model maintains a clear performance edge that the authors trace specifically to superior prompt-engineering quality in its generated subtask instructions rather than to different agent-selection decisions. Separately, the Conductor is shown to allocate more workflow steps to harder problems (e.g., LiveCodeBench code generation, which increasingly receives multi-step plan-then-implement-then-verify workflows over training) than to easier ones (MMLU multiple-choice questions, which converge to simple one- or two-step retrieval workflows), a difficulty-adaptive compute-allocation pattern the authors describe as emergent rather than specified. A large ablation suite in the appendix further isolates specific contributing factors: removing the requirement to write natural-language subtasks (leaving all workers uniformly prompted to "solve the user question," with only agent selection and topology left to the Conductor) causes a consistent performance drop that is largest specifically on the most constraint-heavy benchmark (LiveCodeBench); removing few-shot conditioning examples from the Conductor's prompt similarly degrades performance across all tasks; and, most directly addressing whether the RL training itself (rather than simply having access to a strong LLM as orchestrator) is doing the work, substituting the trained Conductor with an untrained frontier model (GPT-5 or Gemini 2.5 Pro) directly prompted with the identical orchestration scaffold underperforms the trained Conductor substantially, with the untrained frontier-model orchestrators shown to carry over specific, costly misconceptions (e.g., not recognizing that Claude Sonnet 4 underperforms at LiveCodeBench or that GPT-5 underperforms at BigCodeBench) that the trained Conductor has learned to correct through its reward-driven training.

## Learnings

The paper's most broadly transferable idea is a specific reframing of multi-agent orchestration as a pure reinforcement-learning problem with natural language itself as the entire action space (subtask text, worker assignment, and visibility list, all generated freely rather than selected from a fixed menu of hand-designed topologies or routing options), directly contrasted with an established line of prior multi-agent coordination work that instead learns a constrained classifier or router over a fixed set of pre-specified strategies; demonstrating that a comparatively small model can learn effective, genuinely novel coordination behavior (verification steps, targeted prompt engineering, cross-worker critique) purely from an end-to-end correctness reward, without any of these specific behaviors being hand-specified or separately supervised, is a concrete, reusable existence proof for anyone considering whether to hand-engineer a multi-agent workflow versus learning one.

A second, more mechanistically specific and immediately practical learning is the ablation-verified finding that the trained orchestrator's value comes from more than simply having access to a strong LLM to do the orchestrating: substituting an off-the-shelf frontier model into the identical orchestration role and prompting scaffold produces a substantially weaker result than the RL-trained model, specifically because the untrained model's assumptions about which worker excels at which task type do not match the workers' actual, measured performance, while the trained model has learned to correct these specific misconceptions through its reward signal; this is a direct, falsifiable demonstration that orchestration skill is a learnable capability distinct from general model capability, not merely a byproduct of using a sufficiently capable base model for the orchestrator role.

A third, more narrowly scoped but genuinely useful learning from the ablation suite is the observation that the value of explicit natural-language subtask engineering (versus simply picking which agents talk to which other agents, with a generic "solve this" instruction to all of them) scales with how constraint-heavy and format-sensitive a task is, with the largest measured drop from removing subtask engineering occurring specifically on the benchmark with the strictest formatting and correctness requirements (LiveCodeBench) and comparatively little drop on benchmarks answerable in a few sentences (MATH500, MMLU, RLPR); this gives a concrete, testable heuristic for when investing in sophisticated per-worker prompt engineering within a multi-agent pipeline is likely to matter most.

## Verification

The paper backs its central claims with a genuinely substantial ablation and control suite rather than relying only on the headline comparison table. Most importantly, it directly tests and rules out the most obvious alternative explanation for its results, that any sufficiently capable LLM could serve the orchestrator role without needing RL training at all, by substituting untrained frontier models (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro) into the identical orchestration scaffold and showing they clearly underperform the trained Conductor, with a specific, checkable mechanistic explanation (persistent, uncorrected misconceptions about which worker is actually best at which task) rather than only an aggregate score difference. It further isolates the separate contributions of agent selection versus subtask engineering (by comparing against a Conductor variant fixed to a single worker model, and separately against a Conductor variant stripped of its subtask-writing capability), and separately checks whether its efficiency claims hold up against a real, matched-worker-pool comparison against five distinct multi-agent baselines (not just against individual workers), reporting actual token usage and dollar cost rather than only accuracy. The paper is also reasonably transparent about a specific limitation of one of its own reported comparisons: it explicitly states that the "unconstrained" state-of-the-art numbers used in its headline Table 1 comparison may reflect "proprietary, undisclosed prompting and sampling strategies" for the baseline models, an important caveat disclosed directly in the text rather than left implicit, though the more tightly controlled Section 4.3 comparison (same worker pool, same evaluation harness for all methods) partially compensates for this by providing a cleaner, apples-to-apples result that points in the same direction.

Verification is comparatively thinner in a few of the paper's more qualitative, "emergent behavior" claims. The explanation offered for why out-of-distribution few-shot examples improve Conductor performance more than in-distribution ones (that OOD examples convey useful information about agent compatibility while resisting being lazily copied as an exploitable, reward-hackable template) is explicitly introduced with "we posit," and while the underlying empirical pattern itself (Figure 9) is real and reported with training curves across conditions, the specific causal mechanism proposed for it is not independently tested against alternative explanations (for instance, whether the in-domain few-shot examples used happened to be less diverse or lower-quality for reasons unrelated to their being in-domain). Similarly, the paper's characterization of difficulty-adaptive step allocation and other coordination strategies as "emergent" is supported by qualitative trace inspection (reading example completions, as shown in the appendix) rather than a more systematic, quantitative causal analysis isolating exactly which aspect of the training signal produces this specific adaptive behavior, though the qualitative evidence presented (the LiveCodeBench-versus-MMLU step-count contrast in Figure 8) is a real, directly observable pattern rather than an unsupported assertion.

## Field context

The paper positions the Conductor against two related but structurally more constrained families of prior work it cites and compares against directly. First, tool-augmented reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning (code-execution feedback, web browsing, multi-step tool use with step-grained rewards), which the paper frames its own contribution as extending: rather than equipping a single model with narrow tool APIs, the Conductor treats entire other LLMs as callable "tools" it can flexibly instruct via natural language. Second, and more directly, existing learned multi-agent coordination methods (Mixture-of-Agents, MASRouter, RouterDC, Smoothie), which the paper argues are fundamentally more constrained because they learn only to select among a fixed, pre-specified menu of models and/or hand-designed topological patterns, whereas the Conductor's natural-language output format gives it, in principle, unconstrained expressivity over both what instructions to give and how to structure agent communication; the paper's own controlled head-to-head comparison against exactly these methods on an identical worker pool is the direct empirical test of this claimed advantage. Within this corpus, the Conductor's core organizing idea, that a smaller model can learn to extract more value from a pool of larger, more capable models by combining their outputs more intelligently than any single simple aggregation rule, is a direct structural counterpart to two other papers reviewed earlier in this same batch: [2510.00931] (FusioN), which improves on Best-of-N sample aggregation via LLM-based synthesis rather than selection, and which the Conductor paper shares a specific point of contact with, both papers position their contribution directly against Mixture-of-Agents as the closest prior synthesis-based coordination baseline, though FusioN operates on independently-generated samples from typically one workflow stage, while the Conductor learns to design the entire multi-step workflow structure itself; and more loosely to [2509.14786] (Pre-training under infinite compute), whose ensembling-then-distillation results establish a related, independently-derived theme that intelligently combining multiple models' outputs can exceed what any single model or naive aggregation achieves alone, here demonstrated in the pretraining-recipe setting rather than the inference-time orchestration setting this paper studies.

## Critical discussion

The paper's ablation and control methodology is genuinely one of its strongest features: the "untrained frontier model as orchestrator" comparison directly and convincingly rules out the most obvious skeptical explanation for the results (that any capable LLM could do this without RL training), and the combination of a controlled same-worker-pool comparison against real multi-agent baselines, an efficiency/cost analysis with actual dollar figures, and component-level ablations (subtasks, few-shot conditioning, agent selection, topology granularity) together give the central claims a level of support well beyond a single headline benchmark table.

The clearest limitation concerns the paper's most attention-grabbing claim, the "state-of-the-art" results in Table 1 and Figure 1, which the paper itself discloses rest partly on comparisons against baseline numbers drawn from heterogeneous, sometimes undisclosed prior configurations (leaderboards, other groups' private implementations) rather than a uniformly controlled evaluation; while the separate, genuinely controlled comparison in Section 4.3 (same worker pool, same harness, explicit multi-agent baselines) supports the same qualitative conclusion and somewhat compensates for this, the paper's most quotable and prominently-featured claim (new records on GPQA and LiveCodeBench, prominently in the abstract and Figure 1) is the one resting on the less rigorous comparison basis, a distinction a reader skimming only the abstract or Figure 1 could easily miss. A second, more moderate limitation is that several of the paper's more interesting mechanistic and "emergent behavior" explanations, most notably why out-of-distribution few-shot examples specifically help more than in-distribution ones, and the broader characterization of the Conductor's learned coordination strategies as "emergent" rather than simply reward-optimized pattern-matching, are supported by plausible qualitative reasoning and illustrative trace examples rather than a more rigorous, independently-isolated causal test; these are reasonable, appropriately-hedged ("we posit") interpretations rather than overclaimed conclusions, but a reader should treat the specific proposed mechanisms as working hypotheses rather than established facts. A third, smaller point is that all evaluated worker models sit toward the frontier or near-frontier of current LLM capability (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4, plus strong 27-32B open models), so while the paper's randomized-worker-pool and open-source-subset experiments do provide some evidence of graceful degradation with weaker workers, the framework's behavior with a pool of substantially weaker or more narrowly specialized models (an important practical use case for cost-conscious users) is tested less extensively than the frontier-model setting that produces the paper's headline results.

What remains well supported after these caveats: a small, RL-trained orchestrator model can learn, purely from an end-to-end correctness reward and without any hand-specified coordination strategies, to design natural-language agentic workflows that measurably and consistently outperform both any individual constituent worker model and a range of existing, more constrained multi-agent coordination methods evaluated on the identical worker pool; this advantage is not merely a byproduct of the orchestrator having access to strong underlying LLMs, since untrained frontier models placed in the identical orchestrator role perform measurably worse; and the resulting system achieves this while using markedly fewer API calls and lower cost than most of the multi-agent baselines it is compared against, a genuinely demonstrated efficiency advantage rather than only an accuracy one.
