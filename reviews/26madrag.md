Citation: Keramati, A., Zhou, S., Mehrotra, S., and Warschauer, M. MADRAG: Multi-Agent Debate with Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Training-Free Analytic Essay Scoring. NLP4DH Workshop, 2026.

## What this paper is about

Grading student essays consistently on specific dimensions, such as ideas, organization, or grammar, is hard even for trained human raters, and simply asking a language model to assign a score directly is known to produce unstable, poorly calibrated judgments that cluster toward the middle of the scale. This paper proposes MADRAG, a fully training-free pipeline that combines two ideas: have a model argue both sides of whether an essay is strong on a given trait, an Advocate makes the case for its strengths and a Skeptic makes the case for its weaknesses, before a Judge decides; and give that Judge concrete, previously human-scored example essays spanning the full score range to calibrate against. Tested across several backbone language models, this combination gets a system requiring no labeled training data close to, and on some dimensions past, dedicated supervised essay-scoring models and even the agreement level between two human raters.

## Extended summary

MADRAG assigns specialized roles to LLM instances: for each rubric trait, an Advocate produces a single opening argument emphasizing only strengths, a Skeptic reads that argument and produces a rebuttal focused only on weaknesses, and a Judge synthesizes both arguments, each agent's approximate confidence (the log-probability of its first generated token, chosen over sequence-level confidence measures for being more stable across responses of varying length), retrieved exemplar essays, and the rubric to produce a final integer score. A Supervisor coordinates this process across all rubric traits for a given essay. Retrieval is implemented via a vector database of previously scored essays (embedded with a sentence-transformer model), with two retrieval modes: nearest-neighbor retrieval by overall semantic similarity, and, for the Judge specifically, retrieval of one exemplar essay for each possible score value within a given trait's range, so the Judge sees a calibration reference spanning the full scale rather than only similar-scoring essays.

The system is evaluated on ASAP Essay Sets 7 and 8, the only two of the eight ASAP sets with trait-level (rather than only holistic) human annotations, using Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) as the agreement metric, across five backbone LLMs (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-5-mini, GPT-5), with the Judge decoded deterministically and the Advocate and Skeptic decoded at a fixed, manually tuned temperature (0.7) to allow argumentative diversity. Baselines span two supervised trait-scoring models (FeatEng-RF, ProTACT) and two training-free LLM scorers (ZS-LLM, CSR-J), plus a reported Human-Human agreement reference. Under backbone-matched comparisons, MADRAG consistently outperforms the training-free baselines; notably, MADRAG with the weaker GPT-3.5-turbo backbone beats ZS-LLM on every reported trait, and MADRAG with various GPT-4-family backbones beats CSR-J, which the authors use to argue the gains reflect the debate-and-retrieval framework itself rather than raw backbone strength. MADRAG is also competitive with, and on discourse-oriented traits sometimes exceeds, the supervised baselines: on Ideas, MADRAG with GPT-5 exceeds ProTACT by 50% in Set 7 and 18% in Set 8, and exceeds the reported Human-Human agreement in both sets. Performance is strongly trait-dependent: gains are largest on discourse-oriented traits (Ideas, Organization), while on surface-level traits such as Conventions, all MADRAG variants trail the strongest supervised baseline by a wide margin, which the authors attribute to debate's holistic argumentation style being poorly suited to error-counting-style judgments and to limited value of exemplar retrieval for surface-form distinctions.

An ablation study on the merged essay sets compares six configurations: a single-agent Judge (SA), single-agent Judge plus retrieval (SARAG), independent per-trait Judges with no debate or retrieval (MA), per-trait Advocate-Skeptic debate with no retrieval (MAD), per-trait Judges with retrieval but no debate (MARAG), and the full system (MADRAG). Performance improves roughly monotonically as decomposition, debate, and retrieval are added, with full MADRAG performing best overall, particularly on discourse traits, though the paper explicitly notes that on some surface traits (Conventions), the retrieval-only MARAG variant can outperform full MADRAG, suggesting adversarial debate can introduce noise for fine-grained, error-based scoring rather than uniformly helping.

A second analysis specifically targets central-tendency (middle-score) bias, a well-documented weakness of LLM judges, by examining essay-trait instances where at least one human rater assigned an extreme (minimum or maximum) score, using Agree@1 (agreement within one point of the extreme score) and mean absolute error from that extreme as metrics. MADRAG achieves the best Agree@1 on both essay sets and the best or near-best error. The same ablation components disaggregate the source of this specific gain: retrieval is the primary driver, with the retrieval-only MARAG variant alone capturing most of MADRAG's improvement (75.3% and 85.5% Agree@1 on the two sets, versus MADRAG's 79.5% and 88.3%), while decomposition alone (MA) yields limited or even negative improvement, and debate alone (MAD) improves over the single-agent baseline but substantially trails the retrieval-based variants. The paper concludes explicitly that mitigating middle-score bias specifically requires explicit calibration mechanisms (retrieval), with debate providing only a secondary, more targeted refinement on top of that.

A qualitative error analysis manually codes 173 cases where MADRAG's score deviates by more than one point from a human average that the two human raters themselves agreed on within one point. Each wrong case is coded for reasoning quality (fully grounded, partially grounded, or ungrounded), a primary failure mechanism (six categories, including trait-boundary confusion, debate framing capture, spurious debate claim acceptance, exemplar-induced calibration error, rubric boilerplate collapse, and anonymization distortion), and which component (debate, retrieval, their interaction, or neither) plausibly caused the error, established by comparing the same case across the ablation variants. The most common reasoning-quality outcome among wrong cases is "partially grounded" (86.6%): the rationale sounds rubric-consistent but does not cite decisive textual evidence or make an explicit evidence-to-score link. The most common failure mechanisms are debate framing capture (32.7%, the Judge adopting whichever side's framing it read regardless of textual support), anonymization distortion (26.9%, where placeholder tokens such as @PERSON or @DATE in the anonymized essays are misread as genuine writing errors, exclusively producing under-scoring in the sampled cases), and spurious debate claim acceptance (17.5%, the Judge accepting an unverified, merely plausible-sounding assertion raised during debate). Component attribution finds debate implicated, alone or in interaction with retrieval, in over 70% of documented errors, while retrieval alone is implicated in under 9%. Under-scoring dominates overall (146 of 173 wrong cases), while over-scoring cases are comparatively more associated with spurious claim acceptance and generic, template-like rationales.

The paper's stated limitations include: results are based on only two of ASAP's eight essay sets, both narrative prompts from middle- and high-school settings, so conclusions may not transfer to other genres, grade levels, languages, or rubric structures; anonymization placeholders introduce a bias specifically distorting surface-trait (Conventions) scoring that is unrelated to actual writing quality; the framework is substantially more computationally expensive at inference time than single-agent prompting or supervised scoring, since each trait evaluation requires multiple LLM calls plus retrieval; reported results are from a single run per configuration rather than an average over repeated trials, explicitly due to the compute cost of re-running the full pipeline, so numbers should be read as one specific instantiation's outcome rather than a mean or variance estimate; and while MADRAG is training-free in the sense of requiring no parameter updates, it is not data-free, since retrieval presupposes a bank of manually scored essays, though the paper argues this required labeled data is much smaller than what training a supervised scorer would need.

## Learnings

In a debate-structured LLM evaluation pipeline, the two dominant, self-diagnosed failure modes, together accounting for roughly half of all wrong judgments in this study, were the Judge being captured by whichever side's framing it read regardless of actual textual support, and the Judge accepting an unverified, merely plausible-sounding claim raised during debate. A persuasive-sounding argument in a debate transcript is not the same as a text-grounded one, and a judge synthesizing debate arguments needs some independent mechanism for checking groundedness, not only a sense of which side argued more persuasively.

Disaggregating a multi-mechanism evaluation system's gains by targeted ablation revealed that its two headline components are not interchangeable or equally responsible for its improvements: retrieval was overwhelmingly the primary driver of reducing central-tendency bias specifically, while debate contributed comparatively little to that particular problem and instead helped more with discourse-level trait quality, and on surface-level, error-counting-style traits, debate's adversarial framing sometimes actively hurt performance relative to retrieval alone. A system combining two plausible mechanisms should be checked component by component against the specific failure mode each is meant to address, since "both together perform best on average" can still mask one component being net-negative for a specific sub-problem or category.

A data artifact unrelated to the actual quality being measured, here, anonymization placeholders substituted into student essays before public release, can be systematically misread by an LLM as a genuine defect in exactly the trait most sensitive to surface-level correctness, and was the second most common documented failure mechanism in this study, exclusively producing under-scoring in the sampled cases. This is a specific, transferable caution for any dataset that anonymizes or otherwise systematically alters source text before it is used to evaluate a system meant to judge that text's surface-level quality.

## Verification

Verification appears in a narrow but genuine sense in this paper. The confidence-proxy design (first-token log-probability, chosen over sequence-level aggregation after the latter was found to compress into an uninformative narrow range in preliminary experiments) is a small, disclosed engineering choice feeding the Judge's synthesis step, though it is not independently validated against ground truth on its own. More substantively, the paper's qualitative error analysis functions as a genuine post-hoc verification exercise: rather than reporting only an aggregate agreement score, the authors manually code every wrong case along three separate axes (reasoning-groundedness, failure mechanism, and component attribution via cross-ablation comparison), a disciplined effort to understand why the system fails rather than only how often. This coding scheme, however, is not itself validated for reliability in the paper: no inter-annotator agreement is reported for the reasoning-quality, failure-mechanism, or component-attribution codes used in this analysis, so the qualitative findings, while systematic and specific, rest on a single annotation pass without a reported reliability check of their own.

## Field context

This paper sits in the LLM-as-judge and multi-agent-debate literature applied specifically to automated essay scoring, directly engaging documented weaknesses of naive LLM-as-judge scoring such as middle-score bias, prompt sensitivity, and poor calibration. It builds on the general multi-agent-debate literature (citing ChatEval as the originating application of a "jury" of LLMs to text evaluation) and positions itself against two directly comparable, contemporaneous AES-specific multi-agent systems, MAGIC (trait-specialist agents synthesized by an orchestrator) and CAFES (a reflective critic-revises-scorer workflow), differentiating MADRAG by its combination of adversarial (not purely collaborative) debate with rubric-conditioned, score-spanning exemplar retrieval. It also engages a comparative-scoring alternative (LCES, pairwise ranking rather than absolute scoring) and explicitly frames MADRAG's absolute-scoring-with-calibration approach as addressing that method's quadratic scalability limitation. Within this corpus, this paper's central, self-documented finding, that a debate transcript's persuasive framing can capture a judge's decision independent of actual textual grounding, and that an unverified claim raised in debate can simply be accepted, is a domain-specific instance of the same concern already visible in this corpus's reviews of 24selfcor (same-model correction is unreliable) and 25mcrit (a candidate judgment needs validation by its downstream consequences, not merely by how persuasively it was argued, which motivated that paper's MARV mechanism). MADRAG's own qualitative results function as an independent, real-world corroboration of that broader pattern, specifically in the context of debate-structured multi-agent judging.

## Critical discussion

A structural point the paper does not foreground deserves emphasis: "multi-agent debate" here means the same backbone LLM instantiated three times under three different role prompts (Advocate, Skeptic, Judge) within a given run, not genuinely independent models or independently trained critics. Same-model role-play debate lacks the independence that would let one instance's biases be checked by a genuinely different perspective, and this is not a merely hypothetical concern: the paper's own qualitative error analysis finds that "debate framing capture" and "spurious debate claim acceptance" together account for over half of all documented wrong cases, with debate implicated alone or in interaction with retrieval in over 70% of errors versus retrieval alone in under 9% (same-family-judge). The paper's own numbers make a stronger case for this concern than an external critique would need to construct independently, and crediting this self-disclosure is itself a strength of the analysis, even though the framing of "multi-agent debate" in the abstract and introduction does not draw out the same-backbone caveat explicitly.

The comparison to "Human-Human agreement" as a ceiling deserves a caveat the paper does not fully draw out: MADRAG's agreement is measured against the rounded average of the human raters' scores, a more stable target than any single human rater trying to match another individual rater, so exceeding "Human-Human agreement" shows MADRAG tracks the averaged consensus score more closely than one human tracks another, which is a genuine and interesting result, but is not equivalent to MADRAG being more consistent than human judgment in an unqualified sense, since it faces a different, less noisy target than either individual human does. The paper's framing, that the result indicates the framework "yields more consistent content judgments than individual human raters," states this somewhat more strongly than the comparison structure fully supports (overclaim). Relatedly, the paper's own appendix discloses that final prompts were reached through "an extensive, iterative prompt-engineering process involving multiple rounds of pilot runs and refinements," necessarily conducted using the same ASAP Sets 7 and 8 data the headline results are reported on; this is a standard and disclosed practice, but it creates a mild tension with the "training-free" framing set up in contrast to supervised methods that "require labeled data for each new prompt and rubric," since prompts iteratively tuned against this specific essay set and rubric structure carry some of the same set-specific adaptation that framing is meant to distinguish itself from, even though no model parameters were updated; this is consistent with, and reinforces, the paper's own disclosed limitation that results may not transfer to other genres or rubric structures. Finally, the paper is candid that its headline numbers are single-run results without repeated-trial variance, a reasonable trade-off given the framework's per-trait multi-call cost, but it does mean precise comparisons (such as "exceeds ProTACT by 50%") should be read as one instantiation's outcome rather than an estimate with a known margin of error (missing-uncertainty).

What remains well supported: the backbone-matched comparisons against training-free baselines, where MADRAG with a weaker backbone still beats a stronger-backbone baseline using simpler prompting, credibly isolate the framework's contribution from raw model capability, directly addressing a common weak-baseline concern. The component-ablation study is thorough and its conclusions are appropriately disaggregated rather than crediting the whole system uniformly, correctly identifying retrieval as the primary driver of middle-score-bias mitigation specifically while debate contributes more to discourse-trait quality and can even hurt surface-trait scoring. The qualitative error analysis, whatever its own unreported reliability limitations, is a genuine, systematic effort to explain failures mechanistically, and its central finding, that debate-driven ungroundedness is the dominant error source, is a valuable and appropriately reported caution about the paper's own central design choice.

## Relevance to us

This paper is directly relevant to the "independent or stronger critics (same-model self-critique is weak)" concern already in our own framing, and to our thinking about multi-agent verification design generally, echoed in this corpus's review of 25mcrit. MADRAG is a clean, real-world case study of exactly the risk that framing already flags: dressing up a single model's role-played "debate" with itself in adversarial framing does not, by itself, produce genuinely independent scrutiny, and the paper's own failure analysis shows the dominant error modes are precisely the ones predicted by that structural limitation (a persuasive framing capturing the judge, or an unverified claim accepted at face value) rather than a knowledge gap. This is directly relevant to any critique or verification component we build using multiple LLM "roles": if the underlying model is the same across roles, the resulting exchange should be understood as producing structured, differently framed outputs from one model's existing knowledge and biases, not as introducing an independent check, and any judge synthesizing such an exchange needs an additional, non-persuasion-based grounding mechanism (an executable check, a retrieved reference fact, or a deterministic verifier) rather than relying on the debate structure alone to surface the truth, directly echoing 25mcrit's point that a judgment should be validated by its downstream consequences rather than merely by how persuasively it was framed. The paper's own strongest, most reliable calibration gains came specifically from retrieval (grounding a judgment against concrete external reference examples) rather than from debate, which is a useful, disaggregated data point when weighing whether to invest in an adversarial multi-persona critique mechanism versus a retrieval-grounded calibration mechanism for our own evaluation tooling: this paper's ablations suggest the latter may be the more reliable lever for reducing a specific, well-defined bias, while the former is better suited to improving holistic reasoning quality and carries a real risk of adding persuasion-driven noise rather than genuine scrutiny.
