Citation: Zhang, J., Yu, S., Chong, D., Sicilia, A., Tomz, M.R., Manning, C.D., Shi, W. Verbalized Sampling: How to Mitigate Mode Collapse and Unlock LLM Diversity. ICML 2026. arXiv:2510.01171.

## What this paper is about

Aligned LLMs (models fine-tuned with RLHF or similar preference-based methods) are well known to produce noticeably less diverse output than their base, pre-alignment counterparts, a phenomenon called mode collapse: ask an aligned model for a joke about coffee many times and it will keep returning near-identical answers, even though the underlying base model clearly "knows" many different jokes. Prior explanations blame this on algorithmic details of the alignment process itself (imperfect reward models, majority-favoring optimization). This paper instead traces the root cause to the human preference data used to train those reward models: people have a well-documented cognitive tendency to prefer familiar, predictable, easy-to-process text over unusual text, independent of whether the unusual text is actually worse. The paper formalizes this "typicality bias," shows mathematically why it forces alignment toward a narrow mode whenever many responses are roughly equally good, and then proposes a strikingly simple fix that needs no retraining at all: instead of asking a model for one answer, ask it to verbalize several answers together with their probabilities, which the paper shows recovers much of the diversity lost during alignment.

## Extended summary

The paper's theoretical contribution starts by formalizing typicality as the log-probability of a response under a reference (base) model, reasoning that since the base model was trained to maximize likelihood on a huge text corpus, this log-probability is a natural proxy for how "typical" a response is. It then models the observed reward in a Bradley-Terry preference framework as a weighted sum of true task utility and this typicality term, and fits this model on 6,874 correctness-matched response pairs from the HelpSteer preference dataset using two different base models (Llama-3.1-405B and GLM-4.5) to compute the typicality term; the fitted typicality weight is significantly positive in both cases (α≈0.57 and 0.65, both p<10⁻¹⁴), meaning human annotators systematically favor more typical responses even after correctness is held fixed. The paper checks that this is not simply picking up surface-level readability or length effects by adding controls for token count, Flesch-Kincaid readability, type-token ratio, and sentence length; the estimated effect shrinks somewhat but remains significant, leading the authors to describe base-model log-probability as "an imperfect but useful proxy for a typicality-like preference signal" rather than a pure, uncontaminated measure of psychological typicality, an appropriately hedged interpretation rather than an overclaim. Plugging this typicality-augmented reward into the standard closed-form RLHF optimum (under a KL-regularized Bradley-Terry objective) yields a policy that is a temperature-sharpened version of the reference policy; the paper proves that whenever a subset of responses have approximately equal true task utility (a common situation in creative writing, dialogue, and other open-ended domains where "many right answers" exist), any positive typicality weight causes the aligned policy's probability mass on that subset to concentrate on the reference model's own single most likely (most typical) response as the effective temperature parameter grows, formally establishing mode collapse as a consequence of typicality bias in the data even under an otherwise well-specified reward model and optimization procedure.

Building on this, the paper observes that different prompt framings elicit different notions of what is "typical" or "representative": a prompt asking for a single instance collapses to the reference model's single most probable answer (proven as Claim/Theorem 1), a prompt asking for a fixed list of several answers collapses recursively to a "bestseller list" of the top-k most probable answers, which is still narrow (Claim 2), but a prompt asking the model to verbalize a probability distribution over several answers breaks the flat-reward assumption that drives collapse in the first place: annotators (and by extension the reward model trained on their preferences) exhibit a competing preference for representative, appropriately high-entropy distributions when explicitly asked for a distribution rather than a single item, which the paper formalizes as Claim/Theorem 3 and separately validates empirically (a "representativeness heuristic" check, and a "typical set" argument for why representative distributions are inherently diverse). This is the theoretical basis for Verbalized Sampling (VS): rather than prompting "tell me a joke about coffee," the model is prompted "generate 5 jokes about coffee and their corresponding probabilities." The paper defines several concrete variants under a matched total-generation budget N: VS-Standard (one call producing k candidates with probabilities), VS-CoT (chain-of-thought reasoning before listing the distribution), and VS-Multi (repeated multi-turn calls each eliciting k more candidates), compared against instance-level baselines (Direct, CoT) and list-level baselines (Sequence, which asks for k items without probabilities, and Multi-Turn, which elicits one item per conversation turn).

Across a wide model suite (GPT-4.1 and GPT-4.1-mini, Gemini-2.5-Pro and Flash, Claude-3.7-Sonnet and Claude-4-Sonnet, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, Qwen3-235B-A22B, and reasoning models o3 and DeepSeek-R1), VS-Standard consistently and significantly (one-tailed t-tests, p<0.05 to p<0.001 depending on task/baseline) outperforms all baselines on semantic diversity (measured as one minus mean pairwise embedding cosine similarity) across three creative-writing tasks (poem continuation, story generation, joke writing), with VS-CoT and VS-Multi improving diversity further still; on the poem task, VS-CoT reaches the Pareto frontier of the diversity-quality trade-off (quality judged by Claude-3.7-Sonnet using established creative-writing and humor rubrics), meaning it achieves higher diversity without a compensating drop in quality, unlike the Sequence and VS-Standard variants, which show a modest quality cost. A genuinely notable, separately-confirmed finding is an "emergent trend" in which larger models within the same family (GPT-4.1 vs. GPT-4.1-mini; Gemini-2.5-Pro vs. Flash) gain 1.5-2x more diversity from VS than their smaller counterparts, and this scaling also extends to quality: while prior work found complex, structured prompts impose a "cognitive burden" that hurts smaller models, VS-CoT and VS-Multi actually improve quality on larger models rather than degrading it. VS also supports direct diversity tuning by asking for responses below a stated probability threshold in the prompt, with diversity increasing monotonically as the threshold is lowered, a controllable knob the fixed-format baselines do not offer. An ablation across the post-training pipeline of an open Tulu-70B model shows that direct prompting suffers progressively worse mode collapse through SFT, then DPO, then RLVR (a 182.6% total diversity drop relative to the base model by the final stage), while VS maintains substantially higher diversity throughout all stages, directly supporting the paper's claim that VS counteracts collapse introduced across the whole alignment pipeline, not just one stage. A human study on Prolific (90 annotators, 30 per creative-writing task, 4-point Likert scales, moderate-to-high inter-annotator agreement of 0.49-0.87) independently corroborates the automatic diversity metric, with VS-Standard rated more diverse than Direct and Sequence on all three tasks while maintaining comparable quality win-rates.

Beyond creative writing, VS is tested on multi-turn persuasive-dialogue simulation (the PersuasionForGood charity-donation corpus), where VS-simulated donation-amount distributions align more closely with real human donation behavior than direct prompting (assessed via Kolmogorov-Smirnov and L1 distance against the human ground truth), with larger and reasoning-focused models benefiting most; GPT-4.1 with VS matches a dedicated fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B persuadee simulator, and DeepSeek-R1 with VS surpasses it on median donation amount, though a readability gap between VS-simulated and human/fine-tuned dialogue remains. On synthetic data generation for math reasoning, VS variants used to generate 1,000 synthetic competition-math questions (with reasoning traces produced by a separate strong model, Qwen3-32B) produce training data that, after supervised fine-tuning of three different student models, improves average downstream accuracy across MATH500, OlympiadBench, and Minerva Math by a consistent margin over Direct, CoT, Sequence, and Multi-Turn baselines (e.g., 37.5% average for VS-Multi versus 32.8% for the un-fine-tuned baseline and 30.6% for Direct-generated data). A parallel negative-synthetic-data experiment (generating plausible but incorrect GSM8K solutions) finds VS produces a higher rate and greater diversity of convincing wrong answers than baselines, and mixing this negative data into an offline-RL training setup improves downstream GSM8K accuracy over mixing in negative data from any baseline method. The paper also checks two properties needed for VS to be practically trustworthy: factual accuracy on the SimpleQA benchmark (VS-CoT achieves the best Top@1 and Pass@N accuracy among all methods tested, so diversity gains do not come at a demonstrated accuracy cost) and safety on the StrongReject harmful-prompt benchmark across six models (refusal rates stay within roughly 97-98.6% across all methods, with VS only 0.3-0.8 percentage points below the Direct baseline, and qualitative inspection shows VS produces diversely-phrased but still substantively firm refusals rather than a single canned refusal or safety bypass). A further probing experiment compares VS-elicited response distributions against an approximate ground truth derived from a large pretraining corpus (RedPajama) for a simple constrained task ("name a US state"), finding VS-Standard's induced distribution has substantially lower KL divergence from this pretraining-derived reference than either Direct or Sequence prompting, and that the verbalized probabilities themselves carry meaningful (though imperfect, and notably weaker for programming languages than for US states or countries) rank-order correlation with corpus-derived reference frequencies.

## Learnings

The paper's most conceptually important, broadly transferable contribution is a specific, mechanistic, testable explanation for why alignment causes mode collapse that is independent of any particular reward-model architecture or RL algorithm: because human annotators exhibit a well-documented, general cognitive bias toward typical/familiar content, any preference dataset built from human judgments is liable to carry this bias regardless of how good the reward model or optimizer otherwise is, meaning mode collapse should be expected to recur across essentially any RLHF-style pipeline unless the training data itself is corrected, a genuinely different diagnosis than "our reward model isn't good enough yet" and one with direct implications for how future alignment pipelines should be designed.

A second, immediately practical learning is the specific, simple prompting intervention itself: reformulating a request from an instance-level query to a distribution-level query with verbalized probabilities is trivial to implement (no logit access, no training, works across closed and open models alike) yet the paper shows it reliably recovers a large fraction of lost diversity across a very wide range of task types, a low-cost technique any practitioner already using LLM APIs could adopt immediately, distinct from more expensive interventions like specialized decoding algorithms or fine-tuning.

A third, more subtle but important learning is the paper's finding that different prompt framings ("give me one answer" vs. "give me a list" vs. "give me a distribution") collapse to genuinely different notions of typicality, not just different amounts of the same collapse; the finding that list-level prompts still collapse to a narrow "bestseller list" of top candidates, while distribution-level prompts specifically break this pattern because annotators separately prefer representative distributions when a distribution is what's asked for, is a non-obvious mechanistic distinction worth remembering whenever designing a prompt intended to elicit diversity, since simply asking for "several" outputs is shown here to be an incomplete fix on its own.

## Verification

The paper's theoretical claims about typicality bias are grounded in an explicit, falsifiable empirical test (the Bradley-Terry regression on HelpSteer, using two different base models to compute the typicality proxy, with reported effect sizes and p-values) rather than only introduced as a plausible-sounding hypothesis, and the paper proactively checks and reports that the effect survives (with some attenuation, disclosed rather than hidden) after controlling for plausible confounding surface-form correlates like length and readability. The mode-collapse consequence of typicality bias is derived as a formal mathematical proof from the standard RLHF closed-form solution rather than asserted informally, giving the central causal claim a level of rigor uncommon in prompting-method papers. On the empirical side for Verbalized Sampling itself, the paper backs its central diversity claims with statistical significance testing (one-tailed t-tests against baselines) rather than only reporting raw score differences, and critically, corroborates its automatic semantic-diversity metric with an independent human study (90 Prolific annotators with reported, moderate-to-high inter-annotator agreement), a genuinely important verification step since automatic embedding-based diversity metrics could in principle diverge from what human readers actually perceive as more varied output. The paper separately and explicitly checks two properties that could plausibly be traded off against diversity gains, factual accuracy (SimpleQA) and safety refusal behavior (StrongReject), rather than assuming these are unaffected, and reports the specific magnitude of any change (near-zero for both) rather than only a qualitative "no meaningful difference" claim. The extensive ablation suite (post-training stage, number of candidates k, decoding-strategy interactions, alternative probability-eliciting phrasings, probability-threshold tuning) further stress-tests the core finding's robustness to implementation choices rather than presenting a single, possibly cherry-picked configuration as representative.

Verification is comparatively thinner in a few specific places the paper itself is candid about. The pretraining-distribution comparison (Section E.10) is explicitly flagged by the authors as "a proof-of-concept on a simple task" rather than a general validation, since it uses a single constrained question ("name a US state") and an approximate, corpus-frequency-based proxy (RedPajama word counts) for the true pretraining distribution rather than the models' actual, undisclosed pretraining data; the calibration check across three constrained domains (states, countries, programming languages) further shows this calibration is real but imperfect and notably weaker outside the two domains it works best for, a limitation the paper reports plainly rather than only in the domains favorable to its claim. The "emergent trend" that larger models benefit more from VS is demonstrated using only two small-versus-large model pairs (GPT-4.1-mini vs. GPT-4.1; Gemini-2.5-Flash vs. Gemini-2.5-Pro), which is a real, consistent, and clearly-reported pattern within those two comparisons but a comparatively narrow base of size-matched pairs from which to generalize the stronger, scaling-law-flavored language ("emergent trend") used to describe it. Quality judgments throughout most of the automatic (non-human-study) experiments rely on a single LLM judge (Claude-3.7-Sonnet, or GPT-4.1 for factual-accuracy judging), and while the paper's human study independently corroborates the automatic metric specifically for the three creative-writing tasks, the dialogue-simulation, open-ended QA, and synthetic-data-generation quality assessments elsewhere in the paper are not similarly cross-checked against an independent human or differently-sourced judge.

## Field context

The paper positions itself against two prior explanations for mode collapse it explicitly cites and distinguishes from: reward-model inadequacy (Chakraborty et al. 2024) and RLHF's KL-regularized optimization structurally favoring majority-style responses (Xiao et al. 2024), arguing these algorithmic accounts, while not wrong, miss a more fundamental data-level driver that would persist even if both were fixed. It also positions Verbalized Sampling against existing diversity-improving techniques it reviews directly: decoding-time interventions like μ-sampling, mirostat, and min-p sampling (computationally involved and typically requiring logit access, hence restricted to open-source models) and prior prompting-based diversity techniques, which the paper characterizes as relying on prescriptive, handcrafted, task-specific prompts rather than a principled, theoretically-grounded, broadly-applicable mechanism, positioning VS's combination of theoretical grounding and simplicity as its key point of difference from prior list-eliciting or verbalization-based methods in question answering, commonsense reasoning, and synthetic data generation that it cites as sharing a similar surface-level idea without the same theoretical foundation. Within this corpus, the paper's central tension, that post-training alignment systematically narrows a model's output diversity even holding output quality/utility fixed, connects directly to [2504.09389] (the LLM-novelty-measurement paper reviewed earlier in this same batch), which found that post-training generally increases both quality and originality relative to base models on its own novelty metric; the two papers are not strictly contradictory (this paper's mode-collapse argument concerns the diversity of the response distribution across repeated samples for a fixed prompt, while the novelty paper's originality axis concerns similarity to training data for a single generation), but they offer complementary, worth-reconciling perspectives on exactly what post-training does to the space of things a model can produce, and a natural question for future synthesis is whether post-training's demonstrated originality gains in that paper are compatible with, or in tension with, the specific within-prompt diversity collapse this paper documents and explains mechanistically. The paper's practical technique also sits alongside [2510.00931] (FusioN, reviewed earlier in this same batch) as a complementary rather than competing idea in the broader "get more value out of LLM sampling" space: FusioN synthesizes multiple already-generated samples into one better output, while Verbalized Sampling changes how the samples themselves are elicited in the first place to be less redundant, and the two techniques could plausibly be combined (using VS to generate a more diverse candidate pool, then FusioN to synthesize it) though neither paper tests this combination.

## Critical discussion

The paper's theoretical contribution is unusually rigorous for a prompting-method paper: it derives mode collapse as a formal mathematical consequence of a specific, independently-testable data property (typicality bias), rather than only observing that collapse occurs and proposing an ad hoc fix, and it backs the theory with a genuine empirical regression on real preference data with reported effect sizes, significance levels, and a disclosed robustness check against surface-form confounds. The empirical validation of the resulting method is similarly thorough: consistent, statistically-significant gains across a wide range of models, tasks, and languages of application (creative writing, dialogue simulation, synthetic data generation, factual QA, random-number generation, safety), an independent human study corroborating the core diversity claim, and a broad ablation suite checking robustness to implementation choices.

The clearest limitation is the comparatively narrow evidentiary base behind the paper's most attention-grabbing empirical pattern, the "emergent trend" that more capable models benefit more from VS: this rests on only two small-vs-large model pairs from two providers, a real and consistently-observed pattern within those specific comparisons, but a modest sample size for a claim framed in the scaling-law-adjacent language of an "emergent trend," which typically implies a broader, more systematically-sampled relationship across many model sizes rather than two paired comparisons. A second, more moderate concern is the paper's reliance on a single LLM judge (primarily Claude-3.7-Sonnet) for most of its non-human-study quality assessments, including tasks and settings (dialogue-simulation linguistic quality, open-ended QA, synthetic-data-generation quality) beyond the three creative-writing tasks the human study specifically validates; since one of the evaluated generator models in several experiments is Claude-4-Sonnet, a same-family relationship between judge and one of the evaluated systems exists in principle, though the paper's primary comparisons are paired within each generator model (VS vs. baselines for the same model) rather than cross-model rankings, which substantially, though not completely, limits how much a family-preference bias in the judge could distort the central VS-versus-baseline conclusions. A third, appropriately self-disclosed rather than hidden limitation is the paper's own stated trade-off: VS costs more compute and latency at inference time than single-response generation, and its benefits are explicitly noted to depend on sufficient underlying model capability, with smaller or weaker models occasionally showing quality degradation rather than gains, a genuine practical constraint on when the method should be reached for.

What remains well supported after these caveats: typicality bias is a real, measurable, statistically robust property of at least one major human preference dataset, and the mathematical argument that such bias drives mode collapse under standard RLHF objectives is formally sound given the paper's stated assumptions; Verbalized Sampling reliably increases measured output diversity, corroborated by an independent human study on the specific tasks where that study was conducted, across a genuinely broad range of models and use cases without a demonstrated cost to factual accuracy or safety; and the method's downstream value is demonstrated concretely, not just via a diversity metric in isolation, through improved fine-tuned-model accuracy from VS-generated synthetic data and improved behavioral realism in dialogue simulation relative to strong baselines including a dedicated fine-tuned comparison model.
