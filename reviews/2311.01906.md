Citation: He, B., Hofmann, T. Simplifying Transformer Blocks. ICLR 2024. arXiv:2311.01906.

## What this paper is about

A standard transformer block bolts together attention and MLP sub-blocks with skip connections and normalization layers in a specific, historically largely unchanged arrangement, and this precise arrangement is known to be fragile: small changes can make the network untrainable or much slower to train. This paper asks how much of that arrangement is actually necessary, and, combining signal-propagation theory (which studies how activation statistics evolve through a deep network at initialization) with direct empirical measurement, shows that skip connections, the attention sub-block's value and projection matrices, the sequential ordering of attention and MLP sub-blocks, and even normalization layers can each be removed without losing training speed or downstream task performance, so long as specific compensating initialization and reparameterization choices are made at each step. The resulting simplified block trains at the same per-update speed as the standard architecture while using up to 15% fewer parameters and running up to 16% faster.

## Extended summary

All main experiments use an 18-block, 768-width causal decoder-only GPT-style model trained on CodeParrot, a large enough dataset to stay in a single-epoch, minimal-generalization-gap regime that isolates training speed as the object of study, with a standard Pre-LN transformer block (attention and MLP sub-blocks each wrapped in a skip connection with an internal normalization layer, in the classic Vaswani et al. arrangement) as the baseline. The paper builds directly on He et al. (2023)'s finding that respecting signal-propagation principles (via Value-SkipInit, which biases the attention matrix toward the identity at initialization) allows deep skipless transformers to train for the first time, but at substantially reduced per-update convergence speed relative to Pre-LN.

Section 4.1 shows that reparameterizing the attention sub-block's value and projection matrices as an initialization term (fixed, orthogonal or identity) plus a trainable residual term, and explicitly downweighting how much that residual term is allowed to move away from initialization, recovers essentially all of the training speed lost by removing the attention skip connection; identity initialization of the value/projection matrices outperforms the orthogonal initialization used in prior work and essentially matches the standard Pre-LN block's per-update speed. Section 4.2 pushes this further: fixing the value and projection matrices to the identity entirely, rather than only downweighting their trainable deviation, matches Pre-LN performance and is empirically justified by two independent observations, first, that when these matrices are left trainable (but downweighted) their own residual-to-skip gain ratio spontaneously converges toward zero over the course of training for the large majority of layers, indicating the network itself learns not to use the extra linear-projection capacity they would otherwise provide, and second, that a model with these matrices genuinely fixed to identity reaches slightly better final evaluation loss (1.178) than one where they remain trainable but downweighted (1.194). This "Simplified Attention Sub-block" (SAS) removes half the parameters and half the matrix multiplications in the attention sub-block (query and key parameters are all that remain), for a reported 13% total parameter reduction in the models studied, while training at speeds matching or slightly exceeding the Pre-LN baseline in wall-clock runtime.

Section 4.3 addresses the remaining skip connection, in the MLP sub-block, which the authors find is substantially harder to remove: the same signal-propagation-motivated techniques that worked for attention (transforming activations to be closer to linear) still produce a significant per-update training-speed loss under Adam when applied to the MLP, and several variants of "Looks Linear" initialization did not help either, so the paper does not attempt to remove the MLP skip connection directly. Instead, it adopts the previously-proposed parallel transformer block (used in PaLM and ViT-22B), which computes attention and MLP sub-blocks on the same normalized input in parallel rather than sequentially, and combines this with the paper's own SAS attention sub-block and a single shared skip connection weight set to zero, along with a downweighted MLP-branch gain, to remove all remaining skip connections in the block entirely. This combined "SAS-Parallel" (SAS-P) block trains even faster in wall-clock terms than SAS alone, matching the parallel block's speed despite using 13% fewer parameters, with the authors' intuition being that the combination of shaped attention and identity-fixed value/projection matrices preserves inter-block signal well enough to substitute for what a skip connection would otherwise provide. Section 4.4 shows normalization layers can also be removed from this fully simplified block (the resulting architecture has no skip connections, no sequential sub-block ordering, no value or projection matrices, and no normalization) while still matching Pre-LN's wall-clock training speed, though the authors note a small per-iteration training-speed cost remains and that they observed occasional numerical instabilities (NaN activations on a minority of sequences) when fine-tuning this fully normalization-free variant, so they treat the normalization-retaining SAS and SAS-P blocks as their primary recommended architectures rather than the fully stripped-down variant.

Section 5 extends the validation across several dimensions. Scaling network depth from 18 to 72 blocks, the simplified blocks continue to improve with added depth at essentially the same per-update trajectory as Pre-LN, while the earlier Value-SkipInit baseline actually trains slower per update at 72 blocks than at 18 and falls further behind with depth, indicating a scalability problem specific to that prior method rather than to skipless architectures generally. On the bidirectional BERT setting (using the resource-constrained "Crammed BERT" recipe, a fixed 24-hour single-consumer-GPU training budget), the simplified blocks match Crammed BERT's masked-language-modeling pretraining speed and, after fine-tuning on the GLUE benchmark (averaged over 3 seeds), match the Pre-LN baseline's downstream score (78.9 vs. 78.4-78.5 for SAS/SAS-P, differences not statistically significant) while using 101M parameters instead of 120M (a 16% reduction) and running 9-16% faster per iteration depending on the variant; the fully normalization-free variant is reported without a GLUE score because of the fine-tuning instabilities noted above. Interestingly, Value-SkipInit, despite its markedly worse pretraining loss curve, largely recovers competitive GLUE performance after fine-tuning, which the authors flag as evidence that pretraining speed and fine-tuning outcome are not always tightly coupled, a question they leave to future work rather than investigating further. Finally, training the CodeParrot models for 3x as many tokens (roughly 2B tokens total) confirms the simplified blocks' training-speed advantage holds up with substantially longer training, not just in the shorter runs used for the main comparisons.

The Discussion section is candid about scope: all models studied are 100-300M parameters, small relative to the largest contemporary transformers, and the authors specifically flag that PaLM's own reported finding (parallel blocks improving relative to Pre-LN with scale) makes it an open and consequential question whether their simplifications' benefits grow, shrink, or stay constant at much larger scale, noting only that their depth-scaling results are suggestive rather than conclusive on this point. They are also explicit that several open theoretical questions remain unresolved, including why fixing the value/projection matrices to identity outperforms leaving them trainable, why normalization retains some benefit for training speed beyond what current signal-propagation theory captures, and whether inherited default hyperparameters (the AdamW optimizer, the specific fine-tuning protocol) are implicitly overfit to the standard Pre-LN block in ways that could be further improved for the simplified architectures with dedicated tuning.

## Learnings

The paper's most broadly transferable empirical finding is that when a component's trainable weights are allowed to deviate from a well-chosen initialization but the deviation is explicitly downweighted, the network itself will sometimes reveal, through where those weights actually end up, that the component was not adding meaningful capacity in the first place: the value and projection matrices' residual-to-skip gain ratio spontaneously converges to near zero during ordinary training for the large majority of layers, before the authors ever hard-fix those matrices to the identity. This is a specific, actionable diagnostic pattern, watching what a softly-constrained component does on its own during training, worth applying whenever there's a live question about whether a specific architectural component is load-bearing, rather than only removing the component outright and comparing final performance.

A second, more cautionary learning is the paper's own explicit finding that pretraining speed and downstream fine-tuning outcome can decouple: Value-SkipInit trains dramatically worse than every other method during BERT pretraining, yet is largely able to recover competitive GLUE performance after fine-tuning, a result the paper reports plainly without over-interpreting. Any evaluation regime that judges an architectural change purely by pretraining-loss curves risks missing this kind of recovery (or, in principle, the opposite: strong pretraining curves that fail to transfer to downstream fine-tuning), so pretraining speed alone should not be treated as a complete proxy for a modification's ultimate practical value.

A third, more specific architectural learning is that different transformer sub-components resist simplification to different degrees for different reasons: the attention sub-block's skip connection and value/projection matrices could be removed cleanly once the right compensating initialization was found, but the MLP sub-block's skip connection resisted every signal-propagation-motivated technique the authors tried (including several variants of "Looks Linear" initialization), forcing them to route around the problem entirely via the pre-existing parallel-block architecture rather than solving it directly. This is a useful, concrete data point that "make the theory-motivated fix and the problem goes away" does not generalize uniformly across a network's different component types, even within the same overall research program.

## Verification

The paper verifies its central claims (that each simplification preserves training speed and downstream performance) through several complementary checks rather than a single comparison. Wall-clock runtime and per-update-step training curves are reported side by side (Figure 5 for runtime, an appendix figure for step count) specifically so a reader can distinguish "trains just as fast per gradient update" from "trains faster in absolute time because each step is cheaper," which are different and both relevant claims the paper is careful not to conflate. The depth-scaling experiment (18 to 72 blocks) functions as a genuine robustness check on whether the simplifications' benefits are an artifact of the specific, comparatively shallow network size used in the main experiments, and it is also used to falsify a plausible alternative hypothesis by showing the prior Value-SkipInit method's problems specifically worsen with depth rather than improving, strengthening the paper's implicit claim that its own modifications, not just "any skipless approach," are what resolves the scalability issue. The BERT/GLUE experiment is a genuine transfer test to a different architecture family (bidirectional encoder rather than causal decoder), different dataset, and a downstream fine-tuning task rather than only upstream loss, and GLUE scores are reported with variance across 3 seeds specifically so a reader can judge whether the reported parity with the Pre-LN baseline is statistically meaningful rather than a lucky single run; this reported-variance standard is not extended to the primary CodeParrot decoder-only loss curves (Figures 2, 5, 6, 8), which are presented as single training runs without a repeated-seed comparison, a real asymmetry in how rigorously the paper's two main experimental settings are each verified. The longer-training (3x tokens) experiment functions as a check against a specific plausible failure mode, that the simplified blocks' speed advantage might only hold briefly early in training and vanish or reverse over a longer run, and finds the advantage persists.

## Field context

This paper builds directly on He et al. (2023)'s Value-SkipInit and Noci et al. (2023)'s Shaped Attention, both signal-propagation-motivated techniques for training skipless transformers, explicitly identifying and closing the specific gap those methods left open (large per-update training-speed losses) rather than only citing them as background. It also engages the parallel transformer block (Zhao et al. 2019; Wang & Komatsuzaki 2021, used at scale in PaLM and ViT-22B) as a proven, adoptable building block that its own simplifications compose with directly rather than compete against, and cites Trockman & Kolter (2023)'s independent empirical observation that trained transformers' value-projection matrix product often has a large identity component as external corroboration for its own decision to fix those matrices to the identity outright. Within this corpus, the paper's finding in Section 4.2, that value and projection matrices spontaneously converge toward the identity during training and can be fixed there without any performance loss, connects strikingly to [2309.08593] (Attention-Only Transformers, reviewed earlier in this batch): that paper's Theorem 7 proves, as a pure mathematical fact, that a masked attention head with an identity mask reduces exactly to a linear transformation of a token's own representation, and this paper independently, empirically discovers that trained transformers' actual value/projection pathway behaves as though that reduction were already the right answer, converging toward identity-like behavior on its own. One paper arrives at the identity-like structure through formal proof about what an attention head can represent, the other through direct measurement of what a trained attention head actually does; read together they offer converging theoretical and empirical evidence that the value/projection pathway in standard multi-head attention carries substantially less irreducible representational necessity than the architecture's parameter count would suggest. The paper's broader project, determining which specific transformer-block components are load-bearing for training dynamics versus merely conventional, is also a natural methodological sibling to [1911.03864] (Sandwich Transformer) and [2105.01601]/[2105.08050] (MLP-Mixer and gMLP), all reviewed in earlier batches, each interrogating a different axis of the same general question (sublayer order, whether attention is necessary, whether MLPs are necessary, and here, whether skip connections, normalization, and specific weight matrices are necessary) about the same architecture.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claims are supported by a genuinely comprehensive set of checks: each simplification is introduced and validated individually before being composed with the others, the depth-scaling and longer-training experiments each specifically target a plausible way the main result could fail to generalize, and the BERT/GLUE transfer experiment tests a meaningfully different architecture family and task type rather than only re-confirming the decoder-only result. The paper is also unusually candid about what remains unexplained, offering only "half-explanations" for why fixed-identity value/projection matrices outperform trainable ones rather than presenting a possibly premature theoretical account, and explicitly naming normalization's residual training-speed benefit as something current signal-propagation theory cannot yet explain.

The clearest limitation, one the authors themselves flag prominently in the Discussion, is scale: every experiment in the paper uses models in the 100-300M parameter range, and the authors specifically note that a directly relevant prior finding (PaLM's parallel blocks improving relative to Pre-LN as scale increases) makes it a real open question, not a safely extrapolated one, whether the paper's own efficiency gains hold, grow, or shrink at the scale of contemporary frontier models; the depth-scaling experiment (up to 72 blocks) is suggestive evidence in the paper's favor but is explicitly not presented as resolving this question. A second, more specific gap concerns evidentiary rigor across the paper's two main experimental settings: the BERT/GLUE downstream comparison reports variance across 3 random seeds, letting a reader judge statistical significance directly, but the primary CodeParrot decoder-only training-speed curves, the paper's headline result, are presented as single runs without a repeated-seed comparison, so the precision of claims like "matches Pre-LN's per-update speed" on the main experimental setting rests on a less rigorous evidentiary standard than the paper's own secondary GLUE comparison uses. A third, smaller point: the fully normalization-free variant (Section 4.4) is reported to cause fine-tuning instabilities (NaN activations on a minority of downstream sequences) and no GLUE score is given for it, meaning the paper's most radically simplified architecture, the one pictured as its headline "most simplified block" in Figure 1, is in fact not the configuration the authors ultimately recommend or fully validate downstream; the practically-endorsed configurations (SAS and SAS-P) both retain normalization, a nuance easy to miss if a reader takes the paper's title and headline figure at face value without reading through to the Discussion's own caveat.

What remains well supported: skip connections, value and projection matrices, sequential sub-block ordering, and normalization layers can each be removed from a standard transformer block without loss of per-update training speed or downstream task performance, provided specific compensating initialization and reparameterization choices (identity-fixed value/projection matrices, Shaped Attention, downweighted MLP branches, and the pre-existing parallel-block structure) are made at each step; these gains are not an artifact of a narrow, shallow setting, since they persist across a 4x depth increase, a different architecture family (BERT) and task type (GLUE fine-tuning), and substantially longer training; and the resulting simplified blocks achieve genuine, measured efficiency gains (up to 15-16% fewer parameters and 9-16% faster throughput) rather than only matching the baseline at equal cost.
