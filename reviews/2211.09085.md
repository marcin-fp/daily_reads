Citation: Taylor, R., Kardas, M., Cucurull, G., Scialom, T., Hartshorn, A., Saravia, E., Poulton, A., Kerkez, V., Stojnic, R. Galactica: A Large Language Model for Science. Meta AI, 2022. arXiv:2211.09085.

## What this paper is about

Scientific knowledge is growing faster than anyone can read it, and today's interface for accessing it, search engines pointing to human-curated secondary sources like Wikipedia or PubChem, cannot itself organize or reason about that knowledge. This paper proposes replacing part of that interface with a single large language model, Galactica, trained not on a generic web crawl but on a deliberately curated 106-billion-token corpus of papers, textbooks, reference material, and scientific sequences (protein, DNA, SMILES), wrapped in specialized tokens for citations, step-by-step reasoning, and non-text modalities. The central bet is that a curated, high-quality, multi-epoch corpus can let a comparatively small model outperform much larger general-purpose language models on technical knowledge and reasoning tasks, and the paper reports state-of-the-art or near-state-of-the-art results across a wide range of scientific benchmarks to support that bet, while also open-sourcing the model.

## Extended summary

The 106B-token corpus (Table 2) is 83% papers (48 million documents), with the remainder split across academic code, reference material, knowledge bases, filtered Common Crawl, and task prompts; all sources are processed into a common markdown format, and the paper's stated goal is a "normative" curated-data paradigm as a deliberate contrast to the uncurated-crawl paradigm used by contemporaries like GPT-3, OPT, and PaLM (the paper specifically notes 50% of PaLM's corpus is social media conversation, of limited transfer value to scientific tasks). Tokenization is modality-specific: citations are wrapped in `[START_REF]`/`[END_REF]` tokens (with title-based identifiers chosen over alphanumeric IDs after ablation, despite titles being more hallucination-prone at smaller scale); step-by-step reasoning is wrapped in a `<work>` "working memory" token, populated from four prompt datasets (GSM8k, and three custom/sourced problem sets, totaling about 21.5k prompts and 9 million tokens), motivated by the observation that human-written chain-of-thought text in the wild typically omits intermediate steps performed via internal (unwritten) mental calculation, and optionally offloading arithmetic to an executed Python script when a computation is not reliably done in a forward pass (though the paper reports this Python-offload path was not actually needed or turned on for the experiments presented); SMILES, amino-acid, and DNA sequences each get their own start/end tokens with character-level tokenization. A deliberate departure from standard pretraining practice is "prompt pre-training": mixing 358 million tokens of task-formatted prompts (chemical property prediction, multiple-choice QA, extractive QA, summarization, entity extraction, reasoning, dialogue) directly into the general pretraining mix rather than as a separate post-hoc instruction-tuning stage, explicitly to boost task performance at smaller model scale without requiring either a >1 trillion-token corpus or larger model capacity; the paper is careful to flag which downstream evaluations are "in-domain" (their training data included) versus "out-of-domain" throughout. The architecture is a standard decoder-only Transformer (GeLU activations, no biases, learned positional embeddings, 2048-token context, 50k-token BPE vocabulary), trained at five scales from 125M to 120B parameters, with the 120B model trained on 128 A100 80GB nodes and deliberately capped in size so that inference fits on a single A100 node for downstream accessibility.

A specific and unexpected finding (Section 5.1) is that training for roughly 4.25 epochs (450 billion tokens) over the curated corpus continues to reduce validation loss and improve downstream (BIG-bench) performance for all model sizes, with the 120B model only beginning to overfit at the start of the fifth epoch; the paper is explicit this contradicts prior scaling-law literature suggesting repeated tokens harm performance, and offers two untested candidate explanations (a "quality factor" from curation, or a "modality factor" specific to scientific data) without adjudicating between them. Per-source validation loss curves show no source-specific heterogeneity, and the same relative pattern holds for the 120B model up through the point where all sources spike together entering epoch five.

Knowledge probes (Section 5.2), modeled on the LAMA methodology, test recall of 434 LaTeX equations (evaluated via manual human judgment given the possibility of multiple correct forms) and five domain-specific fact datasets (amino acids, biomedical fact triples, chemical reactions, galaxy-cluster classifications, mineral groups). Galactica 120B reaches 68.2% overall on the LaTeX probe versus GPT-3 text-davinci-002's 49.0%, OPT-175B's 8.9%, and BLOOM's 21.4%, with accuracy scaling smoothly and monotonically with Galactica's own model size across all five sub-fields; domain probes show similarly smooth scaling, with the explicit exception of BioLAMA, which the authors attribute to prompt-format difficulty affecting all models rather than a Galactica-specific weakness. On reasoning (Section 5.2.3), the `<work>` token produces large gains on mathematics MMLU (Galactica 120B with `<work>`: 41.3% average versus 35.8% without, versus Chinchilla's 35.7%) and on MATH (Galactica 30B with `<work>`, 11.4%, and with 5-shot chain-of-thought prompting, 12.7%, both exceeding PaLM 540B's 8.8% despite an 18x smaller parameter count; 120B reaches 16.6% with `<work>` and 20.4% with chain-of-thought prompting); the paper reports Minerva (a 540B PaLM variant specifically fine-tuned on LaTeX-formatted math) for context at 33.6%, clearly ahead of base Galactica, and is explicit that the comparison is informative about different mathematical data biases rather than a claim of parity with Minerva. On downstream scientific QA (Section 5.3, Table 10), Galactica generally beats OPT, BLOOM, and (in most tasks) the larger Gopher-280B, is more mixed against Chinchilla (weaker on high-school/memorization-heavy subjects, the authors' stated hypothesis being the corpus's graduate-paper-heavy composition under-represents introductory content, stronger on mathematical and graduate-level tasks), and sets new state-of-the-art results over fine-tuned specialist models on PubMedQA (77.6% vs. prior 72.2%) and MedMCQA dev (52.9% vs. prior 41.0%), with performance close to but below fine-tuned state-of-the-art on BioASQ and MedQA-USMLE.

Citation prediction (Section 5.4) is framed as evidence that a language model's weight-based context association can outperform retrieval infrastructure: across three constructed datasets (ML-concept citations, non-ML-concept citations, and in-context arXiv citations), Galactica 120B (51.9%, 69.1%, 36.6% accuracy respectively) outperforms both a sparse ElasticSearch retriever and dense Contriever-based retrievers (base and fine-tuned on the same corpus), with accuracy scaling smoothly with model size across all five Galactica sizes tested. A separate distributional analysis shows Galactica's predicted citation-count distribution converges toward the true empirical distribution (via declining Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance and increasing histogram overlap) as model size increases, though the paper's own Limitations section later notes some bias toward popular papers persists even at 120B scale. Sections 5.6-5.7 extend to chemistry and biology: Galactica learns IUPAC name prediction from SMILES self-supervised (without explicit supervision for that specific task) and the paper reports it attends to interpretable functional-group features; on MoleculeNet drug-discovery benchmarks formulated as text prompts, weakly-supervised performance scales with model size without task-specific fine-tuning; and for protein sequences, the model is evaluated on sequence-validation perplexity, functional-keyword prediction, and natural-language function description (Figure 21 shows a case where the model reproduces the exact ground-truth UniProt functional description for a held-out protein whose nearest training-set neighbor by sequence similarity is only 83% identical, offered as evidence of genuine generalization rather than memorization of that specific entry, though this is a single illustrative example rather than a systematic novelty analysis).

Toxicity and bias evaluations (Section 6) find Galactica scores lower stereotypical bias than GPT-3 (text-davinci-002) and OPT-175B on CrowS-Pairs (60.5% overall versus 67.2% and 69.5%, with an ideal unbiased score being 50%) and highest ICAT score (a combined bias/language-modeling quality measure) on StereoSet across every demographic category tested, and substantially lower toxic-continuation rates on RealToxicityPrompts across all prompt-toxicity bins; the paper attributes this directly to the scientific corpus's lower base rate of discriminatory or toxic text relative to corpora drawing on sources like Reddit. On TruthfulQA, Galactica exceeds OPT and BLOOM (26% vs. 21% and 19% MC1 accuracy at 120B scale, improving smoothly but slowly with model size) but the paper is explicit that "absolute performance is still low," concluding that a curated corpus alone does not solve the truthfulness problem.

The Limitations section (Section 7.1) is direct about several gaps: the corpus is restricted to open-access sources and self-imposed modality caps (molecules, proteins), so a less-constrained version would likely perform considerably better; the paper does not disentangle how much of its reported gains come from the core curated corpus versus the added prompt pre-training; citation prediction still shows popularity bias at the largest scale tested and would need further work before production use; the trade-offs between prompt pre-training and instruction tuning were not directly ablated; Galactica should not be relied on for general (non-scientific) world knowledge despite incidentally absorbing some via Wikipedia; and the paper explicitly cautions against concluding "text is all you need," noting Galactica has no representation of 3D molecular geometry despite geometry being fundamental to chemical meaning. Future-work items include a new denoising objective, longer context windows, extending to images, more `<work>` training data, continual-learning strategies, and, notably, a "Verification" item stating that "even as language models become more accurate with scale, we need assurances that their generations are correct and factual," framed as future infrastructure work rather than a solved or centrally load-bearing part of this paper's own evaluation.

## Learnings

The paper's most consequential and specific empirical claim is that repeated-epoch training on a curated corpus does not degrade performance the way prior scaling-law literature (cited directly, Hernandez et al. 2022) suggested repeated tokens generally would; validation loss kept falling and downstream BIG-bench accuracy kept improving through more than four epochs for every model size tested, with overfitting appearing only at the very start of a fifth epoch for the largest model. This is a genuinely surprising, well-documented result (per-source loss curves rule out simple source-specific heterogeneity as the explanation) that the authors themselves are honest they cannot fully explain, offering two untested candidate mechanisms (data quality versus data modality) rather than overclaiming a settled cause; it is a concrete data point worth revisiting whenever "just add more fresh tokens" is assumed to be strictly necessary versus training longer on a smaller, well-curated set.

A second, more design-level learning is the specific mechanism proposed for the `<work>` working-memory token: rather than relying entirely on chain-of-thought text mined from the wild, which the paper argues systematically under-represents the actual reasoning steps a human performs (because humans do lower-level computation internally and only write down the "principal" steps), the paper constructs purpose-built datasets that explicitly include the normally-omitted intermediate steps, and shows this closes a substantial part of the reasoning gap on mathematical benchmarks even with a comparatively small (9-million-token) and, by the authors' own admission, not very diverse training set. This is a specific, actionable idea distinct from "just add more chain-of-thought examples": the missing-steps diagnosis, not merely a shortage of reasoning examples, is offered as the actual bottleneck being addressed.

A third learning, of a more cautionary character given what happened after this paper's release, is that measured reductions in toxicity and stereotypical bias (real, well-documented in Sections 6.1 and demonstrated across three independent benchmarks) are a narrower property than general reliability, and do not by themselves indicate a model is safe to deploy for open-ended scientific content generation; a model can be simultaneously less toxic and less stereotype-biased than its predecessors on every standard fairness benchmark tested, while still being prone to generating fluent, confident, and factually fabricated content in exactly the domain, scientific writing, where confident fabrication is most likely to mislead a reader who is not already an expert.

## Verification

The paper's verification practices are uneven across its different claim types. Several core claims are checked with real methodological care: the repeated-token finding is checked per-source to rule out a simple confound (source heterogeneity), and separately checked on an out-of-domain benchmark (BIG-bench, deliberately chosen because it contains task types and prompt styles not present in the pretraining prompt mix) specifically to rule out the possibility that in-domain memorization alone explains the lack of overfitting; the citation-prediction claim is checked against three separate, genuinely competitive retrieval baselines (sparse ElasticSearch, a pretrained dense retriever, and a dense retriever fine-tuned on the same corpus) rather than a single weak comparison point; and downstream QA results are explicitly annotated in-domain versus out-of-domain throughout so a reader can judge how much of a given result reflects the training-prompt inclusion versus genuine generalization, with a stated data-leakage analysis performed for these benchmarks (detailed in the appendix). Other central claims rest on weaker verification: the LaTeX-equation and domain-knowledge probes, arguably the paper's headline technical-knowledge results, are evaluated by the authors' own manual judgment of correctness (necessary given the possibility of multiple valid answer forms) without any reported inter-annotator agreement, blinding, or independent check, which matters because these specific numbers (68.2% overall LaTeX accuracy versus GPT-3's 49.0%) are among the most widely quoted results from this paper. The paper's own explicit "Verification" future-work item, that language-model generations need independent assurances of correctness and factuality as models scale, is a candid, self-aware acknowledgment that this specific concern is not resolved by anything demonstrated in the paper itself, and stands in some tension with how the model was subsequently presented and used in a public-facing demo shortly after this paper's release.

## Field context

Galactica sits in the same late-2022 moment as several papers this corpus has already reviewed or that this paper itself directly engages: it explicitly compares against Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022) on scaling and against PaLM/Minerva (Chowdhery et al. 2022; Lewkowycz et al. 2022) on mathematical reasoning, and it explicitly positions itself against the retrieval-augmented alternative (RAG, RETRO, Atlas), stating plainly that this paper focuses on "how far we can go with model weights alone" while acknowledging "the strong case for using retrieval augmentation for future research on this topic." That specific design choice, betting on weight memory over retrieval for organizing scientific knowledge, is worth reading directly against two other papers reviewed in this same batch: [26mdocrag], a 2026 survey of multimodal retrieval-augmented generation that treats retrieval as close to a default requirement for grounding document-understanding systems and explicitly discusses provenance-verification gaps as an open challenge; and [26mira], a 2026 AI-scientist system that, unlike Galactica, builds an explicit persistent memory/wiki layer and routes physical claims through deterministic simulation and (eventually) wet-lab verification rather than trusting a model's own weight-stored recall. Reading Galactica's "weight memory as an interface for science" thesis alongside these two later, verification-centric designs is instructive: the field's subsequent trajectory (more retrieval, more explicit verification layers, less reliance on a model confidently recalling facts from its own weights) is, in part, a direct response to exactly the failure mode Galactica's public demo made vivid within days of this paper's release, when confidently-generated but fabricated scientific text (invented papers, pseudo-scientific wiki entries) proved indistinguishable from genuine content to non-expert users, a well-documented real-world event external to the paper itself but directly relevant to evaluating its central thesis.

## Critical discussion

Considered purely as a set of benchmark claims, the paper is well-evidenced: the headline numbers are consistently checked against multiple relevant baselines (general-purpose LLMs at comparable and larger scale, retrieval systems, fine-tuned specialist models), scaling trends are shown to be smooth and monotonic across five model sizes rather than resting on a single lucky configuration, and the paper is transparent about where it loses to comparison models (Chinchilla on high-school-level MMLU subjects, Minerva on LaTeX-specific math, absolute TruthfulQA performance remaining low). The repeated-token and citation-prediction findings in particular are checked with real attention to ruling out confounds rather than simply reported at face value.

The more serious limitation is not a specific wrong number but a mismatch between the paper's central framing and what its own evidence can support: the paper repeatedly frames Galactica as a tool that could "synthesize knowledge by generating secondary content automatically: such as literature reviews, encyclopedia articles, lecture notes," and demonstrates this directly with generated examples in the appendix, while its own evaluation of factual reliability (TruthfulQA, at 19-26% MC1 accuracy) shows the model is, by the paper's own numbers, wrong on the large majority of truthfulness-probing questions even at its largest scale, and the paper's own Limitations section does not weight this tension proportionately, treating "verification" as one bullet point among several future-work items rather than a central caveat on the paper's own headline framing. This is precisely the gap that surfaced in public within three days of the paper's companion demo going live: users found the model would generate fluent, citation-formatted, professionally-toned scientific-sounding text, exactly the "secondary content" the paper's introduction envisions automating, that was fabricated (invented papers, pseudo-scientific entries), and Meta withdrew the public demo as a direct result. This is documented external history rather than a claim the paper itself makes, but it is directly relevant to assessing the paper's central thesis: the same properties that produce strong benchmark scores (a model confidently completing scientific-sounding text from a curated corpus) are, without a comparably strong verification layer, also what produced the real-world failure. A second, narrower point is the manual, unblinded evaluation underlying the LaTeX-equation and domain-knowledge-probe results, the paper's clearest head-to-head "curation beats scale" evidence; without inter-annotator agreement or an independent check, a reader cannot fully rule out that the authors' own familiarity with what a "correct" equation or reaction should look like introduced some leniency or strictness bias relative to how an independent evaluator might have scored the same generations, though there is no specific reason offered in the paper (or found in this review) to think this substantially changes the qualitative ranking against GPT-3, OPT, and BLOOM.

What remains well supported: a smaller model trained on a curated, high-quality, domain-specific corpus can match or exceed much larger general-corpus models on technical knowledge recall and several scientific downstream tasks, a genuinely useful and reasonably well-evidenced data point about the tradeoff between data curation and raw scale; repeated-epoch training on such a corpus does not produce the overfitting the field's prior scaling-law intuitions predicted, checked carefully enough (per-source, out-of-domain) to be a credible and not merely coincidental finding; and the model measurably reduces toxicity and stereotypical bias relative to contemporaneous general-purpose LLMs on every standard benchmark tested, a real property distinct from, and not sufficient to guarantee, general factual reliability.
