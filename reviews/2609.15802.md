Citation: Tom Cunningham, Lukas Althoff, Basil Halperin, Brian Jabarian, Andrew Koh, Arjun Ramani, Phil Trammell, Parker Whitfill, Cheryl Wu. The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement. arXiv preprint arXiv:2609.15802 (v1), 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15802

## What this paper is about

AI systems now help build the next AI systems. The question is whether that feedback could make capability growth speed up on its own, without more human researchers or compute. The authors are economists. They write a sequence of small growth models in which each variable (algorithmic efficiency, capabilities, compute, data, economic output) is produced from other variables, and they draw these models as directed graphs whose edges are elasticities. The main technical point is that a feedback loop's strength is the product of the elasticities around it. Growth accelerates by itself when the summed strength of all loops through a stock exceeds one. They also separate "narrow" capabilities (useful for improving AI algorithms) from "broad" capabilities (useful in the wider economy), so that AI R&D can accelerate without GDP following.

They then map each term of the acceleration condition to data. They review what is known and give a wish list of numbers AI labs could measure and share. A rough calibration using the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI) puts the threshold at about 15 percent more research effort per ECI point, where the reported engineer uplift implies about 9 percent. They conclude that feedback loops are probably not yet strong enough for self-sustaining acceleration, that they seem to be strengthening, and that the calibration is highly uncertain.

## Extended summary

**Definitions and framing.** The paper avoids the overloaded term "recursive self-improvement" and defines: feedback loops (outputs routed back as inputs); R&D automatability (AI can autonomously make improvements at least equal to human researchers at equal cost, which does not imply that it is deployed); self-sustaining acceleration (AI alone suffices to accelerate AI capability progress with no growth in exogenous inputs such as human labor or training compute); and intelligence explosion (capabilities reach infinity in finite time). The authors argue that Good's 1965 claim that an ultraintelligent machine would "unquestionably" trigger an explosion does not follow once there are diminishing returns ("ideas become harder to find") or bottlenecks.

**Graph framework.** Nodes are outputs of production functions and edges are partial elasticities of an output with respect to an input. Stocks and flows are drawn differently so each diagram maps to one system of equations. A loop's strength is the product of the elasticities along its edges. The total elasticity of a flow with respect to its own stock is the sum over all loops. Holding exogenous inputs fixed, total elasticity below one means the growth rate falls (sub-exponential), exactly one gives exponential growth (a knife-edge), and above one gives self-sustaining acceleration (condition 1). The conditions are local because elasticities need not be constant, so acceleration can be a temporary "growth spurt."

**Model progression.**

- Jones (1995) model: algorithmic improvements come from R&D labor L and the stock A. Acceleration needs the self-elasticity of improvements with respect to A to exceed one. In traditional industries (agriculture to semiconductors) this elasticity is well below one (Bloom et al. 2020), and studies of software and AI algorithmic progress (Erdil et al. 2024; Eth and Davidson 2025) find the condition fails without extra loops. Algorithmic efficiency A is defined as the inverse of training compute needed for a given capability. By one operationalization (compute to reach GPT-2 pretraining loss), A rose 700-fold over 2019 to 2026.
- Baseline RSI model: capabilities C depend on A and training compute T, and C feeds back into algorithmic improvements. This creates the core loop A to C to A-dot. Condition (2): self-loop elasticity plus the product of the elasticity of improvements to C and the elasticity of C to A must exceed one. A can accelerate while C does not if the A-to-C elasticity falls (for example, a capability ceiling).
- Bottlenecks: experimental compute E and inference compute K enter algorithmic improvements, and data D enters capabilities. The condition is unchanged, but the C-to-A-dot edge can weaken through complementarity with scarce human labor (for example "lack of taste"), experimental compute (Whitfill and Wu 2025), inference compute, data (Farboodi et al. 2025; Millidge 2025; against: Ho et al. 2024 argue most algorithmic progress is "data-augmenting"), or training compute (Gundlach et al. 2025). The authors say they need only the elasticity, not the unknown functional form of how humans, AI, and compute combine.
- Narrow and broad capabilities: a narrow capability C1 feeds algorithmic improvement and a broad capability C2 drives output Y. Acceleration in A depends only on C1. Acceleration in C2 (and so in Y) also needs a superelasticity term, the log-derivative of the elasticity of C2 to A (condition 3). That term would be negative if scaling surpasses humans at algorithmic optimization but not at practical tasks because of data bottlenecks. The authors call the clean split a strong simplification. They suggest a narrow acceleration would plausibly cover easier-to-verify tasks (algorithm optimization, much of mathematics, coding) and could be very jagged, with possible spillover to harder-to-verify tasks.
- Specific optimization ability: if overall efficiency combines two sub-algorithms and AI helps only one, a spurt can stall. One cause is parallel costs: if 1/A = 1/A1 + 1/A2, the elasticity of A to A2 goes to zero as A2 outgrows A1. Another is low ceilings: kernel efficiency at 50 percent can at most double. The authors expect bigger AI boosts where verification is cheap (inference optimization, elicitation) than where it is costly (architectures), and flag this as something to measure.
- Economic feedback loops: capabilities raise output Y (GDP, or one firm's revenue), which funds E, K, T and data. Loops can close through firm revenue without macroeconomic effects. With state vector (A, T, D, E, K) and reduced-form elasticity matrix M, acceleration occurs when the dominant eigenvalue of M exceeds one (Davidson et al. 2026). Assuming a sub-unit self-loop, one-for-one accumulation of capital-like inputs from output, and a sub-unit indirect economic loop, the condition becomes a sum of three terms exceeding one (condition 4). The terms are the core loop divided by one minus the self-loop elasticity, the indirect loop through training compute and data, and the direct loop through experimental and inference compute. This needs five more elasticities.

**Measurement condition.** For empirical work the authors return to the bottleneck model and assume a single R&D effort aggregator R of L, K, and E. Condition (5) is then a product of three terms exceeding one: the return to research (elasticity of improvements to R, divided by one minus the self-elasticity), the elasticity of research effort to capabilities (epsilon_R,C), and the elasticity of capabilities to algorithmic efficiency (epsilon_C,A). For capabilities rather than efficiency, a fourth superelasticity term is added. The authors argue it is near zero. They call estimating and tracking this product "arguably the most important empirical exercise."

- Return to research: on a balanced growth path it equals g_A / g_R. Here g_R is the spend-weighted average of input growth rates, because under cost minimization with constant returns an input's elasticity equals its cost share; if firms do not optimize, shares are a revealed-preference bound. Equivalently, g_R is spend growth minus input-price inflation. The identity holds only on or near a balanced growth path, which RSI would leave. Reported data: algorithmic progress of 3x per year for pretraining (Ho et al. 2024), 3.5x per year for the full stack (Whitfill et al. 2025), 10x per year for the full stack (Ho 2026), and 1.5x per year at small scale versus 2.5x per year at frontier scale (Gundlach et al. 2025). Frontier-lab staff grew about 2 to 3x per year (Epoch AI 2026; Whitfill and Wu 2025 for Anthropic and OpenAI, 2022 to 2024). About one third of training cost went to researcher salaries (Cottier et al. 2024). In 2024, 10 percent of OpenAI's R&D compute went to final training runs and 90 percent to experiments (You 2025), with similar ratios for Chinese companies and the OLMo 3 family. Chip supply grows about 3.3x per year (You et al. 2026). No data exist on inference compute's share of R&D, and no deflator fits AI R&D. Evidence on labor-compute substitutability is inconclusive. The authors propose randomized allocations of labor and compute, AI labor as a cheaper stand-in, or manager surveys (double researchers, double compute, both).
- Research effort to capabilities (the hardest term): one route is direct uplift studies. A second models effective AI labor as K times C to the power gamma, giving epsilon_R,C = gamma times S_K (the inference-compute cost share). Villalobos and Atkinson (2023) imply gamma of about 0.15 to 0.3 under C = exp(ECI), an estimate the authors call outdated and a ratio of two studies. Self-reported uplift is 1.4 to 2X in METR's survey of technical workers (Becker 2026) and about 4X in the Claude Mythos Preview system card; lines of code merged per engineer per day rose about 8X from 2024 to Q2 2026 (Favaro and Clark 2026). The authors note that uplift is not the same object as epsilon_R,C. System cards report models beating humans given 8 to 40 hours on optimization problems but with low pass rates on complex debugging (Mythos Preview system card Table 2.3.3.A; GPT-5.5 system card 9.1.3). Qualitatively, AI research ideas were rated highly (Si et al. 2025), while Trehan and Chopra (2026) found weaknesses in both taste and execution.
- Capabilities to algorithmic efficiency: if A and T enter only as the product A times T, then epsilon_C,A = epsilon_C,T, the scaling-law slope. This is about 6.5 with C = exp(ECI) (Ho et al. 2025; Epoch AI 2025). The magnitude depends on the normalization, but the loop gain epsilon_R,C times epsilon_C,A does not. Scale-biased algorithmic progress (Gundlach et al. 2025) would break the symmetry. The authors still treat this as the best-measured term.
- Superelasticity: the capability-compute relation looks close to log-linear over many orders of magnitude, so epsilon_C,T and by symmetry epsilon_C,A are roughly constant. The scale-bias caveat applies here too.
- Supplementary data: RL scaling laws are largely unpublished. Training compute has grown 4 to 5X per year since 2010. Estimates of lab data spend are minimal. Consumer surplus from chatbots substantially exceeds firm revenue (Brynjolfsson et al. 2026). In a survey of over 5000 executives, 9 in 10 report no AI effect on productivity or employment over the past three years but expect +1.4 percent productivity and +0.8 percent output over the next three (Yotzov et al. 2026). Frontier-lab revenue grows about 3x per year, but spending is financed mostly by outside investment, so the economic loop runs through expectations.

**Calibration (Section 4.1).** Return to research is set to ln(3)/ln(3) = 1. This takes g_A = ln 3 from Ho et al. (2024), and g_R = ln 3 from staff growth (about ln 3) and experimental compute growth (about ln 3). Inference compute growth was probably small until coding agents. OpenAI's July 2026 GPT-5.6 launch post reported that the share of research compute for internal coding inference grew 100-fold in six months, "implying a much larger g_R at present." The authors keep g_R = ln 3 because spend shares are unknown, S_K is likely still small, and g_A was estimated in 2024. They set epsilon_C,A at about 6.5, from a regression of ECI on log(compute) across models in Epoch AI data, setting aside scale bias. The threshold is then epsilon_R,C > 0.15: about 15 percent more research effort per ECI point. That is roughly what Anthropic would need from moving from Claude Opus 4.7 to Opus 4.8, which differ by one ECI point. The authors note that research output per unit of capability should be smaller than human-task uplift because labor is only part of research. For the back-of-the-envelope estimate, Opus 4.8 scores 16 ECI points above Claude 3.7 Sonnet (February 2025, released with Claude Code). Uplift at that time was not conclusively above zero, and Becker et al. (2025) found a negative effect. A 4X self-reported uplift now, "very likely to be an overestimate," implies about 9 percent per ECI point, below the threshold. The authors add that these elasticities are likely rising (GPT-4 to GPT-5 raised research productivity far more than GPT-1 to GPT-2) and call tracking epsilon_R,C a research priority.

**Qualitative evidence (Section 4.2).** For acceleration: the Mythos Preview system card's self-assessed 4X uplift (probably overstated) and a 40-hour time horizon at which Mythos Preview beat human researchers. On AI research decisions, Mythos Preview beat humans 64 percent of the time in April 2026, versus 50 percent for Claude models released in 2025 (Favaro and Clark 2026). OpenAI reported the 100-fold growth in coding inference. In August 2025, experts and superforecasters gave a median 20 and 8 percent chance, respectively, that the growth rate of effective compute triples by 2029 (METR 2025). Jack Clark (May 2026) gave 60 percent that by end of 2028 an AI could autonomously build its own successor. Against: compute bottlenecks (algorithmic gains may need training compute; cognitive labor may need experimental compute; investment may "run out of GDP"); fast diminishing returns to autonomous optimization (the apple-picking model of Cunningham and Shetty 2026); and dependence on data. Pretraining filtering looks like a one-time boost, while post-training data may be more elastic. Narrow acceleration may come before broad acceleration, although a narrow system could discover broadly useful methods such as highly sample-efficient algorithms that update weights during deployment. Political and social barriers: at least 20 US data-center projects were cancelled in Q1 2026 after local pushback, and $85 billion of projects in three years (The Economist 2026). Recent US administration reactions to Mythos are also cited.

**Impacts and future work.** A self-sustaining acceleration would differ from ordinary AI progress in speed. Consequences discussed: transition costs rising with the speed of change; institutions lagging; asymmetric progress (fast in closed-loop, easy-to-verify domains such as software, games, and mathematics, possibly with breakthroughs in math and computational chemistry, and slow in messier domains); power imbalances between firms and states; and greater misalignment risk. Future steps: estimate the economic-loop condition, measure returns off the balanced growth path, combine the narrow and sub-algorithm models, separate quality from quantity in data and compute, model research taste, endogenize the automation decision, add directed technical change, run surveys and field experiments inside labs, and improve prediction markets.

**Appendix A (scale-dependent progress).** Algorithmic progress is usually estimated as a residual, A = C/T, under C = A times T (6). Gundlach et al. (2025) are said to show "conclusively" that this is misspecified because progress has been biased toward large scales. An extreme alternative, C = min(A, T) times T (7), means algorithmic leaps help only when compute is there to use them, and capabilities stagnate at T squared if compute stops growing. The authors offer another reading: large-scale bias may be endogenous to compute scaling, so RSI on fixed compute might develop small-scale-efficient algorithms through directed technical change.

**Appendix B (inference compute as a sector).** Training efficiency A_T and inference efficiency A_I are two stocks, with effective inference compute M = A_I times K. The condition (8) is the core loop plus an inference loop. It comes from a 2x2 reduced-form matrix whose dominant eigenvalue exceeds one exactly when det(I - E) < 0, assuming neither sector is self-sustaining alone. The inference loop enters additively, so it can tip the system over one even when the core loop falls short. The sectors decouple if better inference algorithms stop helping training research. The authors argue that METR's expenditure-horizon result (Cunningham et al. 2026) shows this loop is currently weak. On the NanoGPT speedrun, agents find optimizations cheaply at first, but returns fall much faster than returns to human labor, and performance roughly plateaus in the low thousands of dollars per run. The garbled Figure 8 text mentions an expenditure horizon of up to $3K and human labor at roughly $2.5K per 1 percent speedup, from interviews with contributors.

**Author caveats.** The calibration is "very rough." Parameters, especially epsilon_R,C, are largely unknown, and the model may be misspecified. ECI and the METR time horizon are flawed measures. The balanced-growth identity fails off-path. The A-T symmetry fails under scale bias. The 4X uplift is likely overstated, and uplift does not map cleanly onto epsilon_R,C. The gamma estimate is outdated. Elasticities are time-varying, so a local condition does not imply lasting acceleration.

## Learnings

A feedback system with several loops can be reduced to one testable number: the product of elasticities around each loop, summed over loops, compared with one. For AI R&D the paper splits this into three factors: the return to research, how much research effort one more unit of capability buys, and how much capability one more unit of algorithmic efficiency buys. The middle factor is the least measured. The practical point is that tracking that elasticity over time says more about acceleration than any benchmark slope.

Two economist's identities turn unobservable elasticities into things labs could report. On a balanced growth path, the return to research equals the growth rate of efficiency divided by the growth rate of research inputs. Under cost minimization, each input's elasticity equals its share of spending. The catch is that the first identity holds only while growth is steady, which is exactly what fails once acceleration begins.

If overall efficiency depends on several components whose costs add, AI that speeds up only one component gives a spurt that stalls, much like Amdahl's law. The authors pair this with the conjecture that AI helps most where checking an improvement is cheap. Together these give a concrete reason to expect jagged, verification-shaped progress rather than uniform acceleration, although the paper models this rather than measuring it.

The calibrated threshold is small: about 15 percent more research effort per ECI point, roughly one adjacent frontier release. Because it sits within a factor of two of rough current estimates, modest changes in inputs (which algorithmic-progress estimate is used, how self-reported uplift is discounted) can move the verdict.

## Verification

The paper checks no claims itself, and verification comes up only in passing. The authors conjecture from first principles that AI-assisted algorithm research gains most where verifying an improvement is cheap (inference optimization, elicitation) and least where it is costly (architecture design). They also conjecture that near-term narrow acceleration would concentrate in tasks "easy to verify in closed loops" (software, games, mathematics), and they ask labs to document which algorithm types get the biggest AI boost. Appendix A notes that under a compute constraint, labs could not validate large-scale-biased algorithms even if they found them. On the measurement side, the paper treats self-reported productivity uplift as likely inflated, cites an RCT that found a negative effect of AI on developer productivity (Becker et al. 2025), and asks for RCTs and randomized allocations of labor and compute instead of surveys. It proposes no verifier and does not measure verification cost.

## Field context

By the authors' account, the paper sits in the economics-of-RSI line founded by Aghion, Jones and Jones (2017) on AI-driven explosive growth and by Davidson's (2023) compute-centric takeoff model. It builds most directly on Davidson, Halperin, Houlden and Korinek (2026, NBER), whose general theory of semi-endogenous growth with innovation networks supplies the dominant-eigenvalue condition used here. Halperin is an author on both papers. The authors name three contributions: the networked graphical models, the narrow-versus-broad distinction that they say the reviews by Trammell and Korinek (2025) and Jones (2026) largely set aside, and a data wish list that supplements existing empirical work (Whitfill and Wu 2025; Gundlach et al. 2025; Epoch AI 2026). In my reading, the formal results are standard semi-endogenous growth conditions, and the text states that the general framework is Davidson et al.'s. What is new is the diagrammatic presentation, the three-term measurement decomposition, the tentative calibration, and the policy-facing data asks.

Connections to reviewed work:

- reviews/2609.00137.md (Burtsev, Recursive Criticality of AI Self-Improvement) also reduces RSI to a unit threshold on a loop gain (R_AI, recursive gain over frontier hardening), with a delay term and a spectral-radius condition across actors. That review records that Burtsev cites "Cunningham et al." for an independently derived unit threshold from economic elasticities; whether he cites this preprint or an earlier draft cannot be told from the review. The two papers agree that a local threshold can be crossed temporarily: Burtsev's finite frontier drives R_AI back below one, and this paper's low ceilings and parallel bottlenecks give growth spurts. They differ in that this paper tries a calibration from growth rates and cost shares, while Burtsev's parameters are unconstrained by data.
- reviews/2411.15114.md (RE-Bench, METR) found agents ahead of humans at short budgets but humans ahead at 8 and 32 hours, with agents relying on many cheap attempts. This fits the diminishing returns to agent inference spending that the paper cites from METR's expenditure-horizon study, and the system-card claim of models beating humans given 8 to 40 hours on optimization tasks. RE-Bench's caveat that benchmark tasks are far shorter and cleaner than real AI R&D matches this paper's data ask ("benchmark questions are not the same as real-life LLM training problems").
- reviews/2607.27191.md (CRUX shadow evaluations) and reviews/2609.15818.md (Atria Dawn's self-study, where AI proposed many options but made 6 to 9 percent of final decisions) are direct evidence on the human-taste bottleneck and on the "share of technical advances produced by AI" that the paper asks labs to publish. Neither estimates epsilon_R,C.
- reviews/2606.12683.md (From AGI to ASI) decomposes effective compute growth at about 10x per year, with algorithmic gains of 3 to 6x per year. This agrees with the range of g_A estimates the paper lists, and that report's qualitative map of pathways and bottlenecks is what this paper formalizes as edges that can weaken.
- reviews/2607.07663.md surveys self-improvement at the level of individual models and agents. This paper works at the level of the whole AI R&D production system and says nothing about per-model mechanisms.

## Critical discussion

The formal part holds up. I re-derived condition (4) from the stated reduced-form matrix under the Solow-like one-for-one accumulation assumption. Because the four capital-like stocks have identical rows, the system collapses to a 2x2 problem, and det(I - M) < 0 rearranges exactly into the three-term sum the authors give. The balanced-growth identity for the return to research and the cost-share decomposition of g_R are also correct under their stated assumptions. The graphs are an honest teaching device rather than new theory, and the text says the general result is Davidson et al.'s.

The headline empirical conclusion, that feedback is "not currently strong enough," is much less robust than the abstract suggests. The threshold is 1 / (return to research times 6.5). The paper sets the return to one by pairing Ho et al.'s 2024 pretraining-only estimate (3x per year) with g_R = ln 3, but its own data table lists 3.5x and 10x per year for the full stack and 1.5x to 2.5x per year by scale. Holding g_R = ln 3, those alternatives move the threshold to roughly 13.5 percent (3.5x), 7 percent (10x), 18.5 percent (2.5x), or 42 percent (1.5x) per ECI point. The 10x full-stack figure would put the threshold below the 9 percent estimate. The text does not explain why the pretraining-only figure is preferred, even though ECI scores post-trained models. In the other direction, the 100-fold growth in coding-inference compute that the authors cite would raise current g_R, lowering the return and raising the threshold. The gap between 9 and 15 percent is a factor of about 1.7, smaller than the spread from the choice of g_A alone. The paper reports no interval or sensitivity analysis for either number. The authors hedge in the text ("very rough," "tentative"), but the calibration supports "the same order of magnitude as the threshold, sign undetermined" rather than "below the threshold."

The 9 percent numerator also mixes channels. It spreads a self-reported 4X engineer uplift over 16 ECI points and sets the uplift at Claude 3.7 Sonnet to 1X, citing an RCT that found a negative effect. The authors themselves note three reasons it overstates epsilon_R,C: the uplift is self-reported and probably inflated, it applies to labor rather than to total research effort, and it is not the same object as the model parameter. A fourth follows from their own numbers. Over the same period, inference use and tooling (Claude Code, the 100-fold inference growth) rose sharply, and the calculation credits all of the uplift to ECI gains rather than to more inference or better scaffolds. These biases mostly push the 9 percent upward, so the numerator is plausibly an upper bound. That helps the authors' conclusion only if the threshold is as high as they assume.

The paper's two routes to epsilon_R,C are never set side by side. The cost-share route gives epsilon_R,C = gamma times S_K with gamma of 0.15 to 0.3. By my arithmetic, epsilon_R,C could then not exceed 0.3 even if all R&D spending went to inference, and reaching 0.15 would need inference to be at least half of R&D spend. The authors say S_K is "likely still low," so this route would put epsilon_R,C several-fold below the threshold, and well below the survey-based 9 percent. The gap arises because the N = K C^gamma specification lets AI contribute only as purchased AI researchers and leaves out capability raising human researchers' productivity, which is exactly what the uplift surveys measure. The measurement framework therefore does not yet cover the channel its main number relies on. This is a gap in the model, not an error, but it deserves a sentence.

The one estimate the authors compute themselves, epsilon_C,A of about 6.5 from "a regression of ECI on log(compute) across models," is not reported in enough detail to assess: no sample, specification, date controls, or standard error. If models from different dates are pooled without time controls, the slope absorbs algorithmic progress that correlates with scale, which bears on the A-T symmetry the paper relies on. The text flags scale bias (footnote 11 and Appendix A) but not this issue. Much of the other quantitative input consists of lab self-reports and blog-level sources (system cards, a launch post, company-authored assessments of AI research decisions) that the paper cannot audit. The authors say so about the 4X figure, and their data wish list is a direct response. The claim that loops "appear to be strengthening" rests on comparisons across different surveys and populations (1.4 to 2X versus 4X; 50 versus 64 percent win rates) and on an unquantified GPT-1-to-GPT-2 versus GPT-4-to-GPT-5 contrast, not on a time series of the elasticity. The direction is plausible, but the size is unknown.

The narrow-versus-broad and verification-cost arguments are presented as possibilities with hedged language. The model shows they can happen and does not claim they have. The claims about which domains will accelerate first are conjectures and should be cited as such.

What remains supported after these caveats: a correct and readable reduction of RSI dynamics to loop-gain conditions; a useful decomposition of the key condition into three measurable factors; a sensible, specific list of lab-side measurements that would narrow the uncertainty; and the finding that, under current rough estimates, the threshold and the observed returns are of the same order. That last point is itself informative. The paper does not establish on which side of the threshold the system currently sits.

## Relevance to us

Mostly this is not our problem. It is macroeconomics of frontier AI R&D. Two points touch our framing, both as conjecture rather than evidence. First, the authors expect AI-driven acceleration to be shaped by verification cost: larger boosts where an improvement is cheap to check, narrow acceleration in closed-loop domains such as software and mathematics, and slower progress in "messier" ones. This matches our view that verification cost, not generation capability, sets where automated science works. If it holds, the giants' general progress would absorb the cheap-check band of physics (symbolic and numerical checks) first, while hard-to-verify physics stays slow. That strengthens the case for building graded, well-characterized partial verification (B12) as the durable asset, and warns against competing on the easy-to-check band itself. The paper gives no evidence either way for physics.

Second, the cited METR result that agents' returns to inference spending on an optimization task plateau in the low thousands of dollars, faster than returns to human labor, agrees with RE-Bench. For our own AI Scientist loops it argues for budget-matched comparisons against human-in-the-loop baselines (B01) rather than assuming more agent compute keeps paying.
