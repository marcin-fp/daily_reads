## What this paper is about

Chain-of-thought reasoning models get better at hard problems by writing out long verbal reasoning traces, but every intermediate step has to be projected through a discrete vocabulary, generated one token at a time, and read back in before the next step can happen, which is expensive. Separately, compact "recursive solver" architectures can reason iteratively in a continuous latent space without ever producing text, but the ones built for ARC-AGI (a visual abstract-reasoning benchmark) typically have to be specifically optimized on each new puzzle's own demonstration pairs before they can solve it, rather than genuinely learning a new task purely from being shown examples at inference time. This paper introduces BDH-CQ, a system built on the authors' own prior "Dragon Hatchling" architecture, that combines both properties: it writes task demonstrations into an evolving recurrent memory (in-context learning, with no weight updates) and then solves the query through iterative computation in a continuous latent workspace (no verbalized intermediate reasoning), reporting a new state-of-the-art point on the ARC-AGI-1 cost-accuracy leaderboard at under one-tenth of a cent per task.

## Extended summary

BDH-CQ builds on the authors' previously published Dragon Hatchling (BDH) architecture, a "brain-inspired but not brain-imitative" post-Transformer sequence model using high-dimensional positive activations, low-rank communication, and recurrent associative state, which the authors describe as having already demonstrated language-modeling and interpretability properties in prior work, and which they had previously adapted into an iterative constraint-satisfaction system for Sudoku. BDH-CQ's specific contribution is a system-level interface with two functionally distinct components: a recurrent contextual memory that evolves as task demonstrations are processed sequentially (conceptually related to attention and fast-weight/linear-attention memory, with the paper noting linear attention is the simplest special case of the general update rule it uses), and a separate structured latent reasoning workspace that performs iterative computation to solve the current query after the demonstrations have been ingested, decoding only the final answer. The paper is explicit that the exact dimensions, update rules, and implementation details of both components "remain proprietary," and that the paper reports only the task interface and data provenance rather than the complete internal training recipe.

The system is trained as a 150M-parameter model on a curated mixture combining privately curated ARC-style examples with several public sources (the ARC-AGI-1 training set, RE-ARC, ConceptARC, ARC-Heavy, and ARC-GEN100K) plus additional augmentations, with no task identifiers or evaluation-task demonstration pairs participating in training and no parameters updated at inference time. On the public 400-task ARC-AGI-1 evaluation split, under the leaderboard's standard two-attempt (pass@2) convention, the system reaches 29.5% pass@2 at a computed cost of $0.00070 per task (derived from measured GPU-seconds at an assumed $3/H200-hour rate), which the paper reports as lying beyond the previously reported cost-accuracy Pareto frontier on the ARC Prize leaderboard, and states is roughly 11-57x cheaper than a comparably-scoring GPT-5.6 Luna configuration depending on which of two different OpenAI public pricing snapshots is used. An independent black-box audit, conducted by two of the paper's own co-authors from separate institutions (Bielik AI and New York University) under a documented protocol without access to model weights, reproduced the deployed system's 29.5% pass@2 score and additionally evaluated the system on ConceptARC and a hand-crafted evaluation set in separate technical reports cited by this paper.

The bulk of the paper is a detailed behavioral analysis using ARC and ARC-like controlled tasks as an inspectable testbed. On ConceptARC (16 hand-designed concept families, 10 tasks each), performance varies substantially by concept: families like ExtendToBoundary, FilledNotFilled, and TopBottom2D reach 9/10 pass@2, while Copy and Order reach only 2/10, though with only 10 tasks per family the paper is explicit that the Wilson confidence intervals for these counts overlap broadly enough that the ranking should be read as a capability profile rather than a precise ordering. Several families (notably Copy, at 19/30 correct individual test pairs but only 2/10 fully-correct tasks) show the system frequently produces individually-correct outputs without applying the inferred rule consistently across every test input of the same task, which the paper treats as evidence against a simple "one correctly induced rule transfers everywhere" account of the system's behavior; across the full ConceptARC set, 52 of 160 tasks have one or two (but not all three) correct test pairs.

A series of controlled, post-training-freeze experiments isolate specific capability boundaries. Varying one factor at a time while holding a rule fixed, the system extrapolates cleanly on boundary-propagation (48/48 correct across distances 2-8) and multi-site copying (48/48 as target count increases 1-4) throughout the tested range, while performance on sequence-ordering and nested-containment tasks holds up to a point and then drops sharply (ordering falls to 1/24 at length eight; nesting falls to 29/36 at depth five), with the two failure modes showing different signatures: ordering failures typically produce outputs with the wrong dimensions entirely, while nesting failures at depth five preserve correct output structure with over 99.9% mean cell accuracy, usually differing from the target by a single localized containment decision. Rerunning the identical held-out inputs with one additional in-context demonstration at the test-time complexity level (rather than only easier examples) recovers most of the lost performance (nesting depth-five recovers fully to 24/24; length-eight ordering recovers from 0/24 to 13/24), which the paper interprets as showing the failure is substantially a failure to extrapolate beyond demonstrated complexity levels rather than an intrinsic execution-capacity ceiling, though ordering retains some residual execution bottleneck even with matched support. A separate contextual-memory probe shows the system can bind and consistently apply a dense, task-specific color-permutation mapping across all 96 held-out test cases as the number of simultaneous bindings scales from two to eight. A compositionality experiment (relocating, reflecting, rotating, or swapping colors within a 3x3 motif, alone or composed with a relocation operation) finds composition behavior is highly operation- and representation-dependent: rotation composes perfectly with relocation (72/72), reflection composes on only 47/72 and varies sharply by which specific color-layout family is tested, and color-swap is learned reliably only in one fixed-layout family and never composes with relocation at all (0/72), with the paper explicitly cautioning that the shuffled-family color-swap failures (1/24 even in isolation) confound "composition is hard" with "this specific operation was hard to learn from these demonstrations in the first place."

A methodological control experiment addresses the most direct potential confound in the ConceptARC results: whether the system might be exploiting a semantic task identifier or a concept-grouped batch structure rather than genuinely reading the grids. Replacing identifiers with cryptographically opaque labels and mixing concept areas within batches left aggregate accuracy essentially unchanged (374/480 test pairs solved under both conditions), though the specific candidate answers returned did shift for a meaningful fraction of tasks even when the aggregate score did not, which the paper reads as ruling out this specific combined confound without claiming to have isolated the two interventions (identifier removal versus batch mixing) separately, or to have ruled out training-data exposure or checkpoint-selection effects more broadly. A separate reasoning-effort experiment finds pass@2 increases monotonically from 21% to 27% to 29.5% as a training-time-selectable "reasoning effort" setting increases from LOW to MEDIUM to HIGH, with a MIN-versus-STANDARD inference-cost comparison on the full evaluation set showing a numerically favorable but statistically inconclusive difference (111/400 versus 118/400, two-sided exact McNemar p=0.167), which the paper reports as unresolved rather than rounding to a clean win.

An appendix profiles the public evaluation set and two separately-generated evaluation cohorts (a distribution-calibrated generated set and a set stratified to balance 16 named "mechanics," or required operation types) to check whether coarse surface properties (grid size, color count, object count, demonstration count) explain success; none does so reliably (the strongest single descriptor, grid size, reaches only a pseudo-R^2 of 0.072 on the public set). The mechanic-stratified analysis finds solve rates spanning 65.7 percentage points across operation types (68.6% for flood fill down to 2.9% for gravity-and-stacking), but the paper is explicit that this spread is substantially confounded by task authorship: every generated task in this cohort was itself authored by an LLM (GPT-5.6) prompted with the target mechanic's name, and hand-written task families for the same mechanics score very differently from their generated counterparts (hand-written gravity tasks score 80-100% versus 2.9% for generated gravity tasks; hand-written and generated counting tasks similarly diverge), so the mechanic table is explicitly presented as characterizing this specific evaluation distribution's construction rather than as an intrinsic operation-difficulty ranking. Ten further deterministic controlled-ladder experiments isolate additional specific limitations with statistical testing (Fisher's exact tests): genuine conditional rule selection between two demonstrated rules drops accuracy by 43.3 percentage points relative to a same-structure control where the selector cue does not actually change which rule applies (p=9x10^-9); a parameter value entirely absent from the demonstrations (whether interpolated or extrapolated relative to the demonstrated range) is never solved (0/120) versus 12/40 when the exact test value does appear among demonstrations (p=1.4x10^-8); and solving a task involving three or more separated visual panels drops sharply compared to two panels (26/40 to 1/40), a pattern the paper associates with, but does not directly test as, a segmentation limitation.

The paper's outlook section states an intention to scale BDH-CQ to larger model sizes (citing preliminary, unquantified claims that "Transformer-like scaling laws apply during pretraining at scales from 1B to 600B parameters" for the underlying BDH architecture, without presenting supporting data for this specific claim in this paper) and to extend the approach to ARC-AGI-2, constraint-satisfaction domains, and language/mathematical reasoning.

## Learnings

The specific empirical dissociation between "ordering" and "nested containment" failure modes, where both degrade sharply beyond a threshold complexity but with entirely different error signatures (wrong output dimensions entirely for ordering versus a single, spatially-localized relational error with over 99.9% cell accuracy for nesting), and where both failures are substantially recoverable by adding one in-context demonstration at the test-time complexity level rather than requiring more training or larger capacity, is a transferable diagnostic pattern for evaluating any in-context or few-shot reasoning system: a sharp performance cliff at a specific complexity level does not by itself distinguish a genuine capacity ceiling from a demonstration-coverage gap, and the specific "does adding one matched-complexity example recover the lost performance" test is a concrete, reusable way to tell the two apart.

The compositionality result, that the same nominal operation (color-layout permutation via "color swap") is learned reliably in one specific representational context (a fixed color layout shared across all training instances of that family) but fails almost entirely under an equally-valid but structurally different representation (shuffled/varied color layouts), and that this representational sensitivity persists into and confounds composition experiments built on top of it, is a transferable methodological caution for anyone studying operator composition in a learned system: an apparent "composition failure" can actually be a disguised "atomic acquisition failure under this specific representation," and disentangling the two requires checking each operation's isolated, uncomposed performance under the exact same representational conditions used in the composition test, which this paper does explicitly rather than assuming composition failures are informative about compositionality per se.

The specific finding that an LLM-generated evaluation cohort, even when explicitly designed and prompted to test a named operation category, can produce solve rates wildly different from a hand-authored cohort targeting the nominally same operation (an 80-100 percentage-point gap for the same "gravity" or "counting" mechanic depending on whether tasks were hand-written or LLM-generated from a mechanic name) is a transferable and consequential warning for anyone building or relying on LLM-generated benchmark stratifications: a benchmark's per-category solve-rate table can primarily reflect how a generator instantiates a requested category rather than the intrinsic difficulty of that category, and this paper's practice of directly comparing hand-written against generated instances of the same nominal category is a concrete, reusable check for this specific confound.

## Verification

This paper's evaluation methodology includes several genuine strengths for an industry system-demonstration paper. An independent black-box audit was conducted and reported in separate, cited technical reports, using a documented protocol without model-weight access, and reproduced the headline 29.5% pass@2 score; while the auditors are themselves paper co-authors (from institutions distinct from the system's developer, Pathway), this is a real attempt at external verification with a documented, weight-blind protocol rather than an internal, unaudited self-report. Confidence intervals (Wilson intervals throughout), appropriate paired statistical tests (McNemar for paired binary outcomes, Fisher's exact tests for the controlled-ladder manipulations, permutation tests for the mechanic-solve-rate spread), and explicit control conditions (an opaque-identifier-and-mixed-batch replication to rule out a specific request-side confound; a same-structure "does the marker change anything" control for the conditional-rule-selection ladder; an isolated-versus-composed comparison to disentangle atomic-acquisition failure from genuine composition failure) are used throughout rather than the paper resting on single point estimates. The paper is also candid about several null and inconclusive results it could have omitted or downplayed: the identifier/batch replication showing no aggregate effect, the MIN-versus-STANDARD effort comparison being statistically unresolved, and the mechanic-stratified solve-rate table being explicitly disclaimed as confounded by generator authorship rather than presented as an intrinsic difficulty ranking.

The most consequential verification gap is that the system's core architecture, its recurrent memory update rule, the exact structure and dimensionality of the latent reasoning workspace, and the complete training recipe (beyond high-level data provenance) are explicitly stated to remain proprietary; this means the paper's central architectural and mechanistic claims cannot be independently verified, reproduced, or falsified by a reader in the way an open-weights or fully-specified-method paper would allow, and the "independent audit" that does exist is a black-box behavioral check (does the system output the same score under a documented protocol) rather than a check of whether the described architecture actually implements the claimed recurrent-memory-plus-latent-workspace mechanism. A second gap concerns the headline cost-efficiency claim: the reported $0.00070-per-task figure is the authors' own computed estimate from measured hardware time under an assumed $3/H200-hour rate, for a system that (unlike the GPT-5.6 Luna or Claude points it is compared against) is not independently available via a metered public API with market-determined pricing; the paper's own figure caption discloses that some other leaderboard points (HRM, TRM) are likewise "leaderboard-reported compute estimates rather than API prices," which somewhat levels this comparison, but a reader should still treat the specific dollar figure as a vendor-computed estimate rather than an externally-verified, market-priced cost. Finally, several of the paper's most granular findings (single-digit-out-of-ten ConceptARC family counts; 40-task-per-condition controlled ladders) carry wide, explicitly-acknowledged confidence intervals, and the paper is appropriately careful to flag these as descriptive profiles rather than precise, statistically separable rankings.

## Field context

The paper positions itself directly against two adjacent lines of prior work it distinguishes carefully: continuous/latent-reasoning language models (Coconut's continuous hidden-state feedback with a staged internalization curriculum; recurrent-depth and looped-Transformer architectures that repeatedly apply a shared block to scale test-time compute), which the paper credits with establishing that a single continuous state can carry multiple candidate hypotheses or search frontiers in parallel, but which it argues have not combined this with genuine in-context task acquisition from demonstrations; and task-trained recursive solvers for ARC specifically (HRM and TRM), which the paper argues are transductive rather than genuinely in-context, since their pipelines require backward-pass optimization on each evaluation task's own augmented demonstration pairs (with a learned per-puzzle identity embedding) before that task can be solved, at a reported cost roughly three orders of magnitude higher than BDH-CQ's per the ARC Prize leaderboard's own figures. The paper explicitly frames its own contribution as targeting the specific conjunction neither prior line addresses: writing task information into recurrent memory purely through in-context demonstrations (no puzzle-specific optimization) combined with latent, non-verbalized iterative computation to solve the resulting query. Within this corpus, the closest connection is [reviews/2602.11451.md](2602.11451.md) (LoopFormer), reviewed earlier: that paper addresses a closely related but distinct problem for looped/recurrent-depth Transformers, that such models are typically trained and evaluated at one fixed loop count and degrade ungracefully if run with fewer loops at inference time, and proposes an explicit "how far along, how big a step" conditioning signal plus a training objective that forces short- and long-loop-count runs to agree, so that a single trained model can be run at any inference-time loop budget with quality scaling smoothly; this paper's own Section 7 finding, that pass@2 rises monotonically with a training-exposed "reasoning effort" setting (LOW to MEDIUM to HIGH) and that a lower-effort MIN configuration trades a modest, statistically inconclusive accuracy loss for roughly a third of the cost, is an empirical instance of exactly the elastic-compute-versus-quality tradeoff LoopFormer's architectural mechanism is specifically designed to make well-behaved, though this paper does not describe (or, given its proprietary scope, disclose) whether its own reasoning-effort mechanism uses a comparable explicit conditioning-and-consistency-training approach.

## Critical discussion

The paper's controlled-experiment program is a genuine strength: rather than reporting only a headline leaderboard score, it systematically varies single factors (propagation distance, copy count, sequence length, nesting depth, binding count, panel count, chain length, parameter-value coverage) with appropriate statistical tests and control conditions, and it draws a specific, well-supported distinction between failure to extrapolate (recoverable by matched-complexity demonstration support) and failure of execution capacity or representation-dependent atomic acquisition (not recoverable this way), which is a more mechanistically informative characterization than a single aggregate accuracy number would provide. The independent, weight-blind black-box audit and the paper's own candid reporting of null results and statistically unresolved comparisons both count in its favor as good-faith scientific reporting practices for an industry system paper.

The paper's most significant limitation is that its core architectural and training claims are explicitly non-reproducible and non-verifiable by an outside reader: the recurrent memory update rule, the latent reasoning workspace's structure, and the training recipe are all stated to be proprietary, so a reader cannot independently check whether the reported behavior actually arises from the specific "recurrent memory plus separate latent workspace" mechanism the paper describes, as opposed to some other architectural choice achieving similar benchmark numbers; this is a categorically different and more consequential limitation than the usual small-sample-size caveats the paper itself already flags throughout, since it bears on whether the paper's central mechanistic story is even checkable in principle. Related to this, the headline "new state of the art in cost efficiency" framing rests on a self-computed hardware-cost estimate for a system not independently available at a market-determined price, compared on the same plot against a mix of real API prices and other systems' own compute-cost estimates; the paper's transparency about which comparison points are estimates versus market prices is a mitigating factor, but the specific "$0.00070 per task" figure and the resulting "Nx cheaper" framings should be read as a vendor's own cost accounting rather than an externally-verified market price comparable to, say, an OpenAI API rate.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the specific, mechanistically-interpretable behavioral findings from the controlled ARC-like experiments (the propagation/copying-versus-ordering/nesting extrapolation asymmetry; the demonstration-coverage recovery effect; the operation- and representation-dependent composition results; the conditional-rule-selection and absent-parameter-value cliffs) are each backed by explicit statistical tests and, in several cases, purpose-built control conditions, and would remain informative characterizations of the deployed system's black-box input-output behavior even if the underlying proprietary architecture were later disclosed to work differently than described; and the black-box-audited reproduction of the headline ARC-AGI-1 score, while not a full architectural verification, is a real, documented check against the possibility of a one-off or misreported result.
