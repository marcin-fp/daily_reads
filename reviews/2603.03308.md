Citation: Simhi, A., Barez, F., Tutek, M., Belinkov, Y., Cohen, S.B. Old Habits Die Hard: How Conversational History Geometrically Traps LLMs. ICML 2026. arXiv:2603.03308, 2026.

## What this paper is about

If an LLM hallucinates, refuses, or flatters the user once in a conversation, is it more likely to do so again a few turns later? This paper shows yes, and asks a more interesting follow-up question: is that behavioral "stickiness" visible directly in the model's internal representations, not just in its outputs? The authors build HISTORY-ECHOES, a framework that measures the same phenomenon two ways at once — externally, by treating whether a phenomenon (hallucination, refusal, or sycophancy) occurred at each conversational turn as a two-state Markov chain and checking whether the chain "sticks" to its current state; and internally, by checking whether the model's hidden representations for phenomenon-present and phenomenon-absent turns are geometrically far apart, and whether transitions between those states are geometrically "complete" or leave a residual trace of where the model just was. The two measures turn out to agree closely with each other, which the authors interpret as evidence that behavioral persistence has a literal geometric signature: a "trap" in representation space that makes it harder for the model to fully leave a behavioral state once it has entered it.

## Extended summary

To study carryover effects without needing organically evolving multi-turn dialogue (which would be hard to standardize across models and phenomena), the authors construct simulated conversations from single-turn QA-style datasets: examples are ordered by embedding-similarity nearest-neighbor chaining to produce topically coherent sequences of 20 alternating user-model turns (100 conversations per model-dataset pair), with a shuffled version (D_inconsistent) used as a topic-incoherent control. Three phenomena are studied: hallucination (TriviaQA, Natural Questions — detected via substring match against the gold short answer), refusal (SORRY-Bench, Do-Not-Answer — detected via a list of refusal phrases or substring overlap with the prompt), and sycophancy (SycophancyEval, split into S-pos where the user states a correct answer and S-neg where the user states an incorrect one — detected via curated positive/negative agreement-word lists). The automatic labeling pipeline was manually validated against 900 hand-reviewed examples (50 per phenomenon per model across three open models), yielding a 6.5% average misclassification rate, with specific documented failure patterns (e.g., a correct answer phrased differently from the gold string being misclassified as hallucination; an implicit refusal with no keyword phrase being missed).

The probabilistic perspective models each conversation's phenomenon-present/absent sequence as a first-order two-state Markov chain and computes the trace of the estimated 2×2 transition matrix; a trace above 1 indicates the chain is "stickier" than history-independent behavior would predict (trace = 1 under no carryover effect). Averaged across three open-weight models (Qwen3-8B, GPT-OSS-20B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), traces range from 1.12–1.13 (hallucination datasets) to 1.57–1.59 (refusal datasets), with sycophancy in between (1.14–1.33), consistently exceeding 1 with reported standard deviations.

The geometric perspective computes, per model and dataset, the mean hidden-state vector (from the first answer token, at 85% relative model depth by default) for phenomenon-present and phenomenon-absent turns from a held-out basis set, builds a 2D orthonormal basis from these two means via Gram-Schmidt, and projects a separate held-out analysis set of consecutive hidden-state pairs onto this basis. Two quantities are extracted: θ_ref, the angular separation between the two class means (a static measure of how geometrically distinct the phenomenon states are), and, for each of the four transition types (stay-negative, stay-positive, negative-to-positive, positive-to-negative), an optimal rotation angle estimated via 2D orthogonal Procrustes. θ_ref is largest for refusal (33.99–66.52° across models/datasets), smaller for sycophancy (14.64–28.15°), and smallest for hallucination (6.38–13.87°) — the same ordering as the probabilistic trace. Cross-state transition angles, normalized by θ_ref, mostly fall below 1 (average ratios of 0.77–0.94 across transition types), meaning an actual state change requires less rotation than the full static separation between class means — interpreted as the representation not fully "arriving" at the new state, i.e., retaining a geometric trace of where it came from.

The paper's central quantitative claim links these two perspectives directly: across all three open models and six datasets, the probabilistic trace and the geometric θ_ref show a Spearman correlation of 0.78 (p<0.0002), and this correlation exceeds 0.77 within each individual model. Several robustness/structural analyses follow. (1) On topically inconsistent conversations, θ_ref stays roughly the same but its correlation with the trace collapses (only a marginal trace increase as θ_ref rises), interpreted as the geometric separation persisting while the carryover mechanism itself is disrupted by topic incoherence — explicitly likened to how adversarial jailbreak suffixes work by breaking contextual coherence. (2) A higher-order Markov analysis (Δ_k, the marginal effect of a phenomenon state k turns back after conditioning on the intervening turns all showing the phenomenon) shows the largest effect at k=1 with a sharp drop at k=2–3, though the more distant effect remains positive rather than vanishing. (3) A layer-depth sweep (30%/50%/85%/100% relative depth) finds correlation exceeding 0.60 at every depth tested, peaking at 85% (upper-middle layers), consistent with prior work locating semantic concepts like truthfulness and refusal in similar layers. (4) A conditioned-effect check on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct holds the exact question text fixed and varies only the model's own prior-turn state, finding the same phenomenon-repetition pattern persists — evidence the effect is not simply an artifact of which specific follow-up questions happen to appear after a phenomenon occurs. (5) Applying the probabilistic perspective (only, since refusal datasets are excluded due to API-level content filtering) to GPT-5 and Claude-Opus-4.5 finds broadly similar trace values to the open models (1.02–1.40), offered as preliminary evidence that closed models may exhibit the same underlying geometric traps, inferred indirectly since their internal representations are inaccessible.

## Learnings

The most transferable idea is the dual-perspective design itself: pairing a black-box, model-agnostic Markov-chain measurement of behavioral persistence with a white-box geometric measurement of representational persistence, and then checking whether the two agree. This gives a portable template for validating an interpretability claim (here, "carryover effects have a representational cause") against an independent, non-mechanistic signal (here, purely behavioral consistency), rather than resting the claim on internal-representation analysis alone — a useful methodological pattern for interpretability work more broadly, independent of the specific phenomena studied here.

A second, more specific learning is the finding that different LLM behaviors differ systematically and consistently (across both perspectives and across model families) in how strongly they "stick": refusal is the most persistent, sycophancy intermediate, and hallucination the least persistent, and the paper offers a specific, testable explanation — that refusal is known to be mediated by a comparatively clean, single direction in representation space (citing Arditi et al. 2024), while hallucination is a broad umbrella covering many distinct underlying failure modes that are less likely to share one coherent internal representation. This gives a concrete, falsifiable hypothesis (representational coherence of a phenomenon predicts its carryover strength) rather than just an empirical ranking.

A third learning is the topic-coherence finding and its connection to jailbreaking: carryover effects (the correlation between the probabilistic and geometric signals) specifically dissolve under topically incoherent conversation history, even though the geometric separation between phenomenon states remains intact — meaning the mechanism that "traps" a model in a behavioral state depends on the surrounding context staying thematically consistent, which the authors connect to why injecting unrelated/adversarial tokens is an effective jailbreak strategy (it disrupts exactly this coherence-dependent mechanism). This is a mechanistically specific link between a robustness/safety observation (adversarial suffixes work) and a measured internal cause (coherence-dependent geometric persistence), rather than a coincidental analogy.

## Verification

The paper's verification effort centers on validating its own automatic phenomenon-labeling pipeline, which is the foundation for both the probabilistic and geometric analyses: a manual review of 900 examples (50 per phenomenon per model, across all three open models) found a 6.5% average misclassification rate, and the paper documents specific, illustrative failure cases for each phenomenon (a correctly-phrased-differently answer mislabeled as hallucination; an implicit, keyword-free refusal being missed; an ambiguous "neither agree nor disagree" sycophancy response). This is a genuine, disclosed validation step rather than an unchecked heuristic, and the specific failure examples let a reader judge whether the ~6.5% error rate is likely to bias the reported trends (the documented failures look like roughly symmetric labeling noise rather than a systematic bias toward or against detecting carryover, though the paper does not explicitly quantify directionality). The central correlational claim (Spearman 0.78 between the probabilistic trace and geometric θ_ref) is reported with a significance test (p<0.0002) and is shown to hold within individual models (>0.77) as well as pooled, which is a stronger standard than reporting only the pooled correlation. The paper is also explicit and correct about what this correlation does and does not establish: in its own Limitations section, it states directly that establishing a strong correlation between the two perspectives "does not strictly demonstrate causality," correctly scoping the central finding as an observed, robust association rather than a proven causal mechanism, and framing this observational result as a necessary foundation for future causal/interventional work rather than presenting it as already causal.

## Field context

The paper extends two separate literatures that it explicitly connects for the first time: work on LLM "carryover" or "snowballing" effects, where an initial hallucination or error compounds into further incorrect output (Zhang et al. 2024; Simhi et al. 2024; Bigelow et al. 2025), which the paper credits with documenting the surface-level behavioral phenomenon but not its representational basis; and work on latent-space geometry of specific LLM properties, particularly Marks & Tegmark (2024)'s linearly-separable "truth direction" and Arditi et al. (2024)'s finding that refusal is mediated by a single direction, both of which study representations within a single generation rather than across multi-turn history. HISTORY-ECHOES's contribution relative to both lines is explicitly the multi-turn, cross-perspective bridge: rather than asking whether a concept is linearly represented at one point, it asks whether behavioral persistence across turns is reflected geometrically, and validates this with an independent behavioral (Markov-chain) signal rather than representation geometry alone. It is also explicitly differentiated from concurrent work modeling LLM generation as Markov processes for other purposes — Kao et al. (2025) use a similar Markov lens to study how quickly unsafe trajectories get absorbed into safe behavior within a single generation, and Zhu et al. (2025) use it to estimate generation difficulty — whereas this paper's Markov chain operates at the level of discrete conversational turns and multiple distinct phenomena rather than within one generation's token-level dynamics. Within this corpus, the paper's finding that a model's own hidden-state geometry can "trap" it in a behavioral mode once entered is a mechanistic, representation-level instance of a pattern this batch of reviews (from the prior session) repeatedly found at a more system-level scale — that once an evaluative or training signal locks a system into a pattern, escaping that pattern becomes disproportionately hard — here demonstrated at the level of a single model's internal geometry across dialogue turns rather than at the level of RL training dynamics or evaluator design.

## Critical discussion

The central methodological choice — simulating "conversations" by chaining semantically similar but otherwise unrelated single-turn QA examples via embedding nearest-neighbor ordering, rather than using organic multi-turn dialogue — is explicitly acknowledged by the authors as a limitation, and the tradeoff they cite (tractability and cross-model, cross-phenomenon comparability without needing model-specific follow-up question generation) is a reasonable engineering choice for a first study of this kind. However, it does mean the paper's "conversational history" is a narrower and more artificial construct than naturalistic multi-turn dialogue: each "turn" is a topically-adjacent but otherwise independent trivia/refusal/sycophancy prompt rather than a follow-up question that references or builds on the model's prior answer, so it remains an open question whether the same geometric-trap dynamics would be observed, weakened, or strengthened in dialogue where later turns genuinely depend on earlier model outputs (e.g., a user pushing back on a previous hallucinated claim), which is arguably the setting where carryover effects matter most in practice. This is a real scope limitation on how far the paper's findings should be generalized to real-world chat usage, even though it does not undermine the internal validity of the specific claim the paper makes about its own simulated setting.

A second, more localized limitation concerns the closed-model extension (Section 6.5), which is the paper's evidence that the phenomenon might generalize beyond open-weight models where representations are inspectable: it uses only the probabilistic perspective (necessarily, since hidden states are unavailable), excludes refusal entirely due to API-level content filtering, and is evaluated on only two of the six datasets per closed model (NaturalQA, TriviaQA, S-pos, S-neg) with no separately reported manual validation specific to closed-model outputs (the paper's manual-validation study covers only the three open models). The resulting closed-model trace values are broadly consistent with the open-model trends, which is a reasonable and appropriately hedged ("initial indication") piece of supporting evidence, but the geometric half of the paper's central correlational claim — the part that gives the "trap" language its literal meaning — remains untested for any closed model, so the extrapolation from "closed models show similar probabilistic consistency" to "closed models may also be subject to internal geometric traps" is an inference the paper itself flags with "may" rather than a demonstrated result.

What remains well supported after these caveats: within the paper's own simulated-conversation setting, the correlation between probabilistic state-persistence and geometric state-separation is demonstrated with appropriate statistical rigor (significance testing, within-model as well as pooled correlations, multiple robustness checks across layer depth, Markov order, and a question-held-fixed conditioning check that rules out the simplest confound of prior-state effects being driven merely by which follow-up question happens to appear next); the phenomenon-specific ordering (refusal > sycophancy > hallucination in both trace and θ_ref) is consistent across both independent measurement perspectives and across all three open models, which is a meaningfully corroborated finding rather than an artifact of one metric or one model; and the topic-incoherence control experiment is a genuine, informative manipulation (not just a correlational observation) showing that the correlation specifically depends on conversational coherence, strengthening the paper's causal-adjacent narrative about coherence-dependent persistence even though the authors correctly stop short of calling the overall probabilistic-geometric link itself causal.
