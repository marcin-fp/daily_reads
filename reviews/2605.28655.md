Citation: Gao, S., Fang, A., Zitnik, M. AutoScientists: Self-Organizing Agent Teams for Long-Running Scientific Experimentation. arXiv:2605.28655, 2026.

## What this paper is about

Most autonomous research-agent systems either follow one reasoning thread or split work up-front according to a plan a central coordinator sets at the start. That works poorly for long-running experimentation, where which directions are worth pursuing changes as evidence accumulates — some hypotheses stop paying off, some emerge only after earlier results are analyzed, and repeating a dead end wastes compute. This paper introduces AutoScientists, a team of LLM agents that coordinates without any central planner: agents read a shared experimental record, propose and critique experiment ideas on a shared forum before any compute is spent, organize themselves into teams around promising directions, run experiments in parallel, and reorganize when a team's progress stalls. The paper tests it on biomedical ML tasks, GPT-training optimization, and protein-fitness prediction, comparing it against single-agent baselines under matched experimental-compute budgets.

## Extended summary

The system alternates between a discussion phase (agents read the task spec, current champion program, and forum history, then propose, critique, and organize into K teams around research directions, without any predefined space partition) and an execution phase (each team runs a continuous propose-execute loop: Analyst agents read the experiment log, identify untested directions, and post ranked proposals; Experiment agents claim proposals, run them, and write results back). All results — including failures — are visible to every agent across every team via a shared state with four components: the current best program (champion), a full experiment log, a structured forum for proposals/critiques/analyses, and per-team dead-end registries. When a team's last several experiments fail to beat the champion, agents reopen discussion and may merge, split, or retire teams. Noisy evaluation metrics are handled by requiring a second-seed confirmation before an improvement is promoted to champion. The system outputs a final model plus a model card and a "research findings report" documenting which directions were tried, which failed and why, and how the final model emerged — explicitly designed as an audit trail, not just an endpoint.

Three evaluation settings are reported, all using the same LLM backend (Claude Sonnet 4.6 via Claude Code) for AutoScientists and its main single-agent comparator, Autoresearch (Karpathy's single-agent iterative-refinement system), so that orchestration structure is the only varied factor. (1) BioML-Bench (24 end-to-end biomedical ML tasks: imaging, drug discovery, protein engineering, single-cell omics): AutoScientists reaches a mean leaderboard percentile of 74.40% (SE 6.20) versus 66.07% (SE 7.38) for Autoresearch under matched per-domain compute, and completes all 24 tasks; the largest gain is in drug discovery (47.91%→64.52% relative to Biomni), while protein engineering is largely saturated (both AutoScientists and Autoresearch reach 96.97%, differing only in mean rank). Additional baselines (MLAgentBench, AIDE, STELLA) are reported from BioML-Bench's own published numbers rather than independently re-run under this paper's matched-budget setup. (2) GPT nanochat training optimization (Autoresearch's own benchmark): starting from the same baseline checkpoint, AutoScientists reaches a target validation bits-per-byte in 34 experiments versus 65 for single-agent Autoresearch (1.9× fewer experiments), attributed to three teams (architecture/schedule/optimizer) advancing multiple axes concurrently versus the single-agent loop advancing one axis per experiment. Starting instead from an AutoScientists-discovered champion with an identical shared "dead-ends" file, AutoScientists accepts 7 of 93 further experiments (reaching val_bpb 0.9730) while Autoresearch accepts 0 of 100, with the first AutoScientists improvement (query-key normalization order) never having been proposed by the single-agent loop in any of its 100 attempts. (3) ProteinGym: starting from Kermut (the strongest existing supervised-substitution baseline) and developing for 10 cycles on a single held-out-difficulty assay (ACE2-Spike binding), AutoScientists discovers a three-GP ensemble (structure kernel + expanded zero-shot features + greedy diversity feature selection + quantile-warped targets) that improves Spearman's ρ from 0.747 to 0.840 (+12.5% relative) on that development assay; frozen without further modification and applied to the full 217-assay ProteinGym supervised-substitution benchmark, it improves the official average Spearman's ρ from 0.657 to 0.700 (+6.5% relative), while MSE increases slightly (+0.006), a trade-off the paper attributes to the rank-oriented quantile-warping and model-selection choices and flags explicitly as a limitation rather than omitting it.

A four-way ablation (removing the Analyst role, cross-agent feedback/critique, self-organization/reorganization, or all coordination — "independent agents" running solo loops with no shared state) is run on four tasks (TDC-hERG, Cell-Cell Communication, Human Plasma-Protein Binding, GPT nanochat). The full system wins every task on its primary metric, but the single most damaging ablation differs by task: removing the Analyst hurts most on TDC-hERG (AUROC 0.867→0.738); removing cross-agent feedback hurts most on Human Plasma-Protein Binding (Pearson 0.873→0.714); removing self-organization hurts most on GPT training (val_bpb 0.9777→0.9833); and removing all coordination hurts most on Cell-Cell Communication (Odds Ratio 0.924→0.435, the largest proportional drop reported). The paper reads this as evidence the four mechanisms address complementary rather than redundant failure modes. The Limitations section is explicit that AutoScientists uses more total LLM tokens than the single-agent baseline (same order of magnitude, per Appendix Table S8) and that the paper's central comparisons hold experimental-compute (GPU-experiment count) fixed rather than total LLM inference cost — the system is framed as improving experiment selection under a fixed experimentation budget, not as being more token-efficient per se.

## Learnings

The most transferable design idea is the negative-knowledge mechanism itself: making the dead-end registry (failed directions plus the reason they were rejected) a first-class, cross-team-readable part of the shared state, rather than private per-agent memory. The from-champion GPT-training result is the clearest demonstration of its value: with an identical dead-ends file, the multi-agent system still finds 7 improvements where the single-agent loop finds 0, and the paper traces this specifically to a wider set of hypotheses being considered (query-key normalization order was never tried by the single-agent loop in 100 attempts) rather than simply more compute — a useful, mechanistically grounded distinction between "explores more broadly" and "explores more."

A second learning, echoed by the ablation's own finding that no single removed component is most damaging on every task, is that different failure modes in long-running agentic search are addressed by genuinely different mechanisms: proposal quality (Analyst), partial-signal correction (cross-agent critique), redirecting when a direction is exhausted (self-organization), and avoiding duplicated or conflicting effort (shared state). This is a useful decomposition for anyone designing a multi-agent research system, since it argues against treating "more agents" or "more discussion" as a single lever and instead suggests diagnosing which specific failure mode a task exhibits before choosing which coordination mechanism to invest in.

A third, more specific learning from the ProteinGym result is the value of explicitly reporting the generalization gap between a development-time result and a frozen, unmodified deployment across a much larger benchmark: the 12.5% relative gain on the single development assay shrinks to a still-real but smaller 6.5% relative gain across all 217 assays, and the paper reports both numbers rather than only the more favorable one — a useful template for honestly characterizing how much of a single-task optimization result actually transfers.

## Verification

Verification here operates mainly at the level of experimental hygiene rather than claim-checking. The clearest mechanism is the second-seed confirmation requirement before an experimental improvement is promoted to champion, an explicit acknowledgment that the evaluation metric can be stochastic (e.g., training-run seed variance) and a concrete guard against promoting noise as progress — a lightweight but real analogue to the "verified registry" mechanisms seen elsewhere in this batch (2605.20025's AutoResearchClaw). The peer-critique mechanism (proposals are debated on a shared forum before any experiment compute is committed) is explicitly framed as filtering weak ideas cheaply, but it is worth noting this critique is performed entirely by agents drawn from the same LLM backend and role population as the proposers (all agents use Claude Sonnet 4.6 via Claude Code) — there is no independent or adversarially-different model checking proposals, so systematic blind spots or biases shared across same-model agents would not be caught by this mechanism, a same-family-judge limitation the paper does not discuss. Beyond this, the paper's headline comparisons are held to a fixed backend and fixed experimental-compute budget across systems, which is a genuine controlled-comparison strength (isolating orchestration structure as the one varied factor between AutoScientists and Autoresearch specifically), but the additional BioML-Bench baselines (MLAgentBench, AIDE, STELLA) are drawn from that benchmark's own previously published numbers rather than independently reproduced under this paper's matched-budget protocol, so those specific comparisons carry weaker guarantees of a controlled, apples-to-apples setting than the Autoresearch comparison does.

## Field context

The paper positions itself against two adjacent design patterns it discusses directly: single-agent iterative refinement systems (AIDE, Autoresearch) that follow one search trajectory, and multi-agent systems that still coordinate through a central structure — a planner that decomposes the problem up front, or debate/voting frameworks (Du et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024's ReConcile) that use discussion to converge on one shared hypothesis. AutoScientists' explicit differentiator is using discussion only to filter weak proposals before execution while letting agents continue pursuing genuinely different directions in parallel afterward, rather than converging to consensus — a distinction the paper draws precisely rather than vaguely. Within this corpus, AutoScientists sits alongside 2605.20025 (AutoResearchClaw) as a second, independently-developed multi-agent research-automation system published within roughly the same window, both benchmarking specifically against single-agent baselines (AIDE appears as a baseline in both papers) and both reporting a second-seed or verification-gate mechanism to guard against noise; the two differ in scope (AutoScientists targets long-running iterative experimentation on fixed benchmarks with a self-organizing team structure, while AutoResearchClaw targets the full idea-to-paper pipeline with structured debate roles), giving a useful natural comparison point for how independent groups are converging on similar coordination primitives (shared state, dead-end tracking, verification gates) while differing in which stage of the research lifecycle they optimize. AutoScientists' finding that decentralized, non-consensus coordination outperforms a single search trajectory under a matched compute budget also connects to 2605.18661's cross-cutting survey observation (Section 7.1.4) that multi-agent systems can introduce coordination problems and that "more agents do not automatically improve performance" — this paper's ablation, which explicitly shows each coordination mechanism addresses a different, task-specific failure mode rather than uniformly helping, is a concrete illustration of that same survey's point that architecture must match task structure rather than simply adding agents.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central efficiency claims (1.9× fewer experiments to a target loss; +8.33 leaderboard-percentile points) are computed under a matched experimental-compute (GPU-experiment count) budget, which the paper itself states explicitly is not the same as a matched total-cost comparison: AutoScientists uses more LLM tokens than the single-agent baseline (Limitations, referencing Appendix Table S8), reflecting the overhead of multiple agents' discussion, critique, and reorganization. This is honestly disclosed rather than hidden, and the paper correctly scopes its claim to "improves experimental search under a fixed experimental-compute budget," but a reader taking away only the headline percentages should be aware they describe experiment-count efficiency, not overall (GPU-plus-LLM-inference) resource efficiency, and the two could diverge in either direction depending on relative GPU and inference costs in a given deployment.

The peer-critique mechanism that is central to the system's design story (filtering weak proposals before committing compute) is performed exclusively by same-backbone, same-role-population agents (all Claude Sonnet 4.6 via Claude Code), which is a same-family-judge concern of the kind flagged repeatedly across this batch's system papers (2605.20025, and the survey 2605.18661's discussion of standalone-reviewer leniency): a systematic blind spot shared by the underlying model across all agent personas would not be caught by internal critique, since no genuinely independent evaluator (a different model family, or held-out ground truth) checks the critique process itself. The ablation study, while genuinely informative about which mechanism matters where, is also run on only four tasks with what appears to be a single run per (task, ablation) cell — no error bars, confidence intervals, or repeated-seed variance are reported in Table 3 for the ablation results specifically (in contrast to the main BioML-Bench comparison, which does report standard errors), so the specific claim that "no single mechanism dominates across all four tasks" rests on point estimates from a fairly small, single-run evidence base and should be read as suggestive of complementary mechanisms rather than statistically established. Finally, only two of the systems compared on BioML-Bench (Autoresearch, and implicitly Biomni) appear to have been run under this paper's own matched-budget protocol; the additional baselines shown in Figure 3 (MLAgentBench, AIDE, STELLA) are drawn from BioML-Bench's previously published numbers, so comparisons against those specific systems carry a weaker guarantee of controlled, apples-to-apples conditions than the head-to-head Autoresearch comparison the paper's own narrative emphasizes.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the head-to-head comparison against Autoresearch under an identical backend and matched GPU-experiment budget is the paper's most tightly controlled result, and it shows a clear, mechanistically explained advantage (parallel teams advancing multiple axes versus one axis at a time) rather than an unexplained aggregate gap; the from-champion GPT-training result (7 accepted improvements vs. 0) is a particularly strong piece of evidence because it holds the starting point and negative-knowledge file identical between systems, isolating orchestration as the source of the difference; and the ProteinGym result's honest reporting of the gap between development-assay gain (+12.5%) and full-benchmark gain (+6.5%) is a credible, non-cherry-picked generalization test rather than a single-number claim.
