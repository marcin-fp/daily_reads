Citation: Li, J. and Ho, A. GeneBench-Pro: Evaluating Multistage Statistical Reasoning in Genomics, Quantitative Biology, and Translational Biomedicine. bioRxiv, 2026.

## What this paper is about

In genomics and quantitative biology, the bottleneck has shifted from generating data to turning messy, real-world data into a trustworthy conclusion that a downstream scientific or clinical decision can rely on. That process is not one well-defined calculation but a chain of judgment calls: which quality-control issues actually matter, which statistical model or estimand fits the situation, and whether an intermediate diagnostic result means the original analysis plan needs to change. GeneBench-Pro, from OpenAI, is a 129-problem benchmark built specifically to test this chain of judgment rather than any single well-scoped analytical step, spanning genetics, cancer genomics, pharmacogenomics, proteomics, and other domains, with every problem built from a fully simulated dataset whose correct answer is knowable exactly. The paper's central finding, after testing dozens of frontier models, is that even the strongest systems solve well under a third of these problems, and that failures usually trace not to models missing the relevant clue in the data, but to models noticing a clue and then failing to actually change their analysis in response to it.

## Extended summary

The paper motivates its design by contrasting most existing biology benchmarks, which begin from a curated dataset and a highly specified prompt and evaluate one narrowly scoped, cleanly verifiable analysis step, against real-world scientific analysis, which starts from data of unknown quality and an open-ended goal and requires deciding what question is even answerable, performing quality control and exploratory analysis, choosing models and estimands, diagnosing failures, and iterating before reaching a decision-relevant conclusion. GeneBench-Pro problems each provide a "minimum viable prompt" (brief context and a target estimand, without prescribing a workflow) and staged data files meant to resemble what an analyst would actually receive from an assay, EHR, or biobank pipeline, and each problem is structured around 3 to 13 "decision points," inferential forks where a plausible wrong choice propagates into a wrong final answer, with a median of 6 across the 129-problem suite.

The paper is explicit about the central methodological challenge this kind of benchmark faces: real, open-ended analyses often admit multiple defensible approaches (different QC thresholds, model parameterizations, or reporting conventions), so a benchmark built around a real historical dataset risks penalizing an equally valid but unanticipated analysis choice as a "failure," conflating benchmark ambiguity with genuine reasoning difficulty. GeneBench-Pro addresses this by building every problem from a fully simulated data-generating process (DGP) with a completely known causal structure, so that the graded ground truth is the answer recoverable from the agent-visible data under the intended correct method (not the underlying simulation parameter itself, which can differ due to sampling variation). A dedicated ablation suite for each problem verifies that plausible-but-incorrect analysis choices and shortcut methods yield answers that are materially, numerically separated from the graded answer, so that a wrong analysis cannot accidentally be scored as correct. Table 1 lays out the full set of design constraints used to keep problems both scientifically realistic and cleanly gradable, covering ground-truth recoverability, answer uniqueness, numerical separation from incorrect answers, minimal and non-prescriptive prompting, QC-threshold robustness, multi-stage inferential structure, correct handling of nuisance variables and representation choices, ablation-validated workflow adjudication, failures distributed across multiple decision points rather than concentrated in one, and reliance on literature-defensible rather than benchmark-specific methods.

Building on the authors' earlier GeneBench, GeneBench-Pro withdrew 3 of the original 103 problems for fatal issues, substantially redesigned and hardened 54 of the remaining 100, and added 29 new problems, for a total of 129 problems across 10 primary domains and 21 terminal subdomains, extending beyond genomics into pharmacogenomics, cancer somatic genomics and liquid biopsy, microbial genomics, forensic genetics, clinical variant interpretation, proteomics, and specialized molecular-omics settings. A distinguishing addition relative to GeneBench is external scientific review: 84 candidate problems were reviewed by 11 domain experts (graduate students, postdoctoral researchers, industry scientists, and professors) with access to the full private construction materials, not just the solver-facing prompt; 82 of these problems were retained in the final suite (two were withdrawn for fatal issues), and the ten publicly released problems were all conditional on having passed this review. Table 2 documents specific review findings and their resolutions, including cases of underspecified estimands (resolved by fully specifying the intended estimand in the answer contract), an alternate defensible method that had to be explicitly accommodated in grading, contrived or unrealistic setups, and, notably, two cases where the review caught errors in the benchmark's own ground truth: one where the reference implementation of a named statistical method (LDSC) was found to not actually implement that method correctly (using an incorrect estimator and sample-size term), and one where a reviewer traced an implausible simulated parent-child correlation to a coding bug in the data-generating process's own formula, both of which were fixed at the source rather than papered over in the prompt.

An illustrative clinical-genomics problem (Figure 3) concerns residual reproductive risk after a negative carrier screen for a recessive condition, given five staged data tables (screened adults, a full partner roster, calibration controls, target metadata, and raw assay observations). Getting the correct answer requires distinguishing true reportable carrier calls from copy-number segmental-duplicate mimics, correctly collapsing or keeping separate phased founder marker calls depending on whether they represent the same or different haplotypes, and standardizing partner carrier frequency to the entire partner roster rather than only the subset that happened to be tested; the paper shows each of these three specific shortcuts explicitly as red nodes that resolve only part of the analysis and lead to numerically distinct wrong final answers, concretely illustrating the ablation-based separation principle from Table 1.

The full evaluation covers 60 model configurations (the GPT-5.2/5.4/5.5/5.6 Luna/Terra/Sol families across multiple reasoning-effort settings, corresponding GPT Pro Extended runs, and roughly 14 non-GPT models and variants from Claude, Gemini, Grok, GLM, Kimi, DeepSeek, MiMo, Tencent, MiniMax, and Qwen), with 10 independent attempts per problem for standard runs (5 for GPT Pro Extended and Claude Opus), binary grading via deterministic Python scripts checking exact-match and numeric-tolerance criteria against pre-specified target fields (not an LLM judge), and 95% confidence intervals from a hierarchical bootstrap resampling both problems and repeated runs. The strongest configuration, GPT-5.6 Sol at maximum reasoning effort, reaches a 28.7% mean pass rate across the 129 problems (a separately reported GPT Pro Extended variant reaches 31.5%); pass rate rises monotonically and substantially with reasoning effort within this model (3.7% at no reasoning, up to 28.7% at maximum). Across the mainline GPT-family progression, pass rate rises from 4.9% (GPT-5.2) to 8.9% (GPT-5.4) to 12.0% (GPT-5.5) to 16.5%, 23.3%, and 28.7% for the GPT-5.6 Luna/Terra/Sol variants respectively. The strongest non-GPT baseline, Claude Opus 4.8 at maximum reasoning, reaches 16.0%, with most other non-GPT baselines well below 5%. A substantial unsolved tail persists even for the best model: 45.7% of problems remain at a 0% pass rate for GPT-5.6 Sol at maximum reasoning (down from 77.5% for the weakest configuration tested), while the share of problems reaching at least 50% pass rate rises from 1.6% to 30.2% across that same progression. The paper explicitly discloses that its externally distributed 50-problem Artificial Analysis subset, used for independent third-party benchmarking, shows systematically lower pass rates than the full 129-problem suite for most reported rows, including the strongest GPT-family configurations (for example, GPT-5.6 Sol at maximum reasoning reaches 28.7% on the full suite but 16.3% on the Artificial Analysis subset), and states plainly that these release and review-status strata "were not randomized."

The paper's central qualitative finding comes from manual review of model-reported reasoning across matched GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol attempts on the same problems (Table 3). In several representative cases, both models correctly identify the relevant local diagnostic signal (for example, that treatment initiation and biomarker measurement interact dynamically in a pharmacogenomic survival analysis, implying treatment-confounder feedback), but the weaker model only partially adjusts its downstream method (continuing with a standard time-varying Cox model), while the stronger model carries the same diagnosis through to a substantively different and methodologically appropriate choice (a new-user marginal structural model with inverse-probability weighting). The paper frames this "notice-act gap," rather than a pure perception or knowledge gap, as the main qualitative driver of the reasoning-effort scaling pattern, and draws an explicit analogy to documented expert-novice differences in human scientific problem solving, where experts integrate observations into a broader problem representation while novices treat them as isolated local issues. The paper is explicit that decision-point counts are currently used as qualitative metadata rather than a quantitative stratification of results.

The discussion situates GeneBench-Pro against a broader landscape of long-horizon agentic benchmarks (general software-engineering suites, broad expert-level science suites, and prior biology-specific agent benchmarks), arguing that realistic, open-ended, multi-stage scientific analysis of the kind central to genomics and translational biomedicine remains comparatively underexamined. It frames the benchmark's binary, all-or-nothing grading as a deliberate design choice meant to mirror real translational stakes (a mostly-correct analysis reaching the wrong decision-relevant answer has not successfully automated the workflow), while acknowledging this discards useful partial-progress information and proposing stage-level or rubric-based auxiliary scoring as future work. It offers a rough economic framing (a typical problem might take a human analyst 10-40 hours at $100-200/hour, putting the labor cost of a single problem at several thousand dollars) to motivate why even partial, reliable automation of this class of work could be operationally valuable, while concluding that current frontier models remain too unreliable to replace that labor despite substantial partial competence.

## Learnings

Building every problem in an open-ended scientific-reasoning benchmark from a fully simulated data-generating process, rather than a real historical dataset, and then explicitly validating with an ablation suite that every plausible-but-wrong analysis path produces a numerically distinct wrong answer, directly targets a documented and endemic failure mode in prior long-horizon biology benchmarks: unacknowledged ambiguity about what counts as the single correct analysis, which otherwise silently conflates benchmark artifacts with genuine reasoning difficulty. This is a concrete, transferable construction template (not just a general principle) for any benchmark meant to measure judgment-heavy, open-ended scientific analysis, regardless of domain.

Independent external review, when granted access to a benchmark's full private construction materials rather than only its solver-facing prompt, can catch errors in the ground truth itself, not just prompt ambiguity. Reported findings from this paper's review process include a case where the benchmark's own reference implementation of a named statistical method was found to be wrong, and a case where an implausible simulated value was traced to a coding bug in the data-generating process's own formula. This is a concrete demonstration that even a carefully simulation-grounded benchmark can encode an incorrect ground truth without independent expert scrutiny of the private construction materials specifically, not merely the public problem statement.

Across a specific worked comparison, the qualitative difference between a stronger and a weaker model attempting the same multi-step statistical analysis was not primarily about whether the model noticed an important diagnostic signal in the data (both models often did), but about whether that signal was actually propagated into a corresponding change in the downstream analytical method, rather than being noted and then set aside while the original plan continued unchanged. This "notice-but-don't-act" pattern is a specific, checkable failure signature, distinct from either a pure knowledge gap or a pure perception gap, worth searching for directly in the traces of any long-horizon agentic evaluation.

## Verification

This paper is substantially about verification at the level of benchmark construction, even though it does not propose a verifier for use inside a deployed agent. Ground truth is established from a fully simulated, known data-generating process rather than an ambiguous historical dataset's "accepted" answer, and grading is entirely deterministic, binary exact-match and numeric-tolerance checks against pre-specified target fields via Python scripts, explicitly avoiding an LLM-as-judge for the primary metric. The paper goes a step further than simply asserting its grading is sound: it builds a dedicated ablation suite specifically to verify that the grading itself discriminates for the right reasons, confirming that plausible-but-wrong analyses produce numerically distinct, clearly incorrect answers rather than landing close enough to pass by chance. On top of this internal check, an external human-expert review layer, with access to the full private construction materials, serves as a second, independent verification pass targeting whether the benchmark's ground truth and problem framing are themselves scientifically defensible, and it is shown concretely to have caught both prompt-level ambiguity and at least one outright implementation error in a reference "correct" method. This multi-layered approach, verifying the grading logic, then separately verifying the ground truth and problem design against independent expert judgment, is among the more rigorous instances of build-time verification-of-the-verification-apparatus reviewed in this corpus.

## Field context

This is the second iteration of the authors' own GeneBench, extending it toward harder problems, broader domain coverage, and a substantially strengthened external-review layer, in much the same direction that this corpus's review of 25tpbench documents for physics: a narrower or smaller predecessor extended toward a harder, more externally validated benchmark for research-level, multi-step scientific reasoning in a specific technical domain. The paper explicitly positions itself against general long-horizon agentic benchmarks in software engineering and broad expert-level science (which it argues do not specifically target the messy-data-to-decision workflow central to computational life science) and against prior biology-specific agent benchmarks, several of which it names directly and critiques for being narrower in scope or built around real historical datasets whose inherent ambiguity undermines confident grading, a critique the paper's own simulation-and-ablation methodology is designed to address. Within this corpus, its central qualitative finding, a "notice-act gap" where models perceive a relevant signal but fail to translate it into a revised course of action, is a domain-specific instance of the broader theme visible in reviews of 25tpbench (models resorting to literature recall rather than genuine derivation once a problem gets hard) and 24selfcor (models struggling to translate a correct judgment about an error into an actual correction), now demonstrated in the very different setting of open-ended applied statistical and genomics workflow judgment rather than physics derivation or simple self-correction.

## Critical discussion

The benchmark-construction methodology here is a genuine strength: the combination of a fully simulated, known DGP, an explicit ablation requirement that wrong analyses be numerically well-separated from the correct one, and an external-review layer with documented, specific, sometimes ground-truth-correcting findings (Table 2) is substantially more rigorous than most cited prior long-horizon biology benchmarks' approach to the "is this benchmark measuring what it claims" problem, and the paper is transparent about exactly how many problems went through review (82 of 129) and what kinds of issues were found, rather than asserting validity without evidence.

At the same time, the paper is both benchmark designer and lead evaluator of its own flagship model family: OpenAI constructed GeneBench-Pro, wrote its grading criteria, selected and iteratively hardened its problems partly through its own frontier-model pilots, and is also the organization whose own GPT-5.x models top every reported leaderboard row by a wide margin over external competitors. The deterministic, non-LLM grading of individual problems meaningfully mitigates the most common form of same-family-judge concern (a model grading a sibling model's free-text output), and this should be credited directly; but the broader structural pattern, the same organization designing, selecting, and reporting results for a benchmark on which its own models are shown to lead, is not addressed in the paper and remains a real consideration for how much weight to place on the specific ranking of GPT-family models relative to competitors (same-family-judge, at the level of benchmark construction and reporting rather than per-item grading). The paper's own disclosed comparison between the full suite and the independently distributed Artificial Analysis subset partially substantiates this concern in a concrete, quantified way: for most reported rows, including the strongest GPT-family configurations, pass rates on the external subset are noticeably lower than on the full suite, and the paper states plainly that these strata were not randomized, so the reader cannot fully distinguish a genuine difficulty difference between subsets from a possible exposure or selection effect favoring the self-reported full-suite numbers. The paper's central qualitative "notice-act gap" finding is demonstrated through a small number of hand-selected worked comparisons between two specific models rather than a systematic count of how often this pattern occurs across the full suite, and the paper is explicit that decision-point counts are used only as qualitative metadata in this report rather than a quantitative stratification, so this compelling explanation should be read as a plausible, illustrative account consistent with the observed reasoning-effort scaling curve, not yet a directly quantified benchmark-wide measurement.

What remains well supported: the headline quantitative results, a clear, monotonic capability gradient across model generations and reasoning-effort settings, are demonstrated on a large, methodologically well-validated 129-problem suite with genuine statistical rigor (hierarchical bootstrap confidence intervals accounting for both problem-level and run-level variance), and the paper's own disclosed limitations (binary grading collapsing partial progress, non-randomized release-subset comparisons, the inherent inability of simulated data to reproduce the full documentation gaps of true historical analyses) are specific and match what the reported evidence can support, rather than functioning as a generic disclaimer.

## Relevance to us

This paper is directly and substantively relevant to our own benchmark-construction and verification work (B01, B10, B12), offering a close cross-domain parallel to what we are trying to build for physics. Its single most transferable idea is the construction discipline itself: building every evaluation problem from a fully simulated, known data-generating process specifically so the correct answer is provably recoverable, then explicitly validating via an ablation suite that every plausible-but-wrong analysis path is numerically distinguishable from the correct one, is exactly the discipline our own physics-benchmark and verification-target construction should adopt for any problem admitting more than one plausible analysis path, which describes most interesting physics problems; Table 1's design-constraint list is a concrete, reusable checklist rather than only a general principle. The external-review findings in Table 2 are a sharp, concrete instance of a concern already live in our own PVRE-line work: the case where a reviewer caught that the benchmark's own reference implementation of a named method was itself statistically wrong, and the case where a reviewer traced an implausible ground-truth value to a simulator coding bug, are non-hypothetical demonstrations that a verifier or benchmark's own construction needs independent verification, not only an agent's output does, in an adjacent scientific domain rather than only argued abstractly. The paper's central qualitative finding, a "notice-act gap" where models correctly flag a relevant diagnostic signal but fail to propagate it into a correspondingly revised downstream method, is a concrete, transferable failure signature to check for directly in our own physics-reasoning evaluations: does a model that correctly notices a symmetry constraint or an invalid approximation regime actually revise its subsequent derivation accordingly, or does it note the observation and continue along its original path regardless. This is checkable at the level of individual reasoning traces, distinct from either a pure knowledge gap or a pure perception gap, and worth building explicit trace-level checks for in our own evaluations. Finally, the paper's own candid limitation, that its self-reported full-suite numbers are somewhat higher than its independently distributed third-party subset for most models, is a useful, concrete illustration of why genuinely independent, held-out evaluation subsets should be prioritized over self-reported headline numbers when assessing any physics-reasoning system, including our own.
