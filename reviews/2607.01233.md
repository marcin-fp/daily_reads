Citation: Chen, Z., Zhao, Y., Cohan, A. Measuring the Gap Between Human and LLM Research Ideas. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01233v1

## What this paper is about

When an LLM is asked to brainstorm a research idea, is it exploring the same space of possible contributions that human researchers do, or a narrower slice of it? Prior work mostly scores individual generated ideas for novelty or feasibility one at a time; this paper instead asks a distributional question, whether the whole population of ideas a model tends to produce looks like the population of ideas real researchers actually pursue. To make the comparison fair, both humans and nine LLMs are given the exact same starting point, a small set of closely related prior papers, and are asked what new idea that context suggests. Labeling thousands of resulting ideas with a two-axis taxonomy of "why is this gap worth pursuing" and "how does the contribution address it," the paper finds LLM ideas cluster heavily around a narrow "connect existing things" template that real papers use only rarely.

## Extended summary

The evaluation corpus is built from 11,683 real published papers (5,994 from ICLR/ICML/NeurIPS 2023-2026, 5,689 from Nature Communications 2023-2025, spanning 71 scientific disciplines). For each paper, an LLM-assisted extraction pipeline identifies the paper's innovation, its departure from prior work, and its key insight, then rewrites this into a structured motivation-and-method proposal (the "human idea"), and separately reverse-engineers 4-8 closely related prior works (by title and abstract only) that plausibly preceded and inspired it. Nine evaluated LLMs (Claude-Sonnet-4.6, Gemini-3.1-Pro, GPT-OSS-20B/120B, GPT-5.4-mini, Qwen3-8B/32B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash/Pro) are then given only this same reconstructed prior-work context and asked to generate their own new motivation-and-method idea, yielding paired human and LLM idea corpora conditioned on identical inputs, which the authors argue controls for topic-selection and generic-format confounds that plague more open-ended ideation comparisons.

Every idea (human or LLM) is labeled along two independent axes by an LLM annotator (GPT-5.4-mini): an opportunity-pattern axis (what kind of gap motivates the work, e.g., contradiction, missing explanation, scope mismatch, disconnected literatures/bridge opportunity, failure/risk, resource bottleneck; 7 categories) and a method-paradigm axis (what contribution strategy addresses that gap, e.g., synthesis/unification, scope extension, robustification, formal derivation, empirical mapping, artifact construction, optimization; 7 categories). The taxonomy itself was built by first reviewing NSF/NIH/AHRQ/DARPA research-proposal guidance, then iteratively refined by merging, splitting, and renaming candidate categories using a held-out set of 150 papers until the final 7x7 scheme was reached. The same 150-paper set was then used to validate the GPT-5.4-mini annotator against two authors' own independent judgments, reporting Cohen's kappa of 0.84 (opportunity label), 0.81 (method label), and 0.93 (a third diagnostic dimension), with disagreements checked to concentrate in semantically adjacent labels rather than reflecting systematic category collapse.

The central result is a large, consistent distributional gap. Human ideas have high normalized entropy on both axes (>0.92, i.e., spread broadly across categories), while every one of the nine tested models is more concentrated (opportunity-axis entropy 0.550-0.758; method-axis entropy 0.723-0.879). Even the closest model on each axis (Gemini-3.1-Pro on opportunity, TVD 0.348; Claude-Sonnet-4.6 on method, TVD 0.211) requires reallocating over a fifth to a third of its probability mass to match the human distribution. The specific direction of the gap is a strong, consistent overrepresentation of "bridge/fragmentation" opportunities (framing the gap as disconnected literatures, methods, or evidence streams that should be connected) and "synthesis/unification" methods: only 12.1% of human ideas are opportunity-labeled as bridge-type and only 5.1% are method-labeled as synthesis, versus 47.1-64.2% and 22.5-38.7% respectively across the nine LLMs. This pattern held under a full-paper-context ablation (giving two models LLM-generated summaries of the full related-work papers instead of just abstracts, on a 1,000-paper subset), which did not move either tested model closer to the human distribution and in fact slightly increased TVD/JSD on both axes, and under alternative generation prompts (reported in an appendix), neither of which changed the qualitative pattern.

Three complementary diagnostic scores (surface stitching, bottleneck specificity, boilerplate; each 0-3, assigned by the same LLM annotator) show most models score worse than the human baseline on specificity and genericness, with Qwen models showing the strongest degradation (highest surface-stitching flags, up to 20.6% of Qwen3-8B's ideas flagged as superficial combinations) and Claude-Sonnet-4.6 as a partial exception, scoring comparably to or better than the human baseline on these diagnostics while still remaining distributionally shifted on the taxonomy axes, indicating the gap is not purely a low-level fluency or quality issue that better-diagnostic-scoring models simply avoid.

A reasoning ablation produces a result that runs against a common assumption: enabling "thinking" mode for Qwen3-8B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash increases, rather than decreases, both models' distance from the human distribution and further concentrates their outputs (Qwen3-8B: bridge opportunities rise from 49.7% to 71.1%, opportunity entropy drops from 0.658 to 0.481, opportunity TVD from humans rises from 0.382 to 0.590; DeepSeek-V4-Flash shows the same direction at smaller magnitude). The authors interpret this as reasoning sharpening a model's preferred ideation template rather than broadening exploration toward more human-like diversity.

Mechanism analyses probe why the synthesis/bridge pattern recurs. Rewriting every proposal into a one-sentence archetype and clustering the main verbs into operation families finds "integrate" appears in 34.2% of model-generated archetypes versus 2.35% of human ones (log-odds 3.07), with other synthesis-like verbs (unify, design, merge, adapt) similarly overrepresented in models; human ideas instead concentrate on local-intervention verbs largely absent from model outputs, especially "replace" (9.13% human vs. 0.92% model) and "decouple" (2.33% vs. 0.21%), which also score higher on bottleneck specificity and lower on boilerplate than model-heavy clusters. Embedding every idea into a shared representation space finds that ideas from two different model families (Qwen3-8B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash) generated from the same input paper are more similar to each other (cosine 0.8316) than either is to the corresponding human idea (0.7242 and 0.7829 respectively), and that human proposals sit more diffusely relative to their prior-work set (higher entropy over similarity to individual prior works, and a higher combined diffuseness score) than either model's proposals. A concept-enrichment analysis finds the specific technical concepts LLMs gravitate toward (multi-omics integration, diffusion policy, multimodal generation, in-context learning, test-time adaptation) are high-frequency, well-represented technical motifs, whereas human-enriched concepts (trajectories/tracking, ligand-molecule interactions, tokenization, equivariance, entropy/mutual information) are narrower and more mechanism-specific, together supporting a picture in which LLM ideation follows a recognizable recipe (pick a salient, well-known technical concept, then apply a safe integrate/unify operation to another nearby concept) rather than generating a genuinely diverse range of contribution types.

The authors' stated limitations (their own enumerated list) include: the corpus, while broad, remains STEM-centered and may not generalize to social science, humanities, or engineering design; the literature-grounded task reconstructs only a local context and cannot capture tacit expertise, failed attempts, collaboration, or reviewer feedback that shape real human ideation; the taxonomy and LLM annotation, while human-validated, necessarily compress nuanced ideas into discrete labels; and the study covers a finite set of models, prompts, and single-shot generations, so interactive agents, retrieval-heavy pipelines, or different prompting strategies might narrow the observed gap. The work was supported by NSF award No. 2541654.

## Learnings

The specific, reusable diagnostic pattern here, comparing not individual output quality but the shape of the full output distribution across a structured taxonomy, is a transferable methodology for any setting where per-item LLM outputs look individually reasonable but the question is whether the model explores a space as broadly as a reference population does; the paper's own demonstration that this distributional view surfaces a large, consistent gap invisible to per-idea novelty/feasibility scoring is a concrete argument for preferring distributional over item-level evaluation whenever a "does the model just produce one kind of good idea" concern applies.

The counter-intuitive finding that enabling extended reasoning/thinking mode made two different models' idea distributions measurably less human-like and more concentrated, rather than more diverse, is a specific, well-isolated result worth generalizing cautiously: it suggests reasoning-time compute can sharpen a model's existing preferred template rather than broaden its exploration, which is a useful caution against assuming that "more reasoning" is a general-purpose fix for narrow or repetitive generation, and is worth checking directly in any other setting where reasoning mode is proposed as a diversity intervention.

The mechanism-level finding that two structurally different open-weight models (Qwen3-8B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash) converge on more similar ideas to each other (cosine 0.83) than either does to the human idea generated from the same input (0.72-0.78) is a specific, measurable instance of cross-model homogenization in a generative task, distinct from and complementary to prior text-detection-style findings that LLM output differs systematically from human output; it suggests that whatever narrows LLM ideation relative to humans may be a shared property across model families and training regimes, rather than an idiosyncrasy of any single model's training data or alignment procedure.

## Verification

The paper's core distributional-comparison methodology is well-controlled in one specific, important way: by conditioning both the human idea (as reconstructed) and every LLM's generated idea on the identical prior-work context, it removes topic-selection and generic-paper-template confounds that would otherwise make a human-vs-LLM ideation comparison difficult to interpret. The taxonomy itself is grounded in an external, non-arbitrary source (funding-agency proposal guidance) rather than invented ad hoc, and its calibration process (iterative refinement against a held-out set, explicit merging of near-duplicate categories, checking that annotator disagreements concentrate in semantically adjacent categories rather than reflecting wholesale category collapse) is a reasonable, disclosed procedure. The central finding is triangulated through several largely independent analytical lenses (taxonomy-label distributional distance, three separate diagnostic scores, archetype-verb clustering, embedding-space diffuseness, and concept-enrichment log-odds) that converge on the same qualitative picture rather than resting on a single metric, and two robustness checks (full-paper context, alternative prompts) were run specifically to test whether the pattern was an artifact of the abstract-only input or a particular prompt wording, in both cases finding the pattern persists.

Two specific verification gaps are worth naming. First, the "human idea" used throughout is not the original paper's text but an LLM-extracted-and-rewritten reconstruction of it into the same structured motivation/method format used for LLM-generated ideas; the paper validates the taxonomy-labeling step (via Cohen's kappa against human raters) but does not report a comparable, separate validation that this upstream LLM extraction step faithfully preserves what the human authors actually intended, as opposed to already smoothing the original idea into a more template-like or LLM-legible form before the comparison even begins. Second, GPT-5.4-mini plays three simultaneous roles in the pipeline: it is one of the nine LLM idea generators being evaluated, it is the taxonomy-label annotator scoring every idea (including its own), and it is the archetype-rewriting tool in the mechanism analysis; while the taxonomy-labeling validation (Cohen's kappa against two human raters) provides some independent check on annotator accuracy in general, the paper does not report whether GPT-5.4-mini's self-generated ideas are labeled with the same accuracy, or with any systematic leniency or strictness, as ideas from other models, leaving open the possibility of a same-model self-annotation effect specific to that one evaluated system.

## Field context

The paper positions its distributional framing as a deliberate departure from the dominant per-idea evaluation paradigm in the LLM-ideation literature (novelty/feasibility/impact scoring, as in the widely-cited large-scale human study of NLP researcher-judged ideas, and various idea-generation and refinement pipelines it cites), and connects to a separate literature on systematic human-versus-LLM output gaps in text detection and social simulation, extending that comparison specifically to the distribution of research contributions rather than surface text statistics. Within this corpus, the closest and most mechanistically illuminating connection is [reviews/2510.01171.md](2510.01171.md) (Verbalized Sampling), reviewed earlier: that paper traces post-training "mode collapse" (aligned models converging on a narrow set of near-identical outputs even when a base model demonstrably knows many alternatives) to a typicality bias baked into human preference data used for RLHF-style alignment, and shows that simply asking a model to verbalize several candidate outputs with probabilities recovers much of the lost diversity without retraining. This paper's own findings, that LLM research ideas cluster tightly around a "safe," familiar synthesis/integration template, that this narrowing is not fixed (and is in fact worsened) by extended reasoning, and that different model families converge on similar narrow outputs, are all consistent with, and read naturally as a domain-specific instance of, the same general alignment-induced mode-collapse mechanism Verbalized Sampling identifies at a more general level; a natural, testable extension this paper does not itself run would be to check whether a verbalized-sampling-style intervention narrows the specific research-taste gap documented here. A secondary, more tangential connection is [reviews/2504.09389.md](2504.09389.md) (Measuring LLM Novelty), which proposes a general originality-times-quality metric for creative LLM output; that paper's broader finding, that different levers (scale, post-training, prompting) move novelty through different mechanisms, is a useful general frame for interpreting why this paper's specific lever (extended reasoning) moves research-idea diversity in the opposite direction from what a naive "more compute helps" story would predict.

## Critical discussion

The paper's controlled, shared-context experimental design is a genuine methodological strength that isolates the comparison of interest (what kind of idea does a source produce from a given context) from confounds that plague more open-ended ideation comparisons, and the convergence of five largely independent analytical approaches (taxonomy distributions, diagnostic scores, archetype clustering, embedding diffuseness, concept enrichment) on the same qualitative conclusion is meaningfully stronger evidence than any single metric would provide on its own; the negative full-paper-context and reasoning-mode ablations are also a credit to the paper, since a less careful study might have stopped at the abstract-only, no-reasoning condition and left the robustness of the central finding untested.

Two points temper how far the specific numbers should be read as a clean measurement of "the gap between human and LLM ideas" as opposed to "the gap between LLM-processed human ideas and LLM-generated ideas." First, because the human-idea side of the comparison passes through an LLM extraction-and-rewriting step before being compared, and only the downstream taxonomy-labeling step (not this upstream extraction step) is validated against human judgment, some unknown fraction of the measured gap could in principle reflect systematic distortion introduced by the extraction pipeline itself (for instance, if extraction tends to flatten or template-ize the human paper's actual framing) rather than a pure difference between what human authors originally conceived and what LLMs generate; the paper does not report a check (such as comparing extracted human ideas against the paper's own abstract or authors' stated contribution in a spot-check) that would rule this out. Second, GPT-5.4-mini annotating its own generated ideas among the nine evaluated systems, without a reported separate accuracy check specific to that self-annotation case, leaves a same-model evaluation question open for exactly one of the nine systems studied; given that GPT-5.4-mini's own results (opportunity TVD 0.512, one of the two most human-distant models on that axis) do not stand out as suspiciously favorable, this particular gap is unlikely to be masking a large self-preference effect, but the paper does not close it directly.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the core distributional gap (LLM ideas concentrating heavily on bridge/synthesis-type contributions relative to a much broader human reference distribution) is large in magnitude, consistent across nine models spanning five different developers and multiple parameter scales, and robust to two specific ablations designed to test whether richer context or explicit reasoning would close it; and the mechanism-level evidence (archetype-verb overrepresentation, cross-model idea convergence exceeding human-model similarity, and technical-concept enrichment patterns) offers a coherent, multiply-triangulated account of what the narrowing actually consists of, a preference for combining well-known concepts via safe integration operations, rather than resting on the taxonomy-distance numbers alone.
