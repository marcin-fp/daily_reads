Citation: Press, O., Smith, N. A., Levy, O. Improving Transformer Models by Reordering their Sublayers. ACL 2020. arXiv:1911.03864.

## What this paper is about

A standard transformer layer alternates a self-attention sublayer and a feedforward sublayer in a fixed pattern (attention, feedforward, attention, feedforward, ...) with no particular justification for that specific order. This paper asks whether a different ordering of the same building blocks, with no new parameters and no extra compute, can do better. The authors train dozens of randomly-reordered transformer language models, find that models with more self-attention near the input and more feedforward near the output tend to do best, and use that observation to hand-design a new pattern, the "sandwich transformer," which reliably beats the standard ordering on several language-modeling benchmarks while performing no better (and no worse) on machine translation.

## Extended summary

Using notation where a transformer is a string over {s, f} (self-attention, feedforward), the paper starts from Baevski and Auli's (2019) strong 16-layer WikiText-103 language model (18.65 ± 0.24 perplexity over 5 seeds) as its baseline. Section 3.1 generates 20 random reorderings with the same 16 self-attention and 16 feedforward sublayers (same parameter count as the baseline) and trains each from scratch with unchanged hyperparameters: 7 of 20 beat the baseline's average, with the best reaching 18.19 perplexity, which the authors take as evidence "a better ordering than interleaving probably exists," while noting the baseline's own average still beats the average random model. Section 3.2 relaxes the constraint of equal attention/feedforward counts, generating 20 unbalanced models with the same total parameter budget (depth ranging 24-48 sublayers, since feedforward sublayers cost 2x a self-attention sublayer's parameters); only 4 of 20 beat the baseline average and both mean and median perform worse than the balanced sample, leading the authors to conclude balance is desirable, though not necessary, given the small sample. Section 3.3 is the paper's key empirical finding: slicing each generated model in half by parameter count (not layer count) and counting sublayer types per half, models that beat the average baseline systematically have more self-attention in the bottom half and more feedforward in the top half; the authors state plainly they have no explanation for this pattern but can exploit it.

Section 4 turns that observation into a manually-designed family, the sandwich_k^n transformer: s^k (sf)^(n-k) f^k, i.e., k pure-attention sublayers first, then the standard interleaved core, then k pure-feedforward sublayers last, with k (the "sandwich coefficient") as the only new hyperparameter (k=0 recovers the original transformer). Training all k in {0,...,15} for n=16 (same budget as baseline), all sandwich models except k=14,15 beat the average baseline; the best, k=6, reaches 17.84 perplexity on the validation set, roughly double the reported gap between the Baevski-Auli baseline and Transformer-XL, at no cost in parameters, memory, or training time (same hyperparameters, same epoch count). To check robustness, the authors retrain sandwich^16_6 with 4 additional random seeds (5 total) and report a mean development-set perplexity of 17.98 (SD 0.10) versus the baseline's 18.65 (SD 0.34) over the same number of seeds, a larger and more consistent margin than any single run would suggest, and evaluate the model on the WikiText-103 test set exactly once (18.63±0.26 baseline vs. Sandwich^16_6's single test-set score reported in Table 3, alongside Transformer-XL and kNN-LM for context; kNN-LM's much larger gain comes from an orthogonal nearest-neighbor memory mechanism the authors suggest could be combined with sublayer reordering).

Section 5 tests transfer beyond the original setting. On the Toronto Books Corpus (word-level, different domain, k tuned on dev in {4,...,8} around the WikiText-103-optimal k=6), sandwich^16_7 improves perplexity by 1.06 over baseline and roughly matches kNN-LM. On character-level modeling with Sukhbaatar et al.'s (2019) adaptive-span model as the new baseline (24 layers), the sandwich pattern is within baseline seed variance on text8 but gains about 0.007 bits-per-character on enwik8, matching the Compressive Transformer's state-of-the-art result while using 209M parameters against the Compressive Transformer's 277M and without Transformer-XL's slower recurrent attention. On WMT14 En-De machine translation, applying the sandwich pattern to either the encoder alone or the decoder alone (grouping self-attention and cross-attention as one unit for the decoder's reordering) produces no significant BLEU change in either direction across sandwich coefficients 0-5, including the most extreme decoder reordering (sc)^6 f^6, which performs almost exactly like baseline, mirroring the earlier finding that the most extreme language-model reordering (s^16 f^16) also matches baseline. The authors read this as showing transformer robustness to reordering generally, while the specific gain from this pattern is task-dependent rather than universal.

Section 6 offers one mechanistic hint without a full explanation: computing an attention-distance metric (Hungarian-matched, per-token, per-layer earth-mover's distance between corresponding attention heads of two trained models) between pairs of models, models with the same architecture (baseline-baseline or sandwich-sandwich) show smaller attention distances from each other than baseline-sandwich pairs do, indicating the reordering measurably changes what attention patterns the model learns, though the authors explicitly do not characterize what that difference consists of.

## Learnings

The paper's most transferable methodological move is using a small, cheap random search (a few dozen trained models, not the thousands typical of neural architecture search) purely to generate a hypothesis about structure, more self-attention early, more feedforward late, that is then hand-crafted into a simple, interpretable design rather than searched for directly. This human-in-the-loop pattern, cheap random probing to find a qualitative regularity, then manual design guided by that regularity, is a reusable alternative to full-scale architecture search whenever the search space is combinatorial but a plausible structural regularity might exist.

A second, more cautionary learning: the same intervention (reordering sublayers) produced a reliable, seed-robust gain on word- and character-level language modeling but no effect at all on machine translation, and the authors' own explanation is architectural, translation decoders have an extra cross-attention sublayer the reordering method treats crudely (grouped with self-attention rather than considered as its own axis), which is a concrete illustration of how an architectural finding validated on one task family can fail to transfer to a structurally different task without being wrong on its original domain.

## Verification

The paper's own empirical claims are checked with real statistical care for a paper of this era: every baseline number is reported as a mean ± standard deviation over 5 independently-seeded training runs rather than a single run, the sandwich^16_6 model's headline result is likewise re-verified across 5 seeds before being trusted (mean 17.98, SD 0.10, versus baseline's larger SD of 0.34, meaning the sandwich model is also more stable, not just better on average), and the authors explicitly restrict themselves to a single test-set evaluation per model to avoid the multiple-comparisons risk of tuning against the test set, doing all coefficient tuning (k selection) on validation/development data instead. The random-search claims in Section 3 (7 of 20, then 4 of 20 models beating the baseline average) are reported honestly as suggestive rather than conclusive, with the authors themselves flagging "no guarantees, given the small sample sizes." The attention-distance analysis in Section 6 is a genuine attempt at mechanistic verification of why the method works, but the authors are explicit that it identifies a measurable difference without explaining its qualitative nature, correctly distinguishing observation from explanation rather than overclaiming a mechanism.

## Field context

This paper sits within the immediate post-Transformer, pre-scaling-era wave of transformer architecture-modification papers (2018-2019): it explicitly compares against and cites Transformer-XL (Dai et al. 2019), the adaptive-span model (Sukhbaatar et al. 2019) used directly as a second baseline for character-level modeling, kNN-LM (Khandelwal et al. 2019) as the contemporaneous state of the art on WikiText-103, and the Evolved Transformer and other neural-architecture-search work, which it positions itself against methodologically (a few dozen trained models versus architecture search's typical thousands). Its closest direct predecessor is Lu et al. (2019), who proposed a related but distinct reordering (fsf per block instead of sf) at the cost of a 50% increase in depth and inference time; this paper's sandwich pattern is explicitly framed as achieving a comparable idea at no parameter, memory, or runtime cost. As a foundational architecture-design paper from 2019, predating the large-language-model and agentic-systems literature that makes up most of this corpus, it does not directly engage with retrieval, multi-agent coordination, or LLM-agent evaluation themes; it is included here as a piece of the pre-LLM architectural groundwork (interleaved self-attention/feedforward transformer blocks) that the models discussed elsewhere in this corpus are built on, rather than as a paper in active dialogue with them.

## Critical discussion

The core sandwich-transformer result is unusually well-supported for a paper of this scope: the headline gain (17.84 vs. 18.65 baseline perplexity) survives a 5-seed robustness check with a tighter standard deviation than the baseline itself, transfers with a smaller but real gain to a different word-level domain (Toronto Books Corpus) and to character-level modeling (matching the Compressive Transformer's state of the art at fewer parameters), and is honestly bounded by a negative result on machine translation that the authors report rather than omit. The single test-set evaluation policy and validation-only hyperparameter tuning are exactly the right discipline to avoid the most common failure mode in this kind of small-search paper (implicitly tuning to the test set).

The main place to apply caution is the random-search evidence in Sections 3.1-3.2, which is used to motivate the reordering hypothesis rather than to establish the final result, but is still presented with some rhetorical weight ("a better ordering than interleaving probably exists"). With only 20 random samples per experiment and a baseline standard deviation of 0.24-0.34 perplexity, finding that 7 of 20 (or 4 of 20) random models beat the average baseline is the kind of small-sample, multiple-comparisons-adjacent observation that could partly reflect run-to-run noise rather than a genuine population-level tendency; the paper does not report a statistical test controlling for this, though it does correctly hedge its own claim as directional rather than conclusive, and importantly the paper's actual final claim (the sandwich^16_6 design) is separately and more rigorously re-verified across 5 seeds, so the weaker evidentiary standard in the exploratory phase does not propagate into the confirmed result. A second, smaller limitation: the paper's own explanation for the machine-translation null result, that grouping self-attention and cross-attention as a single reordering unit is too crude, is offered as a plausible conjecture rather than tested directly (e.g., by trying a reordering that treats all three sublayer types independently), so it remains an open question rather than a demonstrated cause. What remains well supported: sublayer reordering, with zero added cost, produces a real, seed-robust improvement on language modeling across two granularities and two domains, transformers are broadly robust to sublayer reordering rather than fragile to it (even the most extreme reorderings tested do not catastrophically degrade performance), and the specific sandwich pattern's benefit does not generalize to machine translation as tested here.
