Citation: Wiemann, M. L., Smith, L. M., Melchior, P., Mishra-Sharma, S., Wilson, A. G., Izmailov, P., Cuesta-Lázaro, C. DiscoverPhysics: Benchmarking LLMs for Out-of-the-Box Scientific Thinking. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26087v2

## What this paper is about

Most physics benchmarks for LLMs test whether a model can solve or recall a known law, sometimes with the numbers changed, but real scientific discovery requires figuring out which variables matter and what kind of law applies in the first place, when nobody has told you the answer's shape in advance. This paper builds 22 simulated "worlds" governed by genuinely non-standard physics (screened or fractional-power forces, hidden particle species, extra dimensions, time-varying couplings) and asks an LLM agent to discover each world's law purely through active experimentation: proposing test particles, observing noisy trajectories over several rounds, and finally submitting both a plain-language explanation and working code for the inferred law. Across eleven frontier and open models, even the best agents pass only about half the worlds, and the paper finds that predicting well and understanding correctly are not the same thing — the model with the lowest prediction error is not the model that best identifies the actual physics.

## Extended summary

Each of 22 worlds (11 public, 11 held out to guard against contamination) is defined by a pairwise force law between particles carrying vector-valued "charges," simulated on demand by a symplectic N-body integrator (Yoshida4 by default) rather than shipped as static data. In a session, the agent gets a fixed round budget (up to 16), and in each round submits test-particle initial conditions and measurement times; the simulator returns noisy (5% Gaussian, position-only in the main results) trajectories, and the agent can request a least-squares parameter fit of a candidate law via a tool built on scipy.optimize. At the end of the budget, the agent must submit a natural-language explanation and a Python function implementing its inferred law. An optional "randomized" mode replaces the agent's chosen probe placements with a uniform grid, isolating experimental-design skill from pure data-analysis skill. Scoring uses two axes: normalized trajectory MSE on held-out, noise-free test particles, and an LLM-judged (claude-opus-4-6, not among the evaluated agents) explanation score against a human-written rubric per world (0-10 scale). A world counts as "passed" if normalized MSE < 0.1 and explanation score >= 0.9; pass@k is estimated by resampling without replacement from 5 seeds per model per world, 1,000 resamples.

Across 11 evaluated models, claude-opus-4-7 leads on explanation score and pass rate at every k (pass@5 = 50.0%), while gpt-5.5 achieves the lowest normalized MSE (0.002) without matching Opus's explanation score or pass rate (pass@5 = 36.4%) — the paper's central decoupling finding. Open-source models lag substantially: the best open model (Qwen3.5-397B-A17B) reaches pass@5 = 9.1%, and models below a certain capability threshold (gpt-oss-120b, gpt-oss-20b, Llama-3.3-70B) score exactly 0% at every k. A guided-vs-random experimentation ablation on the two strongest models shows claude-opus-4-7 improves monotonically across all three metrics as round budget grows and clearly benefits from choosing its own experiments over random ones, while gpt-5.5 plateaus in pass rate and explanation score after round 4 even though its MSE keeps improving — read by the authors as gpt-5.5 refining a fixed candidate law's parameters rather than revising its conceptual picture. A per-world noise ablation (Yukawa vs. Ether) shows explanation quality degrading with noise at very different rates depending on particle count and world structure, with claude-opus-4-7 notably retaining high explanation scores on Ether even at 50% position+velocity noise by explicitly reasoning about and designing around the noise (a transcript excerpt is given). A detailed post-hoc failure analysis (Appendix F, "summarize-then-classify," itself performed by claude-opus-4-7 as the analyzer model, with the authors explicitly flagging the same-model-as-subject caveat and mitigating it by releasing every per-run output for independent re-analysis) sorts all 50 failed runs (30/55 for Opus, 20/55 for GPT-5.5, on the 11 public worlds) into seven capability categories: the two models fail for substantially different reasons despite similar overall pass rates — GPT-5.5 concentrates on "considering the right kind of law" and numerical-integration robustness near singularities, while Opus 4.7 spreads across experimental design, writing code that matches its own stated reasoning, distinguishing real signal from noise, and knowing when to stop experimenting. Authors' stated limitations: worlds are curated (deliberately unusual but human-designed) rather than procedurally novel, are small-scale with no instrumental systematics beyond simple Gaussian noise, the explanation score relies on a single judge model (mitigated only partially by the counter-intuitive observation that this Claude-family judge actually ranks GPT-5.5 above Opus 4.7 on several dimensions at low round budgets, which the authors argue is evidence against naive same-family favoritism), and pass/fail thresholds (10% MSE, 0.9 explanation score) are somewhat arbitrary, mitigated by reporting continuous metrics alongside pass@k. Simulator, public world definitions, and evaluation framework are released (gated HuggingFace access for the full public+private suite).

## Learnings

The dual-metric design (predictive trajectory MSE alongside an independent, rubric-graded conceptual explanation score) is a directly reusable pattern for any agentic-science benchmark that wants to distinguish "fits the data" from "understood the mechanism" — and the paper's headline result (the lowest-MSE model is not the highest-explanation-score model, and gains less explanation score from additional experimentation) is a concrete demonstration that these two things can and do diverge in practice, not just in principle, which is a useful cautionary data point for anyone using held-out predictive accuracy as a stand-in for scientific understanding.

The seven-axis failure taxonomy (considering the right hypothesis class; handling integrator singularities; designing discriminating experiments; writing code that matches stated reasoning; distinguishing signal from noise; acting on parameter-fitting tool feedback; knowing when to stop experimenting) is a transferable failure vocabulary for evaluating any agent doing iterative, self-directed empirical work, and the finding that two models with near-identical aggregate pass rates (45% vs. 64%) fail for almost entirely disjoint reasons is a strong argument against relying on a single aggregate accuracy number to characterize an agent's scientific competence — two agents can look equally capable in aggregate while having qualitatively different, non-overlapping weaknesses.

## Verification

Verification is a central, well-executed part of this paper's own design, not an afterthought. Predictive correctness is checked against held-out, noise-free test particles the agent never observes, which is a clean ground-truth check independent of the LLM judge. The explanation score is checked against a human-written rubric per world (available in the appendix), reducing (though not eliminating) judge subjectivity. The authors directly confront the most obvious threat to their own evaluation's validity — that Claude Sonnet/Opus models serve as both benchmarked agents and the explanation judge (claude-opus-4-6) — and offer an empirical counter-argument rather than only a disclaimer: the judge rates gpt-5.5's explanations above claude-opus-4-7's at three of four tested round budgets, which is the opposite of what naive same-family favoritism would predict. This is a genuine, falsifiable robustness check, though it is not the same as using a fully independent judge model (unlike, for instance, a GPT-family second-judge replication), and the authors themselves note it "does not fully resolve the concern." The Appendix F failure-mode analysis carries an analogous, similarly self-acknowledged limitation: claude-opus-4-7 serves as both a subject being evaluated and the analyzer classifying its own and a competitor's failures, mitigated by releasing every per-run classification output for external audit or re-analysis with a different model, though no such independent re-analysis is reported within the paper itself. Statistical treatment (bootstrapped standard errors with 5,000-10,000 resamples, pass@k computed via repeated resampling without replacement) is reported consistently throughout.

## Field context

DiscoverPhysics positions itself explicitly against a specific, well-articulated gap in prior interactive-physics-discovery benchmarks: NewtonBench, PhysGym, and Gravity-Bench (all cited and contrasted directly) alter parameters or restrict variables within an otherwise-known canonical law, leaving the "which variables matter and what functional family applies" question largely given rather than probed — a gap this paper's non-canonical, hidden-structure worlds (extra particle species, dark-matter-like sources, extra dimensions) are specifically designed to close. It is also positioned against more ambitious end-to-end "AI Scientist" systems (The AI Scientist and similar), which the paper argues are typically evaluated by LLM-judged paper quality rather than recovery of an objective, ground-truth mechanism — a critique directly relevant to two AI-for-science agent systems already reviewed in this corpus, [reviews/2510.09901.md](2510.09901.md) ("Autonomous Agents for Scientific Discovery") and [reviews/2510.26887.md](2510.26887.md) (the Denario project), both of which orchestrate broader research pipelines without the kind of objective, simulator-verified ground truth this paper insists on. It is most directly complementary, within this same reviewed corpus, to [reviews/2604.00149.md](2604.00149.md) ("Towards Verifiable and Self-Correcting AI Physicists for Quantum Many-Body Simulations"): both papers build physics-specific, code-executable ground-truth verification into an agentic evaluation loop rather than relying purely on LLM judgment of correctness, though DiscoverPhysics targets open-ended law discovery from experimentation while 2604.00149 targets faithful reproduction of a specified published result — two different, complementary slices of "can an agent do real physics work" that a downstream synthesis could usefully compare.

## Critical discussion

The benchmark's core design decisions are well justified and represent a genuine methodological advance over parameter-shifted-canonical-law benchmarks: worlds are truly non-canonical rather than superficially altered, ground truth is objectively checkable via held-out simulation rather than solely LLM-judged, and the guided-vs-random ablation directly demonstrates (rather than assumes) that the benchmark rewards genuine experimental design skill, since performance would not differ between guided and random conditions if agents were simply pattern-matching to the shape of returned data. The MSE-vs-explanation decoupling finding is credible because it holds up under an independent held-out test set and a separate rubric-graded metric, not a single conflated score.

The most significant limitation is evaluation circularity that the paper discloses candidly but does not fully close: the same model family (Claude, specifically claude-opus-4-6/4-7) simultaneously appears as one of the top-scoring benchmarked agents and as the sole explanation judge, and also as the analyzer in the Appendix F failure-mode taxonomy that is used to characterize both its own and a competitor's weaknesses. The authors' mitigation — noting that the judge sometimes rates gpt-5.5 above claude-opus-4-7 — is a real and relevant data point, but it addresses only the crudest form of same-family bias (systematically inflating the family's own scores); it does not rule out subtler biases, such as the judge structurally favoring explanations phrased in a Claude-like style or reasoning pattern regardless of correctness, which a same-family judge is architecturally well-positioned to reproduce without necessarily inflating raw scores. A second, related limitation is scale: the headline capability-gap taxonomy in Appendix F is built from 50 failed runs across only the 11 public worlds (not all 22), split roughly 30/20 between two models, meaning most individual capability-category counts in Table 2 are single-digit — informative as a qualitative pattern (the two models clearly fail for different reasons) but too small a sample to support fine-grained quantitative claims about the relative frequency of any one capability gap. The benchmark's own stated scope limitation — small particle counts, no instrumental systematics beyond simple Gaussian noise, human-curated rather than procedurally generated worlds — is honestly presented and appropriately narrows any claim to "discovery of hand-designed non-canonical mechanics in an idealized noise setting," not general scientific discovery. What remains solidly supported: current frontier models can, some of the time, design informative experiments and revise conceptual hypotheses under genuine (not merely parameter-shifted) physical novelty, but predictive accuracy and correct conceptual understanding are measurably dissociable capabilities in the models tested, and the specific ways two comparably-performing frontier models fail are qualitatively different rather than a shared, generic ceiling.
