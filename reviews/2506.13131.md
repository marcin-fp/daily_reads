AlphaEvolve: A coding agent for scientific and algorithmic discovery. arXiv, 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131v1

## What this paper is about

Many problems in mathematics and computing can be attacked by writing a program whose output can be scored automatically: a construction that gives a better bound, a faster algorithm, a cheaper circuit. This white paper from Google DeepMind describes AlphaEvolve, a system that improves such programs by evolution. Language models (Gemini 2.0 Flash and Pro) propose code edits, a user-written evaluation function executes and scores each new program, and a database of scored programs supplies parents and examples for the next round. The authors report that it found 14 matrix-multiplication algorithms with fewer multiplications than previously known, including 48 complex multiplications for two 4 by 4 matrices (Strassen's recursive scheme needs 49). It matched the best known constructions on about 75% of a curated set of more than 50 open mathematical problems and improved on about 20%, including a kissing configuration of 593 spheres in 11 dimensions. It also produced heuristics and code changes that are now deployed in Google's data-center scheduler, Gemini training kernels, a TPU arithmetic circuit and a compiled attention kernel. The authors are explicit that the approach applies only where an automated evaluator can be written.

## Extended summary

**System.** The user supplies an initial program with blocks marked for evolution (comment markers `EVOLVE-BLOCK-START`/`END`), an `evaluate` function mapping a program to a dictionary of scalar metrics to maximize, and optional configuration. The initial program can be rudimentary, for instance functions that return constants. A distributed asynchronous loop repeats: sample a parent and "inspiration" programs from the database; build a prompt; ask an LLM for code changes as SEARCH/REPLACE diff blocks (or a full rewrite for short code); apply the diff; evaluate the child; store it with its scores. Prompts can include explicit context (human instructions, equations, code, literature such as PDFs), stochastic formatting of template alternatives, rendered evaluation results, and meta-prompt evolution, where instructions suggested by the LLM are co-evolved in a separate database. The LLM ensemble mixes Gemini 2.0 Flash, for throughput, with Gemini 2.0 Pro, for occasional higher-quality proposals. The program database is described only as inspired by MAP-Elites and island population models. Evaluation supports a cascade of increasingly hard test stages, with small-scale filtering first; optional LLM-graded properties such as simplicity, added to the scores or used to discard programs; parallelized evaluation (the authors say sample efficiency makes on the order of 100 compute-hours per candidate evaluation feasible); and multiple metrics. The authors report that optimizing several metrics often improves a single target metric, and suggest diverse high performers in the prompt may cause this; no data is shown for this. Table 1 contrasts AlphaEvolve with FunSearch. FunSearch evolved a single Python function of 10 to 20 lines, needed evaluation of at most 20 minutes on one CPU, used millions of samples from small LLMs with no benefit from larger ones, and optimized one metric. AlphaEvolve evolves whole files of hundreds of lines in any language, can evaluate for hours on accelerators, needs "thousands" of samples, benefits from frontier LLMs, and optimizes multiple metrics. The authors also note that the same problem can be encoded at different levels of abstraction: the raw solution, a constructor function, a bespoke search algorithm, or co-evolved solution and search. They hypothesize that constructor functions suit highly symmetric solutions and search algorithms suit non-symmetric ones.

**Matrix multiplication (Section 3.1, Appendix A).** The task is to find low-rank decompositions of the matrix-multiplication tensor ⟨m,n,p⟩, where rank equals the number of scalar multiplications. The starting program was a standard gradient-based decomposition (initializer, reconstruction loss, Adam). The evaluator runs the evolved program on several targets from multiple random seeds through the cascade. The score is the best rank reached and the fraction of seeds that reach it. To guarantee exactness, each entry is rounded to the nearest integer or half-integer at evaluation, and the prompt asks in natural language for near-integral solutions. The program behind the 4 by 4 result, shown in Figure 4 and Figures 9a to 9c (the code extract is garbled but readable in parts), took 15 mutations. It changed the optimizer to AdamW with weight decay, rescaled the complex initialization, added gradient and parameter noise, soft clipping with a cyclically annealed threshold, noise on the target tensor, random "hallucination" replacement of entries, a scheduled discretization loss toward integers and half-integers, a large-value penalty, and a wider hyperparameter sweep. Of 54 targets (roughly 2 ≤ m,n ≤ 5, sorted m ≤ n ≤ p), AlphaEvolve matched the best known rank in 38, improved it in 14 and fell behind in 2 (Table 3). The improvements are ⟨2,4,5⟩ 33 to 32, ⟨2,4,7⟩ 46 to 45, ⟨2,4,8⟩ 52 to 51, ⟨2,5,6⟩ 48 to 47, ⟨3,4,6⟩ 56 to 54, ⟨3,4,7⟩ 66 to 63, ⟨3,4,8⟩ 75 to 74, ⟨3,5,6⟩ 70 to 68, ⟨3,5,7⟩ 82 to 80, ⟨4,4,4⟩ 49 to 48, ⟨4,4,5⟩ 62 to 61, ⟨4,4,7⟩ 87 to 85, ⟨4,4,8⟩ 98 to 96 and ⟨4,5,6⟩ 93 to 90; concurrent work also found rank 90 for ⟨4,5,6⟩. The shortfalls are ⟨4,4,9⟩ (108 against 104) and ⟨6,6,6⟩ (156 against 153). ⟨3,3,3⟩ stays at 23 and ⟨5,5,5⟩ at 93. The ⟨3,4,7⟩, ⟨4,4,4⟩ and ⟨4,4,8⟩ algorithms use complex-valued multiplications, which the authors say can be used for exact multiplication of complex or real matrices. The authors frame the 4 by 4 result as follows. Recursive Strassen gives rank 49 over any field. AlphaTensor (Fawzi et al.) found rank 47 only over the field with two elements. Rank below 49 in characteristic 0 had been open for 56 years. Algorithms with fewer than 49 multiplications that are not tensor decompositions exist but cannot be applied recursively (footnote 3). Most results, including ⟨4,4,4⟩, came from a simple initial program; for some sizes, seeding the initial program with the authors' own ideas (stochastic evaluation, evolutionary approaches) helped further, and the text does not say which sizes. Beyond ⟨5,5,5⟩, running the discovered programs on 1000 seeds on single-GPU evaluators often ran out of memory.

**Open mathematical problems (Section 3.2, Appendix B).** The authors applied AlphaEvolve to "a curated set of over 50 mathematical problems" in analysis, combinatorics, number theory and geometry, "evaluated across numerous specific parameter settings". In 75% of "the cases" it rediscovered the best known construction and in 20% it improved on it, always from a simple or random starting construction. The main method for these problems was iterative refinement. Each generation evolves a search heuristic that gets a fixed time budget (for example 1000 seconds) and the best construction found so far, and must improve it. Final constructions often came from a sequence of heuristics, with early ones making large gains and later ones fine-tuning, which the authors say "proved crucial for surpassing the SOTA". Most problems were suggested by Javier Gomez Serrano and Terence Tao, who also advised on formulation (Jordan Ellenberg is also acknowledged). Appendix B details only the improved results; the canonical extract lost the display equations there, and prior bounds below are from the raw dump:

For C4, the AlphaEvolve construction refines constants in a Hermite-polynomial test function of the form used by Gonçalves et al. [33]. The authors state that it "empirically confirm[s]" that construction is nearly optimal. An added note says that after the first version of the manuscript, Henry Cohn pointed out an earlier paper with a better constant, 0.3284. Incorporating that refined approach, AlphaEvolve then reached 0.3216. The kissing configuration consists of 593 nonzero integer points in 11 dimensions whose maximum norm is below their minimum pairwise distance; Lemma 1 turns this into a valid kissing configuration. Many packing baselines come from one online compendium (Friedman's Packing Center, accessed 2025-04-22). The data and verification code for all constructions are in the Colab.

**Google infrastructure (Section 3.3).** Data-center scheduling: an early version of AlphaEvolve evolved Borg's machine-scoring heuristic, starting from the production heuristic. The task was framed as two-dimensional (CPU, memory) vector bin packing. The heuristic only ranks machines Borg already deems feasible, so decisions are correct by construction. Feedback came from a simulator on historical fleet snapshots, and the heuristic was tested on an unseen set of recent workloads. After fleet-wide rollout, the discovered heuristic (a short formula in the CPU and memory residual ratios) "continuously recovers on average 0.7%" of fleet compute that would otherwise be stranded. The authors say it was chosen over a deep RL approach for performance and interpretability; no numbers are given for that comparison. Gemini kernel engineering: AlphaEvolve evolved the tiling heuristic of an important Pallas matrix-multiplication kernel, scored on real TPU runtime, with input shapes collected from kernel users split half for training and half for evaluation. Correctness holds by construction because only tiling changes. The result is an average 23% kernel speedup "across all kernels" over the expert heuristic and a 1% reduction in Gemini's overall training time, with optimization time cut from months of engineering to days. TPU circuit: in an already highly optimized Verilog arithmetic circuit in the matrix-multiplication unit, AlphaEvolve found a simple rewrite removing unnecessary bits. TPU designers validated it and it is integrated into an upcoming TPU, but the authors note that downstream synthesis tools caught the same improvement independently. Compiler IR: AlphaEvolve directly edited XLA-generated intermediate representations of a FlashAttention kernel plus pre- and post-processing for one high-impact GPU inference configuration. Each edit was checked against the reference on randomized inputs, and human experts confirmed the final code correct for all inputs. The kernel sped up by 32% and pre/post-processing by 15%.

**Ablations (Section 4, Figure 8).** Two tasks were ablated: tensor decomposition and the kissing number. The variants are "No evolution" (repeatedly feeding the same initial program), "No context in the prompt", "No meta prompt evolution", "No full-file evolution" (only the loss function evolved) and "Small base LLM only" (a single unnamed small model). The last three were run on tensor decomposition only. Curves are averaged over targets, with shading for intra-target standard deviation over three seeds. The text states only that "each of the components is responsible for a significant improvement"; the curves themselves are in a figure that could not be assessed from the extract.

**Discussion and caveats stated by the authors.** They describe AlphaEvolve as a test-time compute agent that substantially enhances the base LLM compared with, for example, repeated sampling. They propose distilling AlphaEvolve-augmented performance into future base models. They note that self-improvement gains so far are moderate, with feedback loops on the order of months. The main limitation they give is that problems need an automated evaluator. Natural sciences where only some experiments can be simulated are largely out of scope, and LLM-provided evaluation is supported but not optimized. They suggest linking to AI co-scientist-style natural-language evaluation for high-level ideas before a code stage with machine feedback. More details on the mathematical problems are deferred to an "upcoming paper".

## Learnings

When a cheap, exact evaluator exists, a generate-and-filter loop with non-reasoning proposers (Gemini 2.0 Flash plus Pro) was enough to set new records on long-studied problems. Wrong proposals cost only evaluation time, so the LLM does not need to be reliable, only occasionally right. In every setting shown the evaluator was programmatic, and correctness was secured by construction, exact checks, or human confirmation, so the paper says nothing about how the loop behaves with a noisy or learned evaluator.

Continuous optimization can be turned into a source of exact algorithms. The evaluator rounded tensor factors to integers or half-integers before scoring, and the prompt asked for near-integral solutions, so every one of the 54 reported decompositions is exact. The same device could transfer to physics problems where exact rational or integer structure is expected. One cost is plausible but untested: the rounding lattice excludes irrational optima. The one clear shortfall, ⟨4,4,9⟩ at 108 against 104, is against a reference whose title calls its solution possibly "irreducibly irrational".

For construction problems, the evolved object was usually a time-budgeted search heuristic seeded with the current best construction, not the construction itself. Successive heuristics specialized from coarse to fine. The authors hypothesize that constructor functions suit symmetric solutions and search programs suit non-symmetric ones. That makes the choice of representation a tunable part of the method, although the paper runs no ablation on it.

"Beats the state of the art" in automated discovery depends on how complete the literature search is. The uncertainty-inequality result was presented as an improvement until an outside expert pointed to an earlier, much better bound. Automated-discovery evaluations need an expert audit of the baseline, not just of the new object.

## Verification

Verification is central by design. Every candidate is executed and scored by user-written code, and the authors say this lets AlphaEvolve "avoid any incorrect suggestions from the base LLM". Selection during evolution always comes from a program, except for optional LLM-graded soft properties such as simplicity; final acceptance in the hardware and compiler cases also involved human experts. Those can be added to the scores or used to discard candidates, and the authors say this mode is not optimized.

The checks differ by application. For tensor decompositions, entries are rounded to integers or half-integers at evaluation, so exactness is enforced, and the score also reports the fraction of seeds reaching the best rank. For the kissing number, the construction uses integer coordinates plus a short proved lemma (maximum norm below minimum pairwise distance), so the bound is a checkable certificate. For the other mathematical bounds, the constructions and "verification code" are released in the Colab, but the text does not say whether that code uses exact or floating-point arithmetic, or with what tolerance. For scheduling and kernel tiling, correctness holds by construction: the heuristic only reorders feasible machines, or only changes tiling. Generalization was checked on held-out workloads or input shapes, and for scheduling by post-deployment fleet measurement. The TPU circuit change had to pass unspecified "robust verification methods" and was validated by TPU designers. For the compiler IR, each candidate was compared with the reference on randomized inputs, which the authors did not treat as sufficient: human experts confirmed correctness for all inputs. The evaluation cascade prunes weak or faulty programs early on small test cases.

The paper does not discuss whether evolved programs game the evaluator. It does not report how often candidates fail evaluation, or the false-pass risk of randomized equivalence testing. It explicitly scopes out tasks whose verdict needs a manual experiment.

## Field context

Authors' positioning: AlphaEvolve extends genetic programming, replacing hand-written mutation operators with LLM-proposed edits. It extends code superoptimization, which goes back to the 1980s (enumeration, stochastic search, deep RL). Most directly, it extends the authors' own FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., 2023), relative to which it evolves whole multi-language codebases with multiple objectives and frontier models (Table 1). For matrix multiplication the direct predecessor is AlphaTensor (Fawzi et al., 2022), a specialized deep-RL system; AlphaEvolve is general-purpose and surpasses it on several sizes. The authors separate themselves from LLM-plus-evolution work on robot policies, symbolic regression, combinatorial heuristics, prompts and architectures, by scale and generality. They contrast themselves with the AI co-scientist, which represents hypotheses in natural language judged by LLMs, by using code and programmatic evaluators, which they say sidesteps hallucination. They name RL fine-tuning during evolution (Surina et al.) and concept-library summarization (Grayeli et al.) as complementary ideas untested at their scale.

Reviewer inference from the corpus: at arXiv v1 in June 2025, this is one of the earliest reviewed instances of evaluator-gated code evolution with frontier LLMs. The Darwin Gödel Machine ([reviews/2505.22954.md](2505.22954.md), May 2025) is contemporaneous and applies a similar archive-plus-evaluator loop to the agent's own code, with benchmark scores as the evaluator. Later reviewed work treats AlphaEvolve as the reference point. ERA ([reviews/26empsw.md](26empsw.md)) describes it as similar in algorithmic spirit but lacking literature-grounded idea exploration. Meta-Harness ([reviews/2603.28052.md](2603.28052.md)) contrasts its own filesystem access to full execution traces with AlphaEvolve's database of programs with scalar scores. The AI co-mathematician ([reviews/2605.06651.md](2605.06651.md)) names AlphaEvolve as an engine it does not yet integrate. [reviews/2607.27191.md](2607.27191.md) classes it among demonstrations on automatically verifiable tasks. Two reviewed papers push against its core design choice. [reviews/26abduct.md](26abduct.md) argues that AlphaEvolve optimizes against a fixed objective and so does not reach abductive invention, which agrees with the authors' own stated limitation rather than refuting a claim they make. The Red Queen Gödel Machine ([reviews/2606.26294.md](2606.26294.md)) reports gains from co-evolving a learned evaluator instead of keeping it fixed; this challenges the assumption that a fixed user-written evaluator is the right interface, but in a different domain.

## Critical discussion

The strongest part of the paper is that its headline scientific results do not require trusting the pipeline. The 14 new tensor ranks are exact integer or half-integer decompositions, and the 11-dimensional kissing configuration comes with integer coordinates and a proved lemma. The other bounds are explicit constructions released with verification code. Anyone can check these objects independently of AlphaEvolve, which makes them far stronger evidence than benchmark scores. The deployed engineering results are backed by post-deployment measurement and held-out splits, not only simulation. Whatever one thinks of the method claims, these records stand as reported, subject to how the floating-point constructions were checked, which the text does not describe.

The claims about why the method works are much weaker than the claims about what it found. There is no compute-matched control without an LLM. The evolved tensor program in Figures 9a to 9c is a gradient optimizer with recognizable numerical tricks: noise injection, annealed clipping, random re-initialization of entries, a scheduled discretization penalty, and a broad hyperparameter sweep. The paper does not test whether a human-tuned version of the same pipeline, or random search over such tricks, reaches the same ranks with the same compute. The "No evolution" ablation is a useful repeated-sampling control, but it still uses the LLM. For the mathematical bounds, many margins are in the fourth to sixth significant digit (C5 0.380927 to 0.380924; circle packings 2.634 to 2.635 and 2.936 to 2.937; Heilbronn 0.0277 to 0.0278). They were obtained by 1000-second heuristics restarted from the previous best. That is a regime where a conventional local optimizer given comparable compute might also gain digits, and the paper does not report that comparison. It also does not report the compute used for any result, only that "thousands" of samples suffice and evaluations can take about 100 compute-hours. So the paper cannot support a claim that LLM-guided evolution is more efficient than alternatives. It supports the claim that it works.

Reporting on the mathematical problem set is incomplete. The text says "over 50 problems ... evaluated across numerous specific parameter settings", with 75% and 20% of "the cases". It never states the denominator or whether it counts problems or parameter settings. It says twice that the full list of problems is in Appendix B, but Appendix B describes only the improvements (by my count, 17 instances in 13 problem families). The matched and failed cases, the number of runs per problem, how the problems were selected, and whether any were dropped are not reported, and details are deferred to an upcoming paper. The C4 note shows why this matters. The text reports 0.3521 as an improvement on 0.3523, and the added note concedes that an earlier paper already had 0.3284, which an outside expert pointed out after the first version was published. The baseline for "surpasses SOTA" is only as good as the authors' literature search, and several baselines come from a single online packing compendium. For the 75% "rediscovered" cases, the text does not separate rediscovery by search from recall of published constructions by the Gemini proposers. This matters only for how that percentage is read, not for the new records.

Several mechanism statements go beyond the evidence shown. The claim that AlphaEvolve "performs increasingly better as the underlying LLM improves" (Section 2.3) rests on a single comparison: an unnamed small model alone against the Flash plus Pro mixture, on one task. That is not a trend across model quality. The claim that multi-metric optimization "often improves results for the single target metric" comes with no data. The claim that evolving search heuristics instead of constructions "proved crucial for surpassing the SOTA" has no ablation against evolving constructions directly. The database design (MAP-Elites plus islands), the evaluation cascade and the Flash/Pro split are never ablated. The ablation conclusion that "each of the components is responsible for a significant improvement" is supported in the text only by a figure that cannot be assessed here. That figure uses three seeds, covers two tasks, and runs three of its five variants only on tensor decomposition. Finally, the statement that execution-based evaluation lets the system "avoid any incorrect suggestions" holds only to the extent that the evaluator captures correctness. The compiler case shows the authors themselves did not trust randomized testing without human confirmation. Evaluator gaming is not discussed, even though a contemporaneous evaluator-gated loop in the corpus documented objective hacking ([reviews/2505.22954.md](2505.22954.md)).

The engineering results are credible but internal and not reproducible from outside. Each compares against the incumbent: the production heuristic, the expert tiling heuristic, or the compiler output. That is the right comparison for the practical claim, but not for the method claim. Kernel tiling was not compared with the search-based autotuning the authors describe as the usual alternative. The deep-RL comparison for scheduling is asserted without numbers. The results are single averages without spread, and the text does not say whether the 23% kernel speedup is measured on the held-out shapes only or on all shapes. The TPU change was also found independently by downstream synthesis tools, which the authors acknowledge, so its value lies in finding the change earlier in the design flow. "Gemini optimizes its own training" amounts to a 1% reduction in training time.

The Strassen framing is mostly well scoped. The abstract says "complex-valued matrices", and footnote 3 excludes non-recursive schemes. However, the rank-48 algorithm counts complex scalar multiplications. The caption says it also applies to real matrices, but the text does not discuss what complex arithmetic costs on real inputs. The phrase that rank below 49 "over any field with characteristic 0" had been open could be read as covering fields the complex coefficients do not live in.

What remains supported: an LLM-proposed, evaluator-gated evolutionary loop produced a set of new, independently checkable mathematical and algorithmic records, and deployable improvements to production systems, from simple starting points. The evaluator is the essential ingredient and the only guarantee of correctness. The attribution of those gains to specific components (frontier models, meta-prompting, multi-metric diversity, heuristic evolution), and any claim of efficiency over non-LLM search, remain reported rather than established.

## Relevance to us

This is the clearest large-scale evidence for the view, set out in our context notes, that cheap verifiers change the architecture. With an exact scorer, proposals can be cheap and often wrong, and the system still sets records. The authors draw the other side of that line themselves: tasks without an automated evaluator, including most natural science, are out of scope. For Theo this points the investment at the evaluator rather than the proposer. The practical question for B12 and B10 is which theoretical-physics tasks sit in the narrow band where a scorer can be written. Our own inference is that construction problems whose score is a rigorous bound would fit: variational upper bounds on ground-state energies, explicit counterexamples, and circuit or tensor-network decompositions (the paper cites AlphaTensor applied to quantum circuit optimization). The many-body and claims-about-nature questions we care most about would not.

The paper also sharpens B01. An AlphaEvolve-style loop is the strong simple baseline for any Theo component that targets a scored problem. A fair comparison needs the control this paper omits, a compute-matched non-LLM optimizer, alongside the repeated-sampling control it includes. The rounding-to-a-lattice device is a candidate mechanism for exactness in our own solver-in-the-loop work, with the caveat that it presupposes the answer's number field. The authors' remark that the level of abstraction (constructor versus search program) matters by problem fits our view that choosing a representation is a first-class design decision. Their proposal to distill AlphaEvolve outputs into base models is relevant to B21, with the usual ceiling: the evaluator defines what counts as good. The paper does not engage with calibration, assumption tracking, or intermediate-step checking. The evaluators here score final objects, which is exactly the regime where process errors do not matter.
