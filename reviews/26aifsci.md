Citation: Cranmer, K., Lawrence, N. D., Montgomery, J., and Thérien, D. AI for Science: Reframing AI's Role in Discovery. Data Science and Artificial Intelligence, Royal Statistical Society, 2026.

## What this paper is about

Amid growing excitement about AI accelerating scientific discovery, this paper, written to open a special issue of a new Royal Statistical Society journal, argues that the hype risks obscuring harder questions about what is actually changing. Is AI simply the next step in a decades-long shift toward more computational science, or does it represent something qualitatively different, potentially producing scientific knowledge that resists human interpretation? What specific technical capabilities, causal reasoning, cross-scale abstraction, and mechanistic simulation, are still missing for AI to move beyond statistical pattern-matching? And what institutional changes, in governance, research culture, infrastructure, and public discourse, are needed for AI's growing role in science to be trustworthy rather than merely prolific-looking? The paper does not claim to answer these questions; its purpose is to frame them clearly enough for a research community to take up.

## Extended summary

The paper opens by applying Kuhn's distinction between "normal science" (solving puzzles within an existing paradigm) and paradigm shifts to the question of whether AI-enabled science is a continuation of the long trend from analytic to numerical and non-parametric computational methods, or a more fundamental shift. It contrasts Gauss and Laplace's celestial-mechanics work, a sophisticated puzzle still solved within Newtonian mechanics, with Einstein's relativity, which changed the rules of the puzzle itself, and raises a reported case (attributed to OpenAI, 2026) of a general-purpose reasoning model disproving a longstanding discrete-geometry conjecture as a possible early instance of AI-generated mathematical knowledge, while noting a gap between adding a result to mathematical knowledge and a Grothendieck-style reframing of how a field's practitioners think about their subject. It flags several features that may distinguish this AI-driven shift from earlier computational science: many AI methods generate predictions without an underlying mechanistic model, the compute infrastructure needed to train frontier systems sits mostly outside public scientific institutions, and AI is increasingly positioned as a general-purpose, autonomous-agent technology rather than a specialized calculational tool.

Section 2.2 weighs this potential paradigm shift against a documented "productivity paradox": research output volume is reported to double roughly every 15 years, while measured scientific progress or discovery appears to be slowing despite rising investment. The paper argues AI risks intensifying this gap, since tools that help generate plausibly publishable manuscripts can create an appearance of productivity without new insight, citing the proliferation of variable-quality AI-generated papers and a nascent "Parallel Science" project proposing that AI-generated research be published and evaluated in a separate space. It introduces the term "agentic debt" for a specific risk that grows as AI systems move from supporting individual research tasks to coordinating whole workflows: the risk of delegating scientific work without clear boundaries around who can take which action, on what evidence, and with what route to recovery or reconstruction of results.

Section 3 proposes a three-dimensional framework, task capabilities, scientific workflow needs, and domain constraints, intended to help match specific AI tools to specific scientific needs rather than treating "AI for science" as one undifferentiated capability. It illustrates the framework with examples: a cross-domain foundation model such as a general-purpose LLM differs in kind from a domain-specific model combining diverse data types for one field (such as multispectral data for galaxy studies); AI now spans the full workflow from hypothesis generation through experimental design to data analysis, with agentic systems increasingly chaining these stages together (planning, executing, interpreting results, drafting papers); and domain constraints vary sharply even within one field, contrasting real-time, MHz-rate particle-accelerator triggering systems against offline analysis, and exploratory research applications against clinical decision-making, which faces stricter validation, safety, and bias requirements.

Section 4 develops the paper's central conceptual concern, framed as a "science gap" between statistical pattern-matching and causal, mechanistic scientific reasoning. Much current AI-for-science practice is described as a "guess-and-verify" loop, where AI proposes candidates from statistical patterns that must then be experimentally validated, contrasted with traditional scientific hypotheses that are typically grounded in an interpretable mechanistic model from the outset. The paper cites the "stochastic parrots" critique of LLMs as one articulation of the resulting worry, an illusion of understanding without insight, and discusses hybrid and agentic approaches, using generative AI as an interface to classical tools such as simulators or databases within an established conceptual framework, as a partial mitigation that could equally widen the gap between operational success and human-understandable insight if it produces results that work without being mechanistically interpretable. It names three specific technical priorities for closing this gap: causality (distinguishing spurious correlation from causal structure), abstraction (discovering intermediate scales linking different levels of explanation, illustrated by entropy's history from an observed macroscopic phenomenon to its later statistical-mechanical derivation), and simulation (a spectrum from purely mechanistic to purely data-driven models, each with characteristic failure modes, that hybrid approaches aim to bridge). It explicitly pushes back against reading Sutton's "Bitter Lesson" as an argument against integrating domain knowledge into scientific AI specifically, noting that AI for science remains comparatively data-limited outside a handful of domains, such as weather forecasting and protein structure, that benefit from decades of carefully curated data.

Section 4.3 introduces a "repository versus interface" metaphor for how an AI system relates to scientific knowledge: as a repository, knowledge is encoded in model parameters, either as a relatively static snapshot requiring retraining to update, or as a flexible function fitted to whatever data it is given; as an interface, the system instead provides a conduit to external sources such as databases, simulation tools, or the literature, which may themselves be settled and curated or live and contested. Each mode carries different failure modes (a repository model can calcify outdated knowledge or absorb any pattern without a signal for when something is scientifically anomalous) and requires different validation (of what has been learned, versus of how knowledge is retrieved and applied).

Section 5 argues that science has historically carried a human orientation toward comprehension and meaning-making, and distinguishes four kinds of understanding at stake: operational (can a system be used safely and reliably), mechanistic (is there an interpretable causal account of why it works), paradigm (can a community reproduce or contest the result), and social (are the ideas legible to the wider community). AI can deliver some of these, such as strong operational understanding from an accurate predictor, while displacing others, a decoupling that did not previously occur when a single human's move from observation to generalization produced discovery and understanding in the same act. The section presents two contrasting stances, AI as a "Socratic guide" that enhances human reasoning versus autonomous systems whose outputs need not be human-interpretable to count as progress, and cites concrete test cases on both sides: mathematicians using AlphaEvolve to generate candidate constructions that they then interrogate and develop, alongside early evidence that routine LLM use in writing and knowledge work may be associated with diminished critical thinking among frequent users.

Section 6 organizes institutional concerns into five named gaps: governance (data bias, consent, and privacy in training; opacity undermining transparency and reproducibility; "careless speech" from generative systems requiring expert validation; and AI's potential to help by flagging code errors or statistical inconsistencies in the literature); research culture (institutions reward disciplinary depth over interdisciplinary AI-science work, with thin career pathways, inadequate AI training for domain researchers, and funding poorly suited to team-science roles such as research software engineers); infrastructure (compute and model access concentrated in a small number of private technology companies, contrasted with historically publicly funded and openly accessible big-science infrastructure such as the Human Genome Project and the Large Hadron Collider, and the risk of science becoming a commercial test-bed for a company's AI capabilities); discourse (well-resourced corporate communications shaping public and policy perception of "AI for science" disproportionately relative to the broader, often publicly funded research base that enabled headline results, illustrated by AlphaFold's dependence on the CASP benchmarking and data infrastructure built since 1994, and by an AI-flavored version of the "lone genius" narrative that obscures large contributing communities such as the Protein Data Bank's thousands of contributors); and policy and coordination (traditional big-science funding models, built around single targeted domains, do not map well onto AI's cross-cutting, dual top-down infrastructure and bottom-up researcher-agility needs).

The paper closes by revisiting the same authors' 2023 research agenda for AI in science, noting that its core technical challenges, grounding AI in scientific understanding, building hybrid simulation, and bridging pattern-matching to causal reasoning, remain mostly unresolved despite rapid capability growth in LLMs, foundation models, and agentic systems, while concerns about reproducibility, compute concentration, and environmental cost have intensified. It ends with an explicit call for technical, methodological, empirical, institutional, and philosophical contributions to the special issue it introduces.

## Learnings

The paper's "repository versus interface" distinction, whether an AI system encodes scientific knowledge in its own parameters or instead provides a conduit to external sources, is a genuinely reusable design axis: the two modes have different failure signatures (a repository can silently calcify outdated knowledge or fit any pattern with no signal for a scientifically meaningful anomaly, while an interface's reliability depends on the currency and curation of what it connects to) and require different validation strategies (checking what was learned, versus checking how knowledge is retrieved and applied), making it a useful question to ask explicitly of any new scientific-AI component: which of the two is this, and is it validated accordingly.

The paper's four-way decomposition of scientific "understanding," operational (safe and reliable use), mechanistic (an interpretable causal account), paradigm (can the community reproduce or contest the result), and social (are the ideas legible to others), gives a concrete way to state precisely what an AI system does and does not deliver, since a system can score well on operational understanding while displacing the other three, a decoupling that historically did not occur when the same human act of generalizing from observations produced discovery and understanding together.

The paper's "agentic debt" concept names a distinct risk that grows specifically as AI moves from supporting a single research step to coordinating a full workflow: the risk that no one has cleanly defined which actions the agent may take, on what evidence, and how a mistaken multi-step trajectory could be recovered from or reconstructed. This is a different concern from ordinary output correctness or reproducibility, and it is worth tracking separately whenever a system's role expands from single-step assistance to multi-step orchestration.

## Verification

Verification appears throughout this paper as a recurring conceptual thread rather than as a single proposed method, consistent with the paper's role as an agenda-setting rather than empirical piece. It frames much current AI-for-science practice as a "guess-and-verify" loop, where statistically generated candidates require downstream experimental validation, and explicitly contrasts this with traditional theory-driven hypotheses that are typically already grounded in an interpretable mechanism. It argues that validation requirements are not uniform: real-time, high-throughput systems (such as MHz-rate particle-accelerator triggering) face different validation challenges than offline analysis, and clinical decision-making applications face materially stricter safety, interpretability, and bias requirements than exploratory research tools. Its "repository versus interface" distinction is itself a verification-relevant framing, since a repository-style system needs its learned content checked, while an interface-style system needs the fidelity of its retrieval and application checked instead, two different verification problems that are easy to conflate if a system's mode is not made explicit. The paper also notes, without developing in detail, that AI could itself contribute to verification and reproducibility in the broader research literature by flagging code errors, statistical inconsistencies, or weak results, an application distinct from the paper's main concern about verifying AI's own scientific outputs.

## Field context

This paper is explicitly an agenda-setting piece opening a special issue, extending the same authors' 2023 AI-for-science research agenda in light of the intervening rise of LLMs, foundation models, and agentic systems, and it draws on an intentionally broad set of adjacent work rather than proposing new results of its own: Kuhn's paradigm framework, the "stochastic parrots" critique of large language models, Pearl's and Schölkopf's causal-reasoning programs, Sutton's "Bitter Lesson," and Saltelli et al.'s prior critique of opaque, creator-outrunning models used in Covid-era policy advice. Within this corpus, it connects most directly to this corpus's review of 25mcpsim, a concrete working instance of exactly the "generative AI as an interface to a classical simulation tool within an established conceptual framework" pattern this paper describes in the abstract, and to 26abduct, whose cognitive-science account of an "abductive gap" in AI-driven discovery echoes, from a different disciplinary angle, this paper's own "science gap" between pattern-matching and mechanistic reasoning. It is best read as a deliberately broad, non-technical framing document meant to organize a field's conversation across a special issue, rather than as a paper that itself resolves any of the technical or institutional questions it raises.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central proposed contribution, the three-dimensional framework of task capabilities, workflow needs, and domain constraints, is presented only through illustrative examples rather than as an operational taxonomy with defined categories or decision criteria, and the paper itself calls it "one attempt" at such a framework, an appropriately modest framing. This means its value lies in being a conversation-organizing heuristic rather than a directly applicable instrument; a reader hoping to classify a specific candidate AI-for-science tool against it would need to supply most of the operationalizing detail themselves.

Some of the paper's stronger empirical-sounding claims rest on a single or a pair of cited sources without discussion of those sources' own limitations. The "productivity paradox" claim, that research output doubles roughly every 15 years while discovery slows, leans on two specific citations whose own methods for measuring "discovery" or "progress" are contestable and are not examined here, and the claim that routine LLM use may erode critical thinking cites two specific studies as "early evidence," appropriately hedged language, but without describing either study's design, sample, or effect size, so a reader cannot independently gauge the strength of this specific claim from the paper alone. Separately, the paper's own worked example of a candidate AI paradigm shift, a general-purpose reasoning model reportedly disproving a longstanding discrete-geometry conjecture, is sourced to the company that built the model making the claim about its own achievement, a pattern the paper itself later criticizes in its discourse-gap discussion (Section 6.4) when describing how well-resourced corporate narratives can distort perceptions of AI-for-science progress relative to the broader, often publicly funded work that enables them; the paper does not flag this tension in its own use of that example, even though its later argument would seem to apply to it directly.

A genuine strength worth crediting is the paper's specificity when it does make a checkable institutional claim: it names compute and model concentration in "a handful of private technology companies" as a structural threat to open scientific inquiry, and it grounds this concretely by noting that a headline success like AlphaFold rested on decades of publicly funded, openly shared benchmarking infrastructure, the CASP competition and its associated data, dating to 1994, that receives comparatively little public credit or funding attention relative to the corporate result it enabled. This is a specific, well-evidenced claim rather than a generic institutional-critique platitude, and it is one of the paper's clearest self-consistent applications of its own political-economy argument.

What remains supported: as an agenda-setting and framework-proposing piece, the paper's value is in the specificity and reusability of several of its conceptual distinctions (repository versus interface, the four kinds of understanding, agentic debt) and in its well-evidenced point about compute concentration and the under-recognition of public research infrastructure, rather than in any claim that would need independent empirical verification; its central technical and institutional questions are consistently and appropriately framed as open rather than settled, matching the stated purpose of a call-for-papers piece.

## Relevance to us

Several of this paper's conceptual distinctions are directly usable design questions for our own work, even though the paper itself makes no physics-specific claims. The repository-versus-interface distinction maps onto a real choice we face for any physics-knowledge component: a system meant to reason over the live literature (B08) or maintain an assumption ledger (B11) is an interface and should be validated on how it retrieves and applies knowledge, while a system meant to encode stable physical law is closer to a repository and should instead be checked for calcification against new results; the paper's point that these two modes need different validation strategies is a useful reminder to make that choice explicit rather than leaving it implicit in a component's design. Its warning that hybrid systems using generative AI as an interface to classical simulation tools can produce results that are "operationally successful yet resistant to mechanistic interpretation" is directly illustrated by this corpus's own review of 25mcpsim, where a numerically converged simulation was shown not to guarantee that the inferred physical model was the one the user actually intended, a concrete instance of exactly the gap this paper describes in the abstract. The "agentic debt" concept is a useful frame for our own long-lived, multi-day research-runtime ambitions (Arc 4, B09): as a system moves from supporting a single derivation step to orchestrating a longer trajectory, this paper's framing suggests tracking not only whether each individual step is correct, but who or what can take which action, on what evidence, and how a mistaken multi-step trajectory could be reconstructed or rolled back, a distinct requirement from step-level verification alone. Finally, the paper's honest acknowledgment that grounding AI in causality, cross-scale abstraction, and hybrid simulation, the same technical priorities our own verification and world-model bets target, "remain mostly unresolved" three years after the authors' own prior agenda paper is a useful, independently sourced confirmation that these are still genuinely open problems rather than solved ones, consistent with rather than a new addition to our own read of the field. Its institutional concerns about compute and model concentration are less directly actionable for a company our size, but the underlying structural point, that a small number of institutions increasingly shape research directions through control of compute and models, is a relevant feature of the competitive landscape we already frame ourselves against, worth keeping in view rather than treating as a design lesson.
