Citation: Han, J., Tong, S., Fan, D., Chen, M., Torr, P., Kokkinos, F., Lewis, M. Towards Physics of Multimodal Pretraining: Knowledge Flow, Modality Synergy, Early Unification, and Recipes. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05000v2

## What this paper is about

Unified multimodal models, which train language understanding, visual understanding, and visual generation together in one model from the start, are becoming the new frontier-model paradigm, but most existing systems are built by heuristics and intuition rather than a systematic understanding of how the three capabilities actually interact during training. This Meta FAIR paper runs a large, carefully controlled experimental program, using both real-world data and a fully synthetic testbed that allows genuinely causal claims, to answer four practical design questions: which capabilities transfer knowledge to which others (and in which direction), when do two modalities help versus hurt each other, does it matter when vision is introduced during training, and what data and architecture recipe actually gets the most out of a fixed compute budget.

## Extended summary

All controlled experiments use a Llama-3-style decoder-only Transformer combined with the Transfusion framework, which unifies discrete next-token prediction for text with continuous flow-matching for images in a single model, with a default 2.3B-parameter (1.5B core) backbone using modality-specific split feed-forward networks. Four visual tokenization schemes are tested for generality (a frozen semantic autoencoder called RAE, raw pixel patches with no pretrained visual prior, a decoupled CLIP-encoder-plus-VAE-decoder setup, and an autoregressive discrete-code scheme called UniTok), with language data from DCLM and image-text data from a large Shutterstock corpus. Evaluation spans standard language benchmarks (11 tasks plus perplexity), 16 visual-understanding benchmarks grouped into four axes (general perception, world-knowledge reasoning, OCR/chart reading, and vision-centric spatial tasks), and visual-generation metrics (GenEval, DPG-Bench, CLIP text-image alignment, and held-out diffusion loss).

Knowledge flow (Finding 1) is established first on real, web-scale data through controlled mixture sweeps that add one capability's data on top of a fixed allocation of another while holding everything else constant. The pattern is starkly asymmetric: adding language data monotonically improves every visual-understanding axis and every generation metric, adding visual-understanding data strongly improves generation (while very mildly hurting pure language, attributed mostly to vision-language captions having a different text distribution than pure web text) but adding visual-generation data produces no significant transfer to either language or understanding in either direction, neither helping nor much hurting. Because real data conflates data volume, distributional shift, and genuine capability transfer, the paper then builds a fully controlled synthetic testbed on procedurally-generated CLEVR scenes (Finding 2), where a specific visual concept (a color, shape, spatial relation, size, or count) can be surgically removed from exactly one modality's training stream while a matched control removes it from both, isolating genuine cross-modal transfer from confounds like dataset size. This reveals concept-dependent transfer: low-level perceptual attributes (color, shape) fail to transfer zero-shot in either direction at all, collapsing to the same performance as never having seen the concept; but higher-level structural concepts (spatial relations, size, count) transfer asymmetrically, with understanding-only exposure giving the model real zero-shot generation ability for the concept, while generation-only exposure gives almost no zero-shot understanding benefit. A follow-up fine-tuning experiment on the same held-out concepts uncovers a further asymmetry in latent (non-zero-shot) priors: models that only ever saw a color or shape through generation recover VQA performance on that concept dramatically faster once explicitly fine-tuned on it, while models that only saw it through understanding show little to no such acceleration for generation, which the authors interpret as generative training forcing the model to learn dense, pixel-level representations that remain latently useful even though they aren't zero-shot accessible, while understanding-derived representations are too abstract and sparse to directly drive pixel-level synthesis.

Modality synergy versus competition (Finding 3) is dissected along data complexity, architecture, and tokenizer choice. Holding one modality's data fixed while sweeping the other's task complexity from trivial synthetic patterns up to real natural-image or natural-language distributions shows a genuine crossover: pairing a modality with an extremely simple counterpart task (solid-color backgrounds, or single-repeated-letter "simple" text) actually improves that modality's own performance beyond even its unimodal baseline, but as the counterpart's complexity rises toward real-world distributions, this synergy is progressively overtaken by capacity competition, ultimately degrading performance relative to the unimodal baseline. Dissecting the Transformer block itself (holding data fixed at a synergy-maximizing simple/simple pairing) finds that fully shared parameters ("dense") suffer the worst competition, that decoupling only the feed-forward networks while keeping attention and normalization shared captures essentially all of the available synergy while eliminating the competition, and that decoupling attention or normalization instead sharply reduces the achieved synergy, with fully separating everything reducing the model to two independent unimodal networks; the authors interpret shared attention/normalization as the channel through which cross-modal context and feature-scale alignment happen, with modality-specific FFNs acting as isolated capacity that absorbs each modality's distinct computational demands. This synergy pattern, and the earlier real-world asymmetric-transfer pattern, both replicate across all four tested vision tokenizer designs, including raw, unaligned pixel patches with no pretrained semantic prior, arguing that a pre-aligned shared representation space is not strictly required for synergy to emerge, though it remains practically beneficial for reducing representational friction.

The necessity of early unification (Finding 4) is tested by varying how many tokens of pure-language pretraining precede a fixed-size unified training phase, and separately by forcing strict sequential single-modality training stages (testing all six possible language/understanding/generation orderings, with and without a replay buffer) against a fully joint baseline. Delaying vision's introduction produces only modest, quickly-plateauing language gains but a steep, monotonic decline across every visual-understanding and visual-generation metric; and no sequential ordering, including a human-inspired vision-first curriculum, comes close to matching joint training on visual tasks even with replay-based forgetting mitigation, indicating the modalities need to actively co-evolve rather than simply avoid catastrophic forgetting of each other. A mechanistic investigation (Finding 5, "vision laziness") explains why: across four independent, orthogonal measurements (the activation magnitude of vision-specific feed-forward layers during training, the embedding norm of special image-boundary tokens, the same feed-forward activations at inference time, and the fraction of attention actually directed at image tokens during a real forward pass), a longer pure-language warm-up before unification consistently and monotonically produces a "quieter," less-engaged, less-attended-to visual pathway, converging on a single mechanism: a hardened, heavily pretrained language trunk increasingly resists integrating a newly-introduced visual modality and instead "shortcuts" visual tasks using language priors, rather than genuinely committing capacity to processing the image.

The Recipes section (Finding 6) closes the loop by using the knowledge-flow asymmetry (language and understanding drive generation; generation gives little back) to justify a highly skewed data mixture rather than an even split. A three-axis grid search at 1T-token scale finds language needs the dominant compute share, understanding benefits from roughly a quarter of the budget, and generation reaches near-peak quality with only about 5% of tokens, once bootstrapped by strong language-and-understanding priors; the resulting L70/U25/G5 recipe is then validated at meaningfully larger scale (13.5B-parameter MoE models, 1.5B active parameters, 256 experts with two fixed modality-specific experts among 16 routed per token, trained on 2T tokens) through three controlled, single-variable comparisons against a common Balanced-Recipe (L50/U25/G25) reference: the asymmetric data mixture beats the balanced one on language and understanding while achieving comparable or better generation quality (including a competitive FID) despite using five times fewer generation tokens; the MoE architecture with shared attention/normalization and modality-specific FFNs beats an equivalent dense model on every axis; and early unification beats an otherwise-identical late-fusion baseline (vision introduced only after 60% of training, with the same total volume of vision tokens packed into the remainder) on language, understanding, and generation alike. All three scaled comparisons point the same direction as the smaller controlled studies that motivated them.

The paper's own stated limitations confine the study to static text and images, explicitly flagging video, audio, and other continuous or temporal modalities as untested extensions where data complexity and required architectural decoupling may differ; and note that while the recipes are validated at a substantial 13.5B/2T-token scale, the specific thresholds at which synergy tips into competition, and the strict asymmetry found for generation's backward transfer, might shift or even reverse at frontier (>1T-parameter) scale, where a sufficiently capacious model might in principle use generative modeling as an internal world-simulator with genuine bidirectional knowledge flow, a possibility the paper labels explicitly as a hypothesis for future work rather than a demonstrated result.

## Learnings

The specific two-stage methodological pattern used throughout, first documenting a correlational pattern on real, web-scale data, then reproducing and sharpening it into a genuinely causal claim using a fully controlled synthetic testbed (CLEVR) where a specific concept can be surgically removed from exactly one modality's training stream with a matched zero-exposure control, is a directly transferable research design for anyone studying cross-capability transfer in any multi-task or multimodal training setup: real-data correlations alone cannot rule out confounds like dataset size or distributional shift, and a synthetic ablation with a matched control is a specific, reusable recipe for closing that gap.

The architectural finding that shared attention and normalization specifically (not the feed-forward network) are the components responsible for cross-modal synergy, while feed-forward decoupling specifically is what isolates capacity and prevents destructive competition, is a concrete, mechanistically-grounded design principle for building any multi-task or multi-modality Transformer, not just a general "add some modality-specific parameters" heuristic: it identifies which specific sub-components should be shared versus separated and offers a tested causal account (contextualization/scale-alignment via attention/norm; capacity isolation via FFN) for why that particular split works, rather than an unexplained empirical recipe.

The "vision laziness" mechanism, that a language trunk pretrained for longer before a new modality is introduced becomes progressively less willing to commit representational capacity and attention to that new modality and instead shortcuts around it using its existing priors, is a transferable diagnostic concept for any setting where a new capability or modality is added to an already-substantially-trained model (not necessarily vision specifically): the four independent, non-overlapping measurement axes used to detect it (training-time activation magnitude, embedding-space accommodation, inference-time activation, and inference-time attention allocation) constitute a reusable toolkit for checking whether a newly-added component is actually being used by a model, versus being nominally present but functionally bypassed.

## Verification

This paper's verification strategy is unusually thorough for a mechanistic/architecture-design study. Its central knowledge-flow claims are triangulated two structurally different ways: real-world mixture sweeps (which are ecologically relevant but confounded) and a fully controlled synthetic concept-ablation study with a matched zero-exposure control (which is less realistic but supports genuinely causal conclusions), and both point to the same qualitative asymmetry. The "vision laziness" mechanism is supported by four independent, methodologically distinct measurements (training-time and inference-time activation magnitudes, embedding-space geometry, and attention allocation) rather than a single proxy, all converging on the same conclusion across two separate task settings (generation and understanding sweeps). Generality claims are explicitly tested rather than assumed: the synergy findings are checked across four structurally different vision tokenizer designs, including one (raw pixels) with no pretrained semantic prior at all, and a specific confound in the unimodal-baseline comparison (whether simply increasing data volume, rather than genuine cross-modal transfer, explains an observed gain) is checked directly via a zero-loss-weight control run. The final scaling section is a genuine attempt to check whether the smaller-scale controlled findings actually hold at larger, more expensive scale (13.5B parameters, 2T tokens) via three separate, single-variable-isolated ablations against a common reference recipe, rather than treating the small-scale findings as automatically scale-invariant.

The main verification gap is that the CLEVR concept-transfer study's generation-side evaluation relies on an automatic VLM judge (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) to score whether a generated image reflects the requested held-out attribute, and the paper does not report a human-validation check of this judge's accuracy on this specific compositional-attribute-scoring task, so some unknown fraction of the fine-grained zero-shot-transfer results could in principle reflect judge noise or systematic bias rather than the underlying generative model's actual grasp of the concept. Additionally, all controlled experiments use a single language corpus (DCLM) and a single image-text corpus (Shutterstock) throughout the main study, so while the paper's "physics" framing implies broadly generalizable principles, the specific numeric thresholds and even some qualitative patterns (e.g., exactly where complexity synergy turns into competition) have not been checked against a genuinely different data distribution for either modality, only against different vision tokenizer/encoder architectures. The large-scale validation runs (13.5B/2T-token scale) also appear to be single training runs per configuration rather than repeated across seeds, which matters most for the architecture ablation, where the reported MoE-versus-dense gap (e.g., 52.86% vs. 52.03% language accuracy) is numerically modest relative to some of the paper's other, larger effects.

## Field context

The paper positions itself explicitly against the two dominant prior paradigms for building multimodal models: late-fusion (aligning independently pretrained visual encoders to a fixed, already-trained language model via adapters), which it argues structurally bottlenecks visual capability by forcing rich visual signal into a pre-existing language space, and the more recent early-fusion wave (cited industry systems described only by name in the paper's own references, without individual technical detail) that lets visual and language representations co-evolve from the start but has so far been guided mostly by heuristics rather than a systematic understanding of the underlying mechanisms. It builds directly on the Transfusion framework (combining discrete next-token text prediction with continuous flow-matching image generation in one model) as its experimental backbone, and positions its architectural findings as empirical support for existing Mixture-of-Transformers- and Mixture-of-Experts-style modality-decoupling designs already proposed in the literature, rather than introducing a wholly new architecture itself. Within this corpus, no directly comparable paper on unified multimodal pretraining dynamics, modality-transfer asymmetry, or early-versus-late vision-language fusion timing is currently present to cross-reference; the closest general methodological kinship in this corpus is with papers that use a fully controlled synthetic testbed specifically to convert a correlational, real-data-confounded finding into a causal claim, a pattern this paper applies to cross-modal knowledge transfer via its CLEVR concept-ablation study.

## Critical discussion

The paper's experimental design is a genuine strength: triangulating real-world correlational evidence against a fully controlled synthetic causal study for its central knowledge-flow claim, backing its "vision laziness" mechanism with four independent and non-overlapping measurements rather than one proxy metric, and validating its small-scale architectural and data-mixture findings at a substantially larger scale (13.5B parameters, 2T tokens) through properly isolated single-variable ablations are all practices that go meaningfully beyond a typical single-scale, single-metric architecture paper.

Two points are worth weighing directly. First, the fine-grained, zero-shot concept-transfer results on CLEVR rest on an automatic vision-language judge (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) scoring whether a generated image correctly reflects a requested compositional attribute, and no human-validation check of this judge's own accuracy on this specific task is reported; given how precise some of the paper's concept-level claims are (for instance, the specific finding that counting shows a minor exception to an otherwise-strict transfer failure), a reader should treat these fine-grained distinctions as somewhat more provisional than the paper's confident framing might suggest, pending an independent check of the judge itself. Second, the study's "physics" framing implies broadly general principles, but every controlled experiment draws from a single language corpus and a single image-text corpus, so the specific quantitative thresholds (exactly how much task complexity is needed before synergy turns to competition, or the exact optimal data-mixing ratios found in the grid search) have been shown to generalize across different vision tokenizer architectures but not yet across genuinely different underlying data distributions for either modality; the paper's own Limitations section is candid about scale-generalization risk but does not separately flag this data-source narrowness.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the qualitative direction of the core asymmetric-knowledge-flow finding (language and understanding as strong forward priors; generation as a weak backward contributor) is corroborated across two structurally different experimental designs and is consistent with, and helps explain, the paper's own scaled-recipe result that generation needs only a small fraction of the compute budget once properly bootstrapped; the FFN-decoupling-plus-shared-attention architectural principle is demonstrated mechanistically (via a systematic ablation of specific Transformer sub-components) rather than merely observed as an aggregate effect; and the early-unification recommendation is backed by both an aggregate performance argument (Section 5.1-5.2) and an independent mechanistic explanation for why it matters (the vision-laziness measurements in Section 5.3), giving the claim two largely independent lines of support rather than one.
