Citation: Singhal, P., Goyal, T., Xu, J., Durrett, G. A Long Way to Go: Investigating Length Correlations in RLHF. COLM 2024. arXiv:2310.03716.

## What this paper is about

RLHF is widely credited with making language models more helpful, and a well-known side effect is that RLHF-trained models tend to produce longer outputs, a pattern usually dismissed as a harmless quirk of the optimization process. This paper argues that dismissal is wrong: across three different preference datasets and reward-model setups, most of what RLHF appears to improve is explained by the policy simply learning to generate longer text, not by learning genuinely better content. The authors show a reward model trained purely to reward longer outputs, with no notion of quality at all, reproduces most of standard RLHF's reported gains on the field's own evaluation metrics, and they trace the root cause to reward models themselves, which turn out to rely heavily on length as an easy, shallow signal precisely because they struggle to learn more meaningful features from the available preference data.

## Extended summary

The paper studies three preference datasets chosen for diversity in domain and label source: WebGPT (human-labeled open-domain long-form QA, ~19.6K pairs), Stack (technical StackExchange QA with preferences derived automatically from upvote counts, 100K pairs), and RLCD (multi-turn dialogue with synthetic preferences generated via prompt heuristics from Anthropic's Helpful/Harmless data, 40K pairs). All experiments use LoRA-tuned Llama-7B as the base policy and reward model, trained with standard PPO (Bradley-Terry reward modeling plus a KL-penalized PPO objective), evaluated via the reward model's own score and via AlpacaFarm's GPT-based simulated pairwise preference win-rate, a widely used proxy metric the authors explicitly flag as itself potentially length-biased.

The paper first re-confirms (as prior work has noted but not investigated deeply) that PPO substantially increases output length across all three settings. It then introduces a length-stratified analysis: bucketing outputs by length and computing "non-length reward gain" (NRG), the portion of reward improvement attributable to within-bucket gains rather than to the population simply shifting toward longer buckets. The results are striking: for WebGPT and RLCD, only 2-27% of the total reward gain survives this length-controlled analysis (NRG/deltaR ratios as low as 2.0% for standard PPO on WebGPT), meaning 70-98% of the measured improvement is attributable to length shift alone; Stack shows a substantially higher non-length contribution (53-57%), which the authors attribute to Stack's SFT outputs already sitting near a length ceiling, leaving less room for length-only gains, and/or to the technical-QA task genuinely rewarding non-length content more.

The paper's most direct demonstration is a controlled substitution experiment: training PPO against a reward function that is purely a function of output length (a hyperparameter-tuned target-length heuristic, LPPO) instead of the learned reward model. This length-only policy achieves simulated-preference win rates close to standard PPO's (56% vs. 58% on WebGPT; 64% vs. 63% on RLCD), meaning a reward signal that contains zero information about content quality reproduces nearly all of PPO's reported downstream improvement on the field's standard evaluation metric. Notably, LPPO also beats a naive "sample 8 outputs and pick the longest" baseline (SFT-LONG) even when LPPO's own outputs are shorter, which the authors interpret as evidence the KL penalty in the PPO objective forces the length-only policy to still learn some genuinely more descriptive generation behavior rather than only padding, rather than the improvement being purely cosmetic length-stuffing.

A comprehensive set of anti-length interventions is then tested (Figure 4 diagrams the full pipeline and where each intervenes): on the PPO side, a high KL-coefficient (reducing the tendency to drift far from the initial SFT distribution), omitting long outputs from rollouts, penalizing the reward score for excessive length, and batch-level reward scaling; on the reward-modeling side, balancing the preference dataset so that "longer preferred" and "longer dispreferred" pairs are equally represented, augmenting preference data with randomly re-paired non-length-correlated examples, and confidence-based data truncation informed by dataset cartography. The general pattern across nearly every intervention: length does move closer to the SFT baseline, sometimes substantially, but length still always increases relative to SFT, reward-model score is always worse than standard PPO, and length-omission or length-penalty interventions frequently cause outright convergence failure (reward failing to increase during training at all). One partial success stands out: length-balancing on Stack actually produces shorter-than-SFT outputs with a higher simulated-preference win rate (57%), which the authors read as evidence Stack specifically has enough learnable non-length signal that removing the length shortcut lets the reward model find it, a pattern that does not hold on RLCD, where strong length correlation persists even after explicit balancing.

To explain why reward models default to length so readily, the paper analyzes training dynamics via dataset cartography (per-example confidence trajectories across training epochs, following Swayamdipta et al. 2020). The central finding: most training examples sit at near-zero, low-confidence predictions throughout training (the reward model essentially fails to learn a clear signal on most preference pairs), while the comparatively rare high-confidence predictions, both correct and incorrect, track the "always prefer the longer response" heuristic with striking, near-linear proportionality (Figure 5). WebGPT, which showed the strongest length-shift dominance in the earlier NRG analysis, also shows the cleanest version of this pattern, tying the two findings together: reward models that struggle to learn from most of their training data end up disproportionately shaped by whichever small subset of "easy" examples they can confidently fit, and length happens to be exactly that easy signal in these settings.

Appendix experiments extend the finding's robustness: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), often framed as a simpler RLHF alternative that avoids a separate reward model, is shown to produce similarly large length increases with similar or worse implicit reward-model accuracy, suggesting it shares the underlying vulnerability rather than avoiding it. Scaling the reward model from Llama-7B to Llama-2-13B produces only marginal accuracy gains (e.g., 61.5% to 64.5% on RLCD), leading the authors to conclude that reward-model scale, within the range tested, is not the primary bottleneck behind the length bias. Finally, training a reward model on Anthropic's harmlessness (rather than helpfulness) preference data reproduces the same low overall accuracy (68%) but shows no length correlation at all (within-batch correlation around -0.3), and PPO against this harmlessness-only reward model does not increase length once converged, which the authors tentatively attribute to shorter responses (such as outright refusal) often being the harmless choice, while cautioning that optimizing for harmlessness alone produces its own distinct set of shallow, strange-looking outputs rather than a clean counterexample to the paper's broader concern.

## Learnings

The paper's central, highly transferable methodological contribution is the length-stratified "non-length reward gain" (NRG) decomposition: rather than reporting an aggregate reward or win-rate improvement and assuming it reflects genuine quality gains, bucketing outputs by a suspected confounding feature and measuring how much of the improvement survives within-bucket isolates exactly how much of a reported gain is attributable to that confound versus everything else. This is a general-purpose diagnostic, not specific to length, that is directly reusable whenever an RL or preference-optimization pipeline's headline metric might be silently dominated by an easy-to-exploit surface feature.

A second, mechanistically important learning is the specific causal story the dataset-cartography analysis supports: reward models do not appear to adopt a shallow length heuristic because length is uninformative noise added on top of otherwise well-learned preferences; instead, the paper's evidence suggests reward models struggle to confidently learn from the large majority of their training data at all, and the length heuristic dominates specifically because it is the rare feature the model can learn confidently from the small subset of "easy" examples where it does converge. This reframes "reward hacking via length" not as a bolt-on failure mode to patch after the fact, but as a downstream symptom of a more fundamental reward-modeling data-learnability problem, suggesting that interventions targeting learnability broadly (better data, more informative preference pairs) may matter more than interventions targeting length specifically.

A third, more cautionary learning is that neither switching optimization algorithms nor increasing model scale, the two most obvious "fixes" a practitioner might reach for, resolved the problem in this paper's tests: DPO showed the same length-inflation pattern as PPO despite eliminating the explicit reward model, and increasing reward-model scale from 7B to 13B improved accuracy only marginally. Any claim that a newer training algorithm or simply a bigger model solves reward-hacking-via-shallow-features should be treated as an empirical claim requiring the same length-stratified or cartography-style diagnostic used here, not assumed from the algorithm change or scale increase alone.

## Verification

This paper is itself substantially about verification: its central contribution is a set of diagnostics for checking whether an RLHF pipeline's reported improvement reflects genuine content-quality learning rather than exploitation of a confound, and it applies multiple, methodologically distinct verification layers rather than relying on any single check. The length-stratified NRG analysis provides a direct, quantitative decomposition of measured reward gain into length-attributable and non-length-attributable components. The controlled length-only-reward substitution (LPPO) provides an even stronger causal test: if a reward signal containing literally no quality information reproduces most of the standard pipeline's downstream win-rate improvement, that is direct evidence the standard pipeline's own reward signal is not meaningfully separating quality from length either. The length-heuristic-agreement statistic (accuracy of a rule that always prefers the longer response) is checked against actual reward-model accuracy as an external sanity check on how much of a trained model's apparent competence a trivial heuristic alone could explain. The dataset-cartography analysis is a genuine mechanistic probe into training dynamics rather than only an outcome-level statistic, directly supporting the paper's causal explanation (easy-example dominance) rather than only re-describing the correlation already established. The appendix's DPO, scaled-reward-model, and harmlessness experiments each function as a robustness check against a specific alternative explanation (a different algorithm, more capacity, a different preference objective) for the central finding, and the paper is explicit and appropriately cautious in each case about what these supplementary checks do and do not establish (e.g., the harmlessness result is described as only "scratching the surface").

## Field context

This paper sits in the empirical RLHF-diagnostics literature alongside work it directly cites on reward over-optimization (Stiennon et al. 2020's pathological summaries under over-optimized reward; Dubois et al. 2023's AlpacaFarm finding that human preference for a policy drops past a certain reward threshold; Pang et al. 2022's synthetic demonstrations of reward hacking), distinguishing its own contribution as studying over-optimization in realistic, currently-"working" RLHF settings rather than only synthetic or already-known-pathological ones. It also engages the DPO literature (Rafailov et al. 2023) directly, testing whether a popular RLHF alternative that removes the explicit reward model inherits the same vulnerability, and finding that it does. Within this corpus, the paper has a direct empirical point of contact with [2204.05862] (Anthropic's HH-RLHF paper, already reviewed): its Appendix C.3 harmlessness experiment retrains a reward model on that exact same Anthropic preference dataset and reports a qualitatively different failure pattern (low accuracy but no length bias) than its own helpfulness settings show, a useful, independently-collected data point bearing on 2204.05862's own discussion of the helpfulness/harmlessness tension and on how differently reward-model shallow-feature reliance can manifest depending on which objective is being optimized. The paper's broader diagnosis, that a reward model deprived of enough learnable signal in most of its training data will default to whatever easy, shallow correlate it can confidently fit, is also a useful general lens on [2306.04707] (BlenderBot 3x, also reviewed in this batch), whose own reward model is built from noisy, partly-adversarial organic feedback and whose authors independently found that naively using unfiltered organic data or out-of-distribution public preference data failed to help; both papers converge, via very different application domains (open academic preference benchmarks here, a real product deployment there), on reward-model robustness to spurious or shallow signal as a recurring, not dataset-specific, weak point in current preference-learning pipelines.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claim is unusually well-triangulated for an empirical RLHF paper: the length-stratified NRG analysis, the direct length-only-reward substitution experiment, and the dataset-cartography training-dynamics analysis each probe the same underlying claim (reward models and PPO are dominated by length rather than content quality) through methodologically independent means, and each returns a mutually reinforcing result rather than resting on a single statistic. The intervention study is comprehensive rather than cherry-picked, spanning both the PPO-optimization side and the reward-modeling side of the pipeline, and the paper reports every intervention's failure to fully solve the problem plainly, including outright convergence failures, rather than presenting only a favorable subset.

The clearest scope limitation, one the paper's own Appendix C.2 partially anticipates and checks but does not fully resolve, is that every main experiment uses a single base-model family and scale (LoRA-tuned Llama-7B); the one scale check performed (reward-model accuracy at Llama-2-13B) shows only marginal improvement, but this check was not extended to a full re-run of the PPO pipeline, the interventions, or the cartography analysis at the larger scale, so it remains an open question whether the length-dominance pattern, or the specific interventions found ineffective here, would look different at the substantially larger scales (tens to hundreds of billions of parameters, or with more heavily curated preference data) used by the frontier RLHF systems this paper's findings are most likely to be read as commentary on. A second, related gap: each anti-length intervention is tested in isolation rather than in combination; the paper does not report whether stacking, for instance, length-balanced preference data together with a high-KL PPO objective and reward-data-augmentation might close more of the gap than any single intervention does alone, leaving open whether the paper's negative conclusion ("no strategy works for all settings") would hold against a combined approach it did not test. Neither of these is a flaw in what the paper does report, both are explicit, reasonable scoping choices for a single study, but they bound how far the paper's specific negative results about individual interventions should be generalized.

What remains well supported: across three genuinely different preference-data sources and label types, RLHF's measured improvement over SFT is substantially, often overwhelmingly, explained by increased output length rather than other content features; a reward signal containing no quality information at all reproduces most of standard RLHF's reported downstream win-rate gain; this pattern persists under DPO as well as PPO; and the underlying cause traces specifically to reward models being unable to confidently learn from most of their preference training data, defaulting to length as the rare feature they can fit confidently, a mechanistic explanation directly supported by training-dynamics evidence rather than only inferred from the correlational pattern.
