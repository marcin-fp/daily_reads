Citation: Galil, I., Kimhi, M., and El-Yaniv, R. Maximal Brain Damage Without Data or Optimization: Disrupting Neural Networks via Sign-Bit Flips. Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 2025.

## What this paper is about

Neural network weights are usually thought of as being either correct (from training) or corrupted by noise. This paper asks a narrower, more adversarial question: if an attacker can flip only a tiny number of individual bits in a trained network's stored parameters, without ever seeing the training data or running the model, how much damage can they do? The authors show the answer is "a great deal": flipping the sign bit of just one or two carefully chosen parameters, selected using nothing more than the parameters' own stored magnitudes, can collapse an ImageNet classifier's accuracy by over 99%, wreck an object detector's or a segmentation model's output, and (their most striking demonstration) reduce a mixture-of-experts reasoning language model's accuracy on a math benchmark from 78% to 0% by flipping two bits inside two different "experts." They also show that the same critical-parameter analysis that powers the attack can be turned around into a cheap, targeted defense: protecting roughly 1% of the most vulnerable bits is enough to neutralize a much more expensive prior attack.

## Extended summary

The method, Deep Neural Lesion (DNL), works under a deliberately restrictive threat model: the attacker has write access to the model's stored parameters but no training or validation data, and cannot run the model at all (no forward or backward passes). Under this constraint, DNL selects candidate bits to flip purely by parameter magnitude (the largest-magnitude weights, by absolute value, are hypothesized and empirically shown to be the most damaging to flip), with an added constraint for convolutional networks that limits the attack to one flipped bit per kernel (because two flips within the same kernel can partially cancel each other's effect on the kernel's output, whereas spreading flips across kernels compounds the damage) and a preference for early network layers (empirically the most damaging place to intervene, analogous to how early sensory lesions cause outsized downstream impairment). A relaxed variant, 1P-DNL, allows a single forward-and-backward pass on one random input to compute a hybrid magnitude-plus-second-order (Gauss-Newton-approximated Hessian) importance score, which the authors connect explicitly to classical pruning criteria (Optimal Brain Damage / Optimal Brain Surgeon, and later magnitude- and gradient-based pruning methods), reframing them as adversarial saliency scores instead of pruning scores. Sign-bit flips are simple to locate physically in the IEEE-754 floating-point representation (always the most significant bit) and are argued to be more consistent with realistic hardware fault-injection mechanisms (Rowhammer, DMA-based memory tampering, GPU cache tampering, voltage/frequency glitching, firmware or rootkit compromise) than approaches requiring many coordinated flips.

The evaluation spans three domains. In image classification, DNL and 1P-DNL are tested on 60 classifiers, including 48 ImageNet models spanning several architecture families (ResNet, RegNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, ViT, MobileNet, AlexNet, VGG, Inception) plus four additional fine-grained datasets (DTD, FGVC-Aircraft, Food101, Stanford Cars); flipping one or two sign bits collapses most models, and model scale does not predict robustness. In object detection and instance segmentation on COCO, attacking only the backbone (leaving task-specific heads untouched) is enough to drive Mask R-CNN's box and mask AP to near zero with one or two flips, and to remove over 77% of YOLOv8-seg's performance, with qualitatively different failure modes (Mask R-CNN keeps plausible localization but assigns the wrong class; YOLOv8-seg misses the object and hallucinates a different one). In language models, three reasoning models (Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B) are evaluated on a fixed 50-question subset of MATH-500, scored with the benchmark's own canonical verifier; two sign flips into two different expert down-projection weights collapse the Mixture-of-Experts Qwen3-30B-A3B from 78% to 0% accuracy, and 1P-DNL needs only one flip to reach a 71.8% reduction. Corrupted generations degenerate into repetitive boilerplate text rather than plausible near-miss errors. Random sign flips are shown to be far less effective than targeted ones across all three domains, though random flips have a more noticeable effect on language generation than on vision, which the authors attribute to autoregressive error compounding. A parallel evaluation of exponent-bit (rather than sign-bit) flips finds exponent attacks can be even more destructive for language models (and for some vision architectures), though sign-bit attacks are generally the stronger and more consistent choice at low flip budgets in vision.

Compared against three prior weight-attack methods (BFA, DeepHammer, ZeBRA), all of which require either training/validation data or multiple iterative forward/backward passes (or both), DNL and 1P-DNL match or exceed their accuracy reduction while using equal or fewer flips and no data or optimization loop, formalized with a complexity comparison (O(θ) + O(k) versus O(k × B × θ × m) for the iterative baselines). The paper also evaluates existing defenses (bit replication, error-correcting codes, weight-scaling, binarization) and finds each either impractical (bit replication multiplies memory) or bypassable: weight-scaling has no effect on a sign flip because sign inversion is multiplicative and survives rescaling; a codeword-based error-correcting defense (DeepNcode) can be bypassed in a realistic gray-box setting by searching for an alternative valid codeword whose decoded value has the opposite sign; binarized networks still have their sign inverted by a flip. Building on the same critical-parameter identification, the authors propose a targeted defense that shields only the highest-scoring sign bits (via replication or ECC) and show that shielding roughly 0.001% of parameters already substantially reduces a prior iterative attack's (BFA's) impact, while shielding about 1% nullifies it entirely on every tested model.

## Learnings

A trained network's vulnerability to targeted single-bit corruption is concentrated in a small, identifiable set of "critical" parameters (large in magnitude, disproportionately in early layers), while the overwhelming majority of parameters are robust even to tens of thousands of random sign flips — this sharp critical/non-critical split, rather than a uniform fragility, is what makes both the attack and the proposed defense (protect only the critical subset) cheap.

The same critical-parameter identification that makes an attack effective can be repurposed directly as a defense: shielding on the order of 1% of parameters (the ones the attack would target first) neutralized a much more expensive iterative attack (BFA) in this paper's tests, an asymmetry worth remembering whenever a paper frames "we found the most sensitive components of a system" as purely offensive — the same analysis is often the cheapest available defense.

Corrupting a small number of Mixture-of-Experts routing/expert parameters can derail a language model's output even on tokens that never route through the corrupted expert, because the corrupted representation propagates forward through the rest of the network (here, through attention) once it has entered the hidden state — a mechanism worth remembering when reasoning about the isolation properties (or lack thereof) that sparse MoE architectures are sometimes assumed to provide.

## Verification

The paper's own verification content is about checking whether an attack (or defense) worked, not about a model's reasoning correctness. For image classification, detection, and segmentation, verification is a standard automated metric comparison (accuracy, AP) between clean and attacked models. For the language-model results, the paper explicitly states it scores generations "using the benchmark's canonical verifier" for MATH-500, i.e. an existing external, automated oracle rather than an LLM judge or the authors' own scoring — a clean, low-risk verification choice for a math benchmark with checkable final answers. For their attack pipeline itself, the authors report a concrete determinism/robustness check relevant to trusting the numbers: DNL is deterministic given a fixed model, layer budget, and flip budget, and 1P-DNL's only stochasticity (the single random input used to compute its score) produces a standard deviation of only 0.02 in accuracy reduction across 10 seeds on a representative subset of architectures, which the authors treat as negligible relative to the effect sizes reported — a reasonable and appropriately modest robustness check, though it covers only 1P-DNL's seed sensitivity, not sampling variability in the underlying accuracy metrics themselves (e.g. confidence intervals on AR values, which are not reported).

## Field context

The paper positions itself against a specific lineage of weight-space attacks that it treats as the closest prior work: Terminal Brain Damage (Hong et al., 2019), which showed exponent-bit manipulation can be destructive but did not consider sign bits; and three attacks the paper directly benchmarks against — BFA (Rakin et al., 2019) and DeepHammer (Yao et al., 2020), both iterative and gradient-based, requiring data; and ZeBRA (Park et al., 2021), which removes the real-data requirement but still performs an optimization loop using synthetically generated data and multiple forward/backward passes. The paper's stated contribution relative to this lineage is removing both the data and the optimization requirement simultaneously while matching or exceeding the resulting accuracy reduction, and extending the demonstrated scope from image classifiers to object detection, instance segmentation, and — new to this line of work — reasoning language models including Mixture-of-Experts routing. On the delivery side, the paper draws on an established hardware-security literature (Rowhammer-style DRAM disturbance errors, GPU cache tampering, DMA from untrusted peripherals, voltage/frequency glitching, firmware and rootkit compromise) to argue that bit-level write access is a realistic capability rather than a purely theoretical one, though (see Critical discussion) it does not engage with that literature's own well-known difficulty of targeting a specific bit at a specific address rather than achieving an arbitrary flip somewhere. This is the first paper in this corpus focused on adversarial/hardware-level ML security rather than model capability, evaluation methodology, or scientific-domain application; none of its cited precedents (BFA, DeepHammer, ZeBRA, Terminal Brain Damage) are yet reviewed here.

## Critical discussion

The vision, detection, and segmentation results are broad, methodologically consistent, and directly compared against multiple contemporaneous baselines (BFA, DeepHammer, ZeBRA) using the same accuracy-reduction metric, which is a real strength: this is not a single-architecture demonstration, and the comparison table (Table 4/6) makes the OF/DA (optimization-free/data-agnostic) trade-off explicit rather than asserting it qualitatively.

The language-model evaluation is comparatively narrow and, on inspection, somewhat inconsistently reported. Only three models are tested, on a fixed 50-question subset of MATH-500 rather than the full benchmark, and the paper's own caption for its central LLM table (Table 1) states that "when a run reaches at least 90% relative accuracy reduction, we report the first such budget; otherwise, we report a larger illustrative budget directly in the cell" — meaning different cells in the same table report different kinds of statistics (an achieved-threshold budget for some model/attack combinations, an arbitrary illustrative budget for others) depending on how each run went, which makes precise cross-model or cross-attack comparisons from this table less reliable than a uniformly reported metric would be. The paper's claim that "we expect the same corruption mode to disrupt other generation-based benchmarks as well, not only MATH-500" is a reasonable hypothesis given the qualitative nature of the observed failure (repetitive, nonsensical output rather than a narrow MATH-500-specific error), but it is stated as an expectation rather than demonstrated, and no other generation benchmark is actually tested.

The paper's framing — "once an attacker can write to stored parameters, the answer can be disturbingly small [amount of access needed]" — somewhat understates the difficulty of the premise it is built on. All of the hardware delivery mechanisms it cites as motivation (Rowhammer, GPU cache tampering, voltage/frequency glitching, DMA tampering) are themselves an active, difficult research area precisely because achieving a bit flip at a specific, attacker-chosen address in a large parameter tensor — rather than an uncontrolled flip somewhere in memory — is hard; the original Rowhammer literature the paper cites is largely about the difficulty of achieving reliable, targeted flips at all. DNL's genuine contribution is removing the need for training data and iterative optimization to decide which bits to target once write access exists; it does not by itself resolve the separate, and arguably harder, precision-targeting problem of physically directing a fault-injection technique at one specific bit among the billions in a modern model's parameter tensor. The paper's own Limitation paragraph is honest about a related point (the attack is weaker when only part of the model is writable or addressable), which partially, but not fully, covers this gap.

The defense-evaluation section is a genuine strength: rather than only proposing a defense, the authors show it defeats an independent, more expensive prior attack (BFA) under a stress test (100K random flips) that is not tied to their own attack's scoring method, which is a meaningfully more rigorous defense evaluation than testing a defense only against the attack it was designed to stop.

## Relevance to us

Most of this paper sits outside our current scope — it is about adversarial hardware-level attacks on deployed model weights, not about reasoning quality, verification of scientific claims, or physics-specific evaluation. The one genuine connection is to Tension 1 (frontier backbone vs small specialized models), which cites on-prem deployment as a benefit of smaller, specialized models: this paper is a concrete reminder that weight-at-rest integrity is a separate axis from accuracy, cost, or latency when evaluating an on-prem or edge deployment of a smaller model, since a small number of maliciously flipped bits (via a compromised firmware, driver, or hardware fault) can be enough to catastrophically and stealthily degrade a deployed model regardless of how well it performs when its weights are intact. If we ever compare a frontier-API-backed approach against a locally-deployed specialized model on security or reliability grounds (not just cost or capability), this paper is a specific, citable example of a failure mode unique to holding the weights locally, worth weighing against the usual privacy and cost benefits normally cited for that choice.
