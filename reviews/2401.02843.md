Citation: Grace, K., Stewart, H., Sandkühler, J. F., Thomas, S., Weinstein-Raun, B., Brauner, J., Korzekwa, R. C. Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI. Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 84:9, 2025 (reviewed as arXiv:2401.02843v3). https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843v3

## What this paper is about

This is the 2023 edition of the Expert Survey on Progress in AI (ESPAI), the third in a series run by AI Impacts after 2016 and 2022. In October 2023 the authors surveyed 2,778 people who had published at six top AI venues in 2022. They asked when AI would be able to do 39 specific tasks, every human task, and every human occupation, and how good or bad advanced AI would be for humanity. Question wordings were randomized so that framing effects could be measured.

The headline results: the forecasts moved noticeably earlier in the one year since 2022. The aggregate gives a 50% chance of "high-level machine intelligence" by 2047, 13 years earlier than the 2022 survey. Respondents are very uncertain and disagree widely about outcomes. A majority put at least 5% on outcomes as bad as human extinction, and most want AI safety research prioritized more. The data are the opinions of a 15% response sample, not measurements of AI systems, and the authors say that expert forecasts are unreliable.

## Extended summary

**Design and fielding.** The authors collected about 21,800 author names from 2022 papers at NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI, IJCAI, and JMLR. They found emails for 20,066 (92%). 1,607 of those bounced, leaving 18,459 working addresses. They received 2,778 responses (15%), of which 95% were marked "finished" by Qualtrics. The survey ran 11 to 24 October 2023, with a pre-announcement and four or more reminders. A pilot sent 5% of invitees a $50 reward offer (or an equivalent charitable donation) and another 5% no offer. The paid group responded at a "substantially higher" rate (no numbers are given), so the reward was then offered to everyone. The authors estimated the survey took a median 16 minutes. Invitations described the topic only as "the future of AI progress". However, the emails linked to past ESPAI surveys and named the authors' affiliations. The survey had University of Bonn ethics approval (248/23-EP) and was preregistered on OSF.

Most questions repeat the 2016 and 2022 wording. New questions were piloted in interviews with AI researchers and students. Each respondent saw a random subset of question blocks, so per-question samples are much smaller than 2,778. Examples: HLMI n=1,714; FAOL n=774; about 250 estimates per task; long-run value of HLMI n=2,704; the three extinction wordings n=1,321, 661, and 655; eleven concern scenarios about 1,345; 2043 traits 649 to 667; 2028 interpretability 912; safety prioritization 1,329; meta questions 671.

Every timing question came in one of two framings, and each respondent kept one framing throughout (1:1 allocation):

- Fixed-probabilities: give the number of years until a 10%, 50%, and 90% chance.
- Fixed-years: give the probability of the milestone within 10, 20, and 50 years. The HLMI question used 40 instead of 50 years, a mistake kept from earlier surveys for consistency.

Aggregation works as follows. The three year-probability pairs from each respondent are fitted with a gamma CDF. The aggregate is the mean of those CDFs, with the two framings given equal weight. Data cleaning removed several kinds of answer:

- Answers whose probabilities decrease over time (196 participants gave at least one).
- Fixed-years answers summing to 100% from 7 of those participants.
- Non-numerical answers, unless one reading was unambiguous.

Calendar years typed in place of year counts (for example 2033 instead of 10) were converted by subtracting 2023. "Never" was coded as 100,000,000 years; the authors say the results are insensitive to that bound.

**Tasks.** "Feasible" meant that one of the best-resourced labs could implement the task within a year if it chose to. Each respondent answered four tasks. Most tasks got at least a 50% chance within ten years. The abstract highlights a 50% chance by 2028 for three tasks: building a payment-processing website from scratch, producing a song indistinguishable from a new song by a popular artist, and autonomously downloading and fine-tuning an LLM. The text says "all but four" of the 39 tasks fall within ten years, but it then lists six that take longer, with years to a 50% chance:

- Output the differential equations governing a virtual world in symbolic form (the example is recovering Newton's laws in a game engine): 12 years.
- Physically install the electrical wiring in a new home: 17 years.
- Research and write a high-quality ML paper: 19 years.
- Replicate an ML paper to the ML Reproducibility Challenge standard: 12 years.
- Prove theorems publishable in top mathematics journals: 22 years.
- Solve a long-standing problem such as a Millennium Prize problem: 27 years.

32 tasks were identical across 2016, 2022, and 2023. From 2022 to 2023, 21 moved earlier and 11 moved later. The mean shift of the 50th-percentile year was 1.0 year earlier (SD 2.0, SE 0.18).

Appendix F reports Yuen bootstrap tests on 50% years. Section 3.2.2 describes this test as run on fixed-probabilities respondents. Positive differences mean 2023 is earlier than 2022:

- HLMI: trimmed-mean difference 12.5 years (CI 7.6 to 17.4, p reported as 0).
- FAOL: 37.5 years (CI 13.1 to 62, p=0.0052).
- AI researcher occupation: 21.8 years (p=0.0066).
- Tasks: 7 of 32 have p<0.05, with no multiple-comparison correction mentioned. The virtual-world equations task moved later by 3.5 years (p=0.058).

**HLMI and FAOL.** HLMI is "achieved when unaided machines can accomplish every task better and more cheaply than human workers", counting feasibility rather than adoption. The HLMI question also told respondents to assume "human scientific activity continues without major negative disruption". FAOL is reached when every occupation is fully automatable. The FAOL block first asked about truck driver, surgeon, retail salesperson, and AI researcher, and asked for a write-in guess at one of the last occupations to be automated. The top write-in categories were Computer and Mathematical (91), Life, Physical, and Social Science (77), and Healthcare (56).

Aggregate forecasts across the three editions (Table 6):

| Milestone | 2016 | 2022 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|---|
| HLMI, 50% chance | 2061 | 2060 (first reported as 2059) | 2047 |
| HLMI, 10% chance | 2025 | 2029 | 2027 |
| FAOL, 50% chance | 2138 | 2164 | 2116 |
| FAOL, 10% chance | 2036 | 2050 | 2037 |

The 2022 HLMI figure was first reported as 2059; code and cleaning changes since then moved it to 2060. Compared with 2016, the 2023 50% estimates are earlier but the 10% estimates are later.

FAOL's 50% date has been more than 60 years later than HLMI's in all three surveys, about 70 years in 2023. The authors do not know why. Candidate explanations they list:

- A pure framing effect, since HLMI arguably implies FAOL.
- The "no disruption" clause, which only the HLMI question carries.
- Thinking through specific occupations first.
- Reading occupations as including physical manipulation.
- Reading FAOL as adoption rather than feasibility.

The fixed-years versus fixed-probabilities framing effect replicated. The 50% HLMI year was 34 years out under fixed-years and 17 under fixed-probabilities. Even the later of these is earlier than the 2022 combined estimate of 37 years. Respondents whose undergraduate education was in Asia expected HLMI 11 years earlier than everyone else combined. Time in field and citation count had no significant effect. Asked how much they disagree with the typical AI researcher on HLMI timing, 44% said "not much", 46% "a moderate amount", and 10% "a lot".

**Progress dynamics.** Most respondents said progress in their own area was faster in the second half of their time in it (median time in the area was 5 years; the numbers appear only in a figure).

On the causes of progress, respondents imagined having half of each input over the past decade. Halving the decline in compute cost was rated the largest loss, and researcher effort and algorithmic progress the smallest. The compute item was framed differently from the others, as costs falling about half as far on a log scale. Many answers were exactly 0% or 100%, which the authors read as possible misunderstanding. They say the fairly even spread "cuts against" the narrative that cheap compute is the dominant driver.

The intelligence-explosion medians (Table 1) are identical or nearly so in 2016, 2022, and 2023:

| Question | Median answer |
|---|---|
| Explosive technological progress 2 years after HLMI | 20% |
| Explosive technological progress 30 years after HLMI | 80% |
| The intelligence-explosion argument is broadly correct | "41-60%" (about even chance) |
| AI vastly better than humans at all professions, 2 years after HLMI | 10% |
| AI vastly better than humans at all professions, 30 years after HLMI | 60% (50% in 2016) |

One sentence in the text says "2 and 20 years" where the table says 30.

**Traits of AI in 2043.** Respondents rated eleven traits for at least some state-of-the-art systems in 2043. All eleven were rated fairly likely. Only "take actions to attain power" had a median below even chance. No trait drew more than 55% "very likely" or "very unlikely". The share answering likely or very likely was 82.3% for "find unexpected ways to achieve goals", 81.4% for "talk like a human expert on most topics", and 69.1% for "frequently behave in ways surprising to humans". For 2028, only 20% gave better than even odds that users could know the true reasons for a system's choices, meaning the AI correctly explains its internal decision process (n=912). The related 2043 item, whether systems "can be trusted to accurately explain their actions", had a median of even chance.

**Social impacts.** Each of eleven 30-year concern scenarios was rated substantial or extreme concern by more than 30% of respondents. The top five were:

- Spread of false information such as deepfakes: 86%.
- Manipulation of large-scale public opinion: 79%.
- Dangerous groups making powerful tools such as engineered viruses: 73%.
- Authoritarian population control: 73%.
- Worsened economic inequality: 71%.

The authors note that these ratings cannot separate likelihood from severity.

On the long-run value of HLMI (n=2,704; this question could not be skipped):

- 68.3% found good outcomes more likely than bad.
- 64% put nonzero probability on both extremely good and extremely bad outcomes.
- 57.8% put at least 5% on extremely bad outcomes (for example extinction).
- Among net optimists, 48.4% gave at least 5% to extremely bad outcomes. Among net pessimists, 58.6% gave at least 5% to extremely good outcomes.
- The median probability of extremely bad outcomes was 5% (mean 9%). 38% gave at least 10%, and one in ten gave at least 25%.

Since 2022 the mean on extremely bad outcomes fell from 14% to 9.0%. The share giving at least 10% fell "from 48% in 2022" (the 2023 value is missing from the sentence).

Three extinction wordings were asked, each respondent seeing one (Table 2). Q1: future AI advances causing human extinction or similarly permanent and severe disempowerment. Q2: human inability to control future advanced AI systems causing it. Q3: Q1 restricted to the next 100 years.

| Wording | 2022: n, mean, median | 2023: n, mean (SD), median (IQR) |
|---|---|---|
| Q1, future AI advances | 149, 15.7%, 5% | 1,321, 16.2% (23), 5% (19) |
| Q2, inability to control AI | 162, 20.5%, 10% | 661, 19.4% (26), 10% (29) |
| Q3, within 100 years | not asked | 655, 14.4% (22.2), 5% (19.9) |

Depending on wording, 41.2% to 51.4% gave more than 10%. Counting the long-run value question as well, the range is 38% to 51%. Industry and academia both had a 5% median on extinction. Respondents educated in Asia had a 10% median against 5% for North America and Europe. Those who had thought more about the question had a 9% median against 5% for those who had thought little.

Table 4 reports the share giving more than 10% by amount of prior thought about social impacts:

| Outcome, share above 10% | Little thought (n=621) | A lot of thought (n=1,113) |
|---|---|---|
| Extremely good | 68% | 41% |
| Extremely bad | 34% | 71% |

**Preferences.** Preferred speed of global AI progress over the next five years (Table 3): much slower 4.8%, somewhat slower 29.9%, current speed 26.9%, somewhat faster 22.8%, much faster 15.6%. Asked how far public AI-risk concerns come from misunderstanding AI research (n=671): "almost entirely" 10.7%, "to a large extent" 44%, "somewhat" 29.1%, "not much" 14.6%, "hardly at all" 1.6%. Two versions of the safety-prioritization question differed only by an added bias-mitigation example. They did not differ (Welch t(1327) = -0.58, p=0.564, d=-0.03), so the authors pooled them (n=1,329). 70% said AI safety research should be prioritized "more" or "much more", a slight rise since 2022.

On Stuart Russell's alignment argument, 41% called it "a very important problem" and 13% "among the most important problems in the field". 36% said it is harder and 21% much harder than other AI problems. Respondents did not generally think it was more valuable to work on today than other problems.

**Authors' caveats.**

- Forecasting is hard and subject experts are often poor forecasters (Tetlock). Wide disagreement limits how many respondents can be right.
- Large framing effects suggest that even aggregate answers are not an accurate guide. Karger et al. found college graduates' existential-risk estimates varied by nearly six orders of magnitude with elicitation method. The authors argue that respondents who had thought more gave higher numbers, so further low-probability examples are unlikely to push them radically lower.
- A 15% response rate is typical for surveys with more than 20,000 invitees.
- On participation bias, they compared random samples of responders (n=369) and non-responders (n=589) using best-guess public data. Industry researchers were 61% as likely to respond as the base rate, women 66% (about one in ten of the sample), researchers with at least 1,000 citations 69%, and people in Asia 84%. Female respondents expected less extreme outcomes.
- Opinion questions were answered by 96% of those who saw them on average. The least-answered was the AI-researcher occupation question under fixed-probabilities, at 90.3%. Only 7.6% had thought "a great deal" about HLMI timing and 10.3% about social impacts.
- The 2023 sample adds four venues. Where checked, the NeurIPS/ICML subset "appeared to have very similar opinions" to the full sample; no comparison numbers are shown.

## Learnings

Changing only the elicitation format moved expert timelines by a factor of two. Asking for years at fixed probabilities gave 17 years to an even chance of HLMI; asking for probabilities at fixed years gave 34. The effect has replicated across three survey editions. No one knows which format is more accurate, so averaging them with equal weight is a convention, not a correction.

Two descriptions of nearly the same endpoint gave very different forecasts: "every task" (HLMI) and "every occupation" (FAOL) are about 70 years apart in 2023 and more than 60 years apart in every edition. The two question blocks also differ in instructions and in which examples come first, so the survey cannot tell which ingredient causes the gap. Any aggregate "expert forecast of AGI" inherits whichever definition the pollster happened to choose.

Randomizing wordings between respondents also exposes incoherence. The narrower event, extinction through loss of control, got a higher median (10%) and mean (19.4%) than the broader event, extinction from any future AI advances (5% and 16.2%). That is our reading of Table 2, not the authors'. It suggests these numbers work better as order-of-magnitude levels of concern than as probabilities.

Outcome credences do not fit on one optimism-pessimism axis. 64% put nonzero weight on both extremely good and extremely bad outcomes, and about half of net optimists still gave at least 5% to catastrophe. A single mean "probability of doom" throws most of that structure away.

## Field context

**The authors' framing.** This is the third ESPAI, after 2016 (Grace et al., published in JAIR in 2018) and 2022. It has about four times the participants of the 2022 edition and adds four venues. The authors present it as the largest survey of AI researchers to date. They place it next to surveys of the public and of corporate leaders, and treat it as one noisy input alongside trend extrapolation, reference classes, economic models, and forecasting aggregators.

The survey ran in October 2023. That was after ChatGPT, GPT-4, Bard, Bing Chat, and Claude 1 and 2 were released, after the two widely signed AI-risk letters, and during early US, UK, and EU regulatory moves. The paper was posted in January 2024; the arXiv metadata lists a 2025 JAIR version. The bibliography includes an earlier survey of ML researchers (Zhang et al. 2022) and expert elicitations from climate and energy research, but the extracted body never discusses them. Any comparison with that literature is therefore ours, not the authors'.

**Other work in this corpus.** No other review here is an expert-opinion forecast of AI progress. The nearest survey is [reviews/26aisc.md](26aisc.md), a 2026 survey of 637 scientists alongside Gemini usage logs. It asks how AI is used now, not when it will do what, and its population is working scientists, not AI authors.

Two reviewed papers argue about the same intelligence-explosion question. [reviews/2606.12683.md](2606.12683.md) is a 2026 Google DeepMind report that treats a fast AGI-to-ASI transition as an open possibility. [reviews/2601.05280.md](2601.05280.md) is a formal argument that self-imitation training alone gives no direction of improvement. The ESPAI medians, stable since 2016, sit between these positions: 20% for explosive progress within 2 years of HLMI, 80% within 30 years, and about even chance that the argument is broadly correct.

Several of the milestones that ESPAI respondents placed furthest out have since been tested directly by reviewed work, though never under ESPAI's own definition ("a best-resourced lab could implement it within a year"):

- "Research and write a high-quality ML paper" (50% at 19 years). [reviews/2607.27191.md](2607.27191.md) reports that two agent-written papers, graded by the original human authors of the same research questions in 2026, were both rejected (2/6 and 1/6). [reviews/2605.19156.md](2605.19156.md) checks 117 agent-written papers against their code and logs.
- "Replicate an ML paper" (12 years). [reviews/2608.13331.md](2608.13331.md) trains agents on the much narrower task of replicating single redacted figures within 60 minutes.
- Publishable theorems and long-standing open problems (22 and 27 years). The closest tests are research-level math benchmarks such as [reviews/2604.06802.md](2604.06802.md).

These later papers neither confirm nor refute the forecasts. They show where the 2023 expectations are now being measured.

## Critical discussion

**What the survey can establish.** As an opinion survey the paper is careful. It is preregistered, has ethics approval, randomizes framings, repeats earlier wordings exactly, releases its data, and states its caveats openly. The main claims describe what about 2,700 people said in October 2023, and the design supports those descriptive claims well. The difficulty is that the paper's own results show how much those statements depend on the question asked. The reader's main job is to separate findings that survive framing from numbers that do not.

**Headline dates depend on framing.** The fixed-years versus fixed-probabilities gap for HLMI is 17 years, which is larger than the 13-year shift between 2022 and 2023 that the abstract leads with. HLMI and FAOL differ by about 70 years. So "2047" is an equal-weight mixture of two formats that disagree by a factor of two. It is also a mean of per-respondent gamma CDFs, and the paper does not describe how those were fitted (three points, two parameters, loss function unstated).

The direction of the shift is better supported than its size. In the fixed-probabilities subsample the HLMI difference is 12.5 years with a bootstrap CI of 7.6 to 17.4 (Appendix F). Even the later fixed-years estimate for 2023 (34 years) is earlier than 2022's combined 37. No test is reported for the fixed-years subsample.

The task-level story is weaker than "21 of 32 moved earlier" suggests. Only 7 of the 32 tasks reach p<0.05, from 39 uncorrected tests. The mean shift is 1.0 year, and the reported SE of 0.18 does not match 32 tasks as the unit (SD/√32 is about 0.35), so it is unclear what was resampled.

The between-edition comparison also mixes a time change with a population change: two venues in 2022, six in 2023. The authors say the NeurIPS/ICML subset looked similar "where we checked", but they show no numbers.

**Extinction numbers are not coherent probabilities.** The authors call the three extinction wordings "mostly consistent" (medians of 5% or 10%). Appendix A.3 treats that similarity as "robust evidence that the majority of AI researchers think there is a nontrivial risk". But respondents were randomly assigned to the wordings, and the narrower loss-of-control event received more probability than the broader any-AI-advances event (median 10% vs 5%, mean 19.4% vs 16.2%; Table 2). That ordering is the conjunction-style pattern the framing literature warns about. It does not undercut the qualitative finding that many respondents put non-negligible weight on catastrophe. It does mean that specific values like 5%, 10%, or "16%" should not be carried forward as calibrated expert probabilities.

**Table 4 conflicts with the rest of the paper.** Table 4 says 71% of the 1,113 respondents who had thought "a lot" gave more than 10% to extremely bad outcomes. Table 5 gives that same group a median of 5% on extremely bad outcomes, and a median of 5% means at most half the group can be above 10%. Pooled, Table 4's 34% and 71% imply roughly 58% of those 1,734 respondents above 10%, while Section 4.2 reports 38% for all 2,704. The table as printed cannot be reconciled with Table 5 or Section 4.2. The extract matches the raw dump here, so this is not conversion damage.

Section 5.2.1 relies on this table to argue that more-informed respondents would not give radically lower numbers. The direction of that association is separately supported by the A.3 extinction medians (9% vs 5%). The inference still has two problems. It does not address the mechanism Karger et al. describe, anchoring on low-probability reference examples. And, as the authors themselves note in A.2.1, thinking more about the topic is confounded with being concerned about it.

**Representativeness.** A 15% response rate, a paid incentive, and emails that linked to past ESPAI results leave room for self-selection. The paper's bias check is modest: 369 responders against 589 non-responders, with attributes guessed from public data. That check found industry researchers at 61% and highly cited researchers at 69% of the base response rate. No reweighting is attempted. The similar industry and academia medians on extinction (both 5%) reduce the concern for that item.

Even so, the step from "respondents" to "the majority of AI researchers", which Appendix A.3 calls robust, is stronger than the evidence. The robustness shown is across wordings and respondent subgroups, not across non-response. This is a scoped overclaim, not evidence that the population thinks otherwise.

**The compute-versus-other-inputs claim.** The statement that the answers "cut against" the compute-dominance narrative rests on an item that was framed differently from the other four (footnote 2), in a question block the authors themselves suspect was misunderstood, given the excess of exactly 0% and 100% answers. The data show that respondents rated all five inputs as important. They do not give a clean comparison of compute against the rest.

**Reporting slips.** Several are individually minor:

- "All but four" of the tasks, followed by a list of six.
- "2 and 20 years" in the text against 30 years in Table 1.
- The missing 2023 value in "fallen from 48% in 2022 in 2023".
- A duplicated paragraph in Section 3.2.2.
- An unshown check that the 100,000,000-year bound for "never" does not matter.

Together they suggest that specific numbers should be taken from the released data rather than from the prose.

**What still holds.** Several findings are well supported as statements about this respondent pool in late 2023:

- Forecasts moved earlier between 2022 and 2023 under both framings.
- Respondents disagree enormously, and individuals spread their outcome credences widely.
- Depending on wording, roughly 38% to 51% gave at least 10% to extinction-level outcomes, and a majority gave at least 5%.
- About 70% want more priority for AI safety research, and this is stable across two wordings.
- Framing effects are large and replicate across three editions. This is arguably the paper's most solid scientific result.

The specific dates and percentages are best read as format-dependent summaries of opinion, which is close to how the authors themselves frame them in Section 5.2.

## Relevance to us

Mostly context rather than evidence about how to build physics AI. The milestones closest to our work are the ones respondents expected last:

- Recovering the symbolic differential equations of a virtual world from experiments (about 12 years to an even chance, and the one task that moved later in Appendix F, by 3.5 years at p=0.058).
- Writing a publishable ML paper (19 years).
- Proving publishable theorems (22 years).
- Solving a Millennium-type problem (27 years).

As of late 2023, then, the ML community's prior put autonomous theory-level research at the far end of the capability curve. That weakly supports treating deep physics reasoning as the niche the generalists find hardest. It is opinion, though, and it says nothing about whether depth beats scale there.

The more transferable lesson is about eliciting credences, which matters for conjecture-plus-credence work (B10) and for how we interview physicists. Among experts, the elicitation format shifted answers by a factor of two. Task versus occupation wording shifted them by decades. And a narrower event received more probability than a broader one. If Theo asks users or models for credences, the format should be fixed and recorded. Cheap coherence checks, such as confirming that a subset event never gets more probability than its superset, would catch the kind of inconsistency visible in Table 2.

The forecasts that only 20% expect faithful self-explanation by 2028, and that 82% expect systems to find unexpected ways to achieve goals, agree with the trust concerns our interviewees raised. They are predictions, not measurements, and should not be cited as evidence about current models.
