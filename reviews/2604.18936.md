Citation: Woodward, N. S., Gao, Z., Kvasiuk, Y., Smith, K. M., Sala, F., Münchmeyer, M. Fine-Tuning Small Reasoning Models for Quantum Field Theory. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18936v1

## What this paper is about

Industry labs fine-tune huge models for advanced reasoning but rarely publish their training data, exact setup, or intermediate rollouts, which makes it hard for academic researchers to study how domain-specific physics reasoning actually develops during training. This paper is, to the authors' knowledge, the first academic study that fine-tunes a small (7B-parameter) reasoning model specifically for quantum field theory (QFT), building its own pipeline to generate thousands of verifiable QFT problems (auto-checkable via Python test cases) since no such open dataset existed, then comparing reinforcement learning (RL) against supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the same base model, and analyzing in detail how the model's reasoning errors change as a result.

## Extended summary

The data pipeline converts both fully synthetic QFT problems and human-authored ones (from Zee, Peskin & Schroeder, Weinberg, exercise books, MIT OpenCourseWare, and ~100 curated arXiv manuscripts) into a unified schema: a problem statement, a Python function skeleton the model must implement, a "golden solution" with worked derivation, and a set of physically-meaningful test cases for automated verification. Problems are organized along two independent difficulty axes: domain difficulty (background knowledge depth, from Advanced Undergraduate to Post-Graduate) and operational difficulty (mechanical/logical demands, used to define Easy/Medium/Hard tiers). Synthetic problems are generated by Gemini-2.5-Pro (Easy/Medium) or Gemini-3-Pro (Hard and all human-adapted sets), pass a five-metric LLM quality-grading filter, and must additionally be solved by GPT-5 (1 attempt for Easy/Medium, 3 for Hard) to be retained — this "frontier model verification" step removes roughly 15-58% of already quality-filtered problems depending on tier, with the Hard tier failing verification most often. The authors are explicit that this pipeline has known error sources (occasional wrong "golden" solutions from Gemini-2.5-Pro, calculation-convention statements that sometimes accidentally reveal the solution approach — caught only after RL training when validation accuracy dropped from ~60% to 54% after correcting them, and rare golden-code bugs causing false negatives) and that generation/filtering by specific frontier models introduces inductive bias favoring those model families, which they flag as a concern for benchmarking validity (though not, they argue, for training use on non-family models).

Three training experiments use DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B as the base model. (1) RL with GRPO (accuracy-only binary reward, clip-higher, no KL penalty) on QFT Easy raises accuracy from 40.2% to 54.2% in-distribution, with strong zero-shot transfer to Medium (26.2%→44.0%) and a small gain on Hard (0%→2.0%); consistent gains also appear on the human-adapted arXiv (16.6%→24.6%) and QFT Pedagogy (7.8%→14.2%) sets and on TPBench (23.5%→30.5%). (2) SFT on rejection-sampled correct reasoning traces from three open-weight teacher models (oss-120b, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B) shows Qwen3-30B traces give the largest gains despite Qwen3-30B being the weakest teacher on the underlying tasks, which the authors attribute to closer stylistic match to the 7B student's native reasoning style; SFT outperforms RL in-distribution (Easy: 59.7% vs. 54.2%) but RL generalizes better out-of-distribution (arXiv: +5.8pp for RL vs. SFT; TPBench: +1.0pp RL). (3) A narrow-domain RL experiment (LoRA fine-tuning on only 35 fermion/spinor problems) improves in-distribution accuracy substantially (Easy: 14.5%→25.5%; Medium: 8.9%→20.0%) with no measurable catastrophic forgetting but also no positive transfer to other topics.

A three-stage "Distill-then-Classify" chain-of-thought (CoT) analysis pipeline (golden-solution decomposition into logical steps, CoT deduplication/distillation to strip self-correction noise, then LLM-based error classification into Factual/Mathematical/Logical/Executional categories with severity) is developed after two simpler approaches (whole-CoT analysis, line-by-line analysis) failed due to context-length and granularity problems. Applied to the 20 most-improved problems for each method: both RL and SFT reduce incorrect-rollout counts by ~65-67%, and in both cases the largest per-rollout error-frequency drop is in factual errors (RL: 0.59→0.37; SFT: 0.51→0.44), with mathematical errors becoming proportionally dominant among the remaining failures — a finding the authors call surprising against the conventional view that SFT teaches knowledge while RL improves strategy, since both methods show the same factual-error-preferential-reduction pattern. Trace-length and backtracking analysis (8,000 traces per model) finds RL does not change aggregate backtracking frequency but reallocates it: more backtracking on eventually-correct traces (12.9→20.6 events), less on eventually-incorrect ones (31.4→27.7). A baseline benchmarking appendix (Appendix D) situates the difficulty gradient using Claude-Opus-4.5 and Gemini-2.5-Flash: Claude drops from 81.5% (Easy) to 44.5% (Hard), and the authors use this frontier-model degradation, independent of their own generator/verifier models, as external confirmation that the difficulty tiers are real rather than an artifact of their pipeline. A separate CoT-analyzer consistency check (Appendix G) cross-validates the gpt-oss-120b-based error classifier against Claude-Sonnet-4.6 on 52 incorrect attempts across 7 problems; aggregate error-category totals agree within about 5 percentage points, with most disagreement traced to genuine definitional ambiguity between adjacent error categories rather than analyzer unreliability. Data pipeline, VerifiableQFT training data (~200M tokens of reasoning traces), and code are publicly released.

## Learnings

The "Distill-then-Classify" CoT-analysis pattern (decompose a golden solution into reference logical steps; separately deduplicate and distill a noisy, self-correcting rollout into an analogous step sequence; then classify errors by comparing the two step sequences with a fixed global label set) is a directly reusable recipe for any project needing to analyze long reasoning-model traces at scale — the two simpler approaches the authors tried first (whole-trace analysis, line-by-line analysis) failing for well-explained, generalizable reasons (context saturation from self-correction noise; granularity mismatch between lines and logical steps) is itself useful negative-result guidance.

The finding that both RL and SFT preferentially eliminate factual errors first, leaving mathematical/executional errors as the dominant residual failure mode, cuts against the common assumption that "SFT teaches knowledge, RL teaches strategy" and suggests that for a small base model with meaningful factual gaps, both training signals will first correct recall before improving derivation execution — a plausible, generalizable claim about where the easiest gains sit during any small-model domain fine-tuning, though established here on one base model and one narrow physics subdomain. The RL backtracking-reallocation result (unchanged aggregate backtracking, but redistributed toward eventually-successful traces and away from eventually-failed ones) is a specific, testable mechanistic claim that connects directly to the broader "overthinking" literature on reasoning-length dynamics.

The observation that operational difficulty (number of derivation steps and manipulations), not domain difficulty (depth of required physics knowledge), is the primary driver of solve rate — post-graduate topics were sometimes easier than advanced-undergraduate ones because generating models defaulted to shallow reasoning for "easy" post-graduate problems — is a useful methodological warning for anyone building difficulty-graded physics or science benchmarks: nominal knowledge level is a weak proxy for actual task difficulty as experienced by a model.

## Verification

Verification is unusually layered for a paper at this scale. Every training and evaluation problem is code-verifiable by construction (a Python function checked against physically-meaningful test cases), removing free-form correctness judgment from the core training signal. Synthetic problem validity itself goes through a five-metric LLM quality grading pass plus mandatory independent solvability by GPT-5 (a different frontier model than the Gemini generators), explicitly reasoned as "model consensus as a proxy for ground truth" rather than a claim of certainty — and the paper is transparent about the residual error modes this does not catch (miscalibrated calculation conventions that trivialize a problem, discovered only retrospectively when they caused an unexplained accuracy jump; rare golden-code bugs). The CoT-error-classification pipeline is itself cross-validated against a second, stronger model family (Claude-Sonnet-4.6 vs. the primary gpt-oss-120b analyzer) on a 52-attempt subsample, with aggregate agreement reported and the specific source of residual disagreement (genuine boundary ambiguity between "mathematical" and "logical" error, and between "factual" and other categories, on specific worked examples) identified and explained rather than glossed over — a good-faith calibration check, albeit on a small sample (52 attempts, 7 problems) that is not claimed to generalize beyond illustrating that the two analyzers are broadly consistent. The one calibration step notably not reported is any check of the automated classification pipeline's labels against pure human expert grading, so the whole error-taxonomy analysis ultimately rests on inter-LLM agreement rather than a human ground truth.

## Field context

This paper sits at the intersection of two lines already represented in this corpus. On the benchmark/evaluation side, it uses TPBench ([reviews/25tpbench.md](25tpbench.md)) directly as an external validation and difficulty-calibration tool (finding its own Hard tier roughly matches TPBench Level 4, and reproducing TPBench's "reasoning cliff" pattern with its own baseline models), and is thematically adjacent to [reviews/2604.15411.md](2604.15411.md) (PRL-Bench) and [reviews/2604.14188.md](2604.14188.md) (Grading the Unspoken), both reviewed earlier in this batch, in treating theoretical/research-level physics as a domain uniquely suited to auto-verifiable or expert-gradable reasoning evaluation — though this paper's contribution is a training pipeline and study rather than a benchmark per se. On the reasoning-dynamics side, its backtracking/trace-length analysis is a direct empirical counterpart to the "overthinking" literature reviewed in this same batch: [reviews/2604.10739.md](2604.10739.md) ("When More Thinking Hurts") and the earlier-reviewed [reviews/2508.17627.md](2508.17627.md) both study when extended reasoning stops helping or actively hurts, while this paper shows a complementary, training-side mechanism — RL fine-tuning reallocates backtracking toward productive (eventually-correct) traces without changing its aggregate frequency, suggesting one lever by which the overthinking/underthinking balance those papers measure at inference time could in principle be shifted by training rather than by inference-time stopping rules. It also extends physics-specific agentic work such as [reviews/2604.00149.md](2604.00149.md) (Verifiable AI Physicists for quantum many-body simulations) by targeting model capability development itself (via fine-tuning) rather than agent scaffolding around a fixed frontier model.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central empirical claims are well-supported for what they are: consistent, replicated-across-methods accuracy gains from both RL and SFT on a 7B model, cross-validated difficulty calibration against an external benchmark (TPBench) and against frontier proprietary models unconnected to the data-generation pipeline, and a genuinely informative mechanistic CoT analysis with an explicit cross-model consistency check rather than a single-analyzer black box. The honesty about pipeline failure modes (the calculation-convention leak that trivialized some problems, discovered and corrected after initial RL training) is a good sign of methodological care rather than a weakness.

The main limitation, which the paper does not fully foreground, is scale of generalization: every substantive claim about RL-vs-SFT dynamics, error-type evolution, and backtracking reallocation rests on a single base model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) — the one other model attempted for RL (Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507) failed to improve and was dropped without further diagnosis beyond a hypothesis ("low capacity and extremely optimized"), leaving open whether the reported RL/SFT patterns are properties of RL and SFT generally or specific to this one base model's particular pretraining and distillation history. Statistical uncertainty is also thin throughout: most headline percentages (e.g., Table 3's accuracy deltas) are computed from k=5 rollouts per problem with no reported confidence interval or variance, and even the larger 100-rollout CoT-analysis runs (Section 8) report standard deviations only for the aggregate accuracy metric (Table 6), not for the per-category error-frequency comparisons that carry the paper's most novel claims (e.g., the "factual errors reduce first" pattern). The narrow-domain fermion/spinor experiment (Section 7) is informative but very small (35 training, 9 in-distribution validation problems), and its "no catastrophic forgetting" conclusion rests on OOD accuracy changes as small as -0.3 to -1.5 percentage points, well within what could be run-to-run noise given the reported lack of variance estimates. The generation-pipeline-bias concern the authors themselves raise (Gemini and GPT-5 involved in both generating and filtering data, and GPT-5 also serving as one of several evaluation touchpoints) is explicitly and appropriately flagged as a benchmarking-validity risk rather than swept aside, though it is not fully resolved: the paper's own baseline appendix shows Claude-Opus-4.5 (a non-generator, non-filter model family) performing competitively with or ahead of the pipeline's associated models on several tiers, which is a reasonable but not dispositive argument against systematic favoritism. What remains solidly supported: for this specific base model and this specific verifiable-QFT data pipeline, both RL and SFT reliably improve physics reasoning accuracy in- and out-of-distribution, the improvement is concentrated in eliminating factual errors first, and RL specifically reallocates (rather than reduces) self-correction behavior toward successful reasoning paths.
