Citation: Anandkumar, A. How Do We Build AI to Push the Frontiers of Scientific Discovery? Dædalus 155 (1-2) (2026): 216-228.

## What this paper is about

A researcher who helped invent Neural Operators argues that the real bottleneck in scientific discovery is not a shortage of ideas but the cost and time of validating them against physical reality, and that language models, however good they become at generating hypotheses from text, cannot close that gap on their own. Her proposed direction is AI that is physically grounded across scales, from atomic to planetary, rather than trained only on text, with Neural Operators offered as the concrete architecture that can learn continuous, resolution-independent mappings between physical function spaces instead of fixed-resolution statistical pattern matching. The essay surveys what this approach has already achieved, most prominently in weather forecasting, and what she sees as still missing.

## Extended summary

The essay opens with a "needle in a haystack" framing: an AI system that generates more candidate hypotheses without a way to know which ones will actually work in the physical world only increases the validation effort required, since experimentation, not idea generation, is the real bottleneck. It argues that "AI scientist" systems built on LLMs, sometimes with domain-specific fine-tuning, that have shown success in specific cases such as predicting chemical synthesis or drug repurposing, are a genuine but partial contribution rather than a complete answer, because textual training data alone cannot give a hypothesis physical certainty. Successes from training AI directly on domain-specific physical data are cited (discovering functional enzymes, predicting coronavirus variants, protein-ligand structure prediction, extreme-weather modeling), with the common thread being access to sufficiently large, curated datasets, and the essay names domains where such data is scarce or unavailable (extreme weather under climate change, underrepresented protein families) as a real limitation. It cites a specific, widely publicized cautionary case directly against the field's own hype: a claim that AI had discovered "2.2 million structures" that "escaped previous human chemical intuition" was later tempered by follow-up experiments showing many of these AI-proposed materials did not function as predicted, used explicitly as evidence that unverified, pattern-matching-scale discovery claims can mislead.

The essay's central technical argument is that purely data-driven AI struggles to extrapolate beyond its training distribution and tends to be overconfident and miscalibrated, so apparent successes may reflect being close enough to the training data (interpolation) or luck rather than genuine extrapolation. Its proposed remedy is integrating governing physical laws and symmetries as domain constraints alongside data, which both improves extrapolation and, crucially, provides a way to verify AI-generated discoveries against what is physically valid, potentially reducing or eliminating the need for physical prototyping. Modeling physical systems, however, requires handling processes spanning more than twelve orders of magnitude in space and time, for which standard fixed-resolution architectures such as transformers and convolutional networks are argued to be ill-suited. Neural Operators are presented as a universal framework for learning mappings between function spaces rather than fixed-size input/output arrays: they are discretization-invariant, can be trained on data at multiple resolutions and fidelities, can incorporate physics losses (such as PDE residuals) at resolutions finer than the training data, and are differentiable, which supports inverse design.

FourCastNet, described as the first high-resolution, fully AI-based global weather model, is presented as the flagship demonstration: competitive accuracy at tens of thousands of times the speed of traditional numerical weather prediction, correctly predicting hurricanes Lee and Beryl several days ahead of traditional models despite few comparable events in its training data, using only about fifty thousand training samples (curated from the forty-year ERA5 physics-informed reanalysis dataset) compared with web-scale language-model training. The essay is explicit that a few hurricane-prediction successes do not constitute a definitive scientific claim, given weather is a chaotic system fundamentally unpredictable past a certain horizon under deterministic forecasting; it describes FourCastNet3's response of generating large probabilistic ensembles and filtering out physically implausible ensemble members, reported to achieve calibrated probabilistic skill and to surpass both traditional and other AI-weather models in medium-range skill, speed, and stability up to sixty days ahead. A further reported result is that FourCastNet, despite being trained only on short (roughly six-hour) autoregressive steps, extrapolates stably to seasonal and multi-year timescales where other AI-weather models "blow up," attributed to its Spherical Fourier Neural Operator architecture's built-in rotational equivariance on the sphere, in contrast to architectures that implicitly assume a rectangular domain and accumulate distortion near the poles. The essay names real, disclosed limits: such models cannot forecast "gray swan" events entirely absent from training data, a live concern under climate change, and protein-structure predictions similarly degrade for protein families outside the training distribution, offered as evidence that purely data-driven extrapolation has a ceiling that physics-informed methods are needed to push past.

The essay then contrasts machine-learning's top-down, pattern-matching approach with the applied-math tradition's bottom-up, mechanistic PDE modeling, noting that many governing equations are analytically simple but numerically intractable at the required resolution (exactly solving Schrödinger's equation for a roughly hundred-electron molecule would take longer than the age of the universe with current numerical methods; resolving clouds in global climate models would require several orders of magnitude more compute than is currently available), which motivates approximations such as density functional theory or large-eddy simulation that require deep expertise to select and are often still too expensive. It argues Neural Operators can learn faster PDE solvers than hand-engineered numerical methods, drawing an analogy to how learned features surpassed hand-engineered ones in computer vision. It distinguishes Neural Operators from Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), which solve a single problem instance via test-time optimization against a PDE-residual loss, an approach the essay describes as often facing an intractable optimization landscape for time-varying problems and, even when it converges, typically no faster than classical numerical solvers; Neural Operators instead learn a reusable solution operator via supervised training on precomputed numerical solutions, and a "physics-informed Neural Operator" variant can additionally impose the PDE residual as a loss at a resolution finer than the training data, potentially exceeding the training data's own accuracy. The mathematical foundation is described as extending linear PDEs' Green's-function-style integral-operator solutions, learnable via graphs, Fourier transforms, or attention, to general nonlinear operators by composing learnable linear integral operators with nonlinear activations, yielding a family of architectures (Fourier, Graph, and Transformer Neural Operators) that are discretization-invariant and universal approximators of continuous nonlinear operators.

Reported applications, each attributed to a specific cited paper by the author or close collaborators, include four-to-six-orders-of-magnitude speedups over numerical solvers across domains; plasma-disruption detection for nuclear fusion; carbon-dioxide storage-reservoir modeling for climate mitigation; detecting climate "tipping points" as deviations from assumed physics; an inverse-designed medical catheter reducing bacterial contamination by two orders of magnitude, validated with only one physical experiment; faster, higher-quality inverse lithography mask design via progressive self-training; more accurate lung-ultrasound aeration assessment working directly on raw radio-frequency signals rather than subjective image evaluation; diffusion-based Neural Operators for inverse-problem priors; and reinforcement-learning-derived control policies for turbulent drag reduction. The essay also discusses combining Neural Operators with formal, symbolic verification: it cites 2025-era AI systems reaching gold-medal-level performance at the International Mathematical Olympiad using Lean, a formal proof system that checks every proposed proof step, framing this as preventing errors from accumulating catastrophically over long reasoning chains, and mentions the author's own team's LeanDojo, an open-source framework combining language models with Lean, as a contribution to this direction; it proposes combining formal verification with Neural-Operator-based physical modeling to refine PDEs and their approximations with guaranteed correctness, as a complementary rather than competing capability.

Finally, the essay compares two paradigms for physics-informed learning: end-to-end replacement of numerical solvers with Neural Operators, versus "closure modeling" (also called coarse-graining or renormalization in other domains), where a traditional coarse-grid numerical solver is augmented with a learned additive correction term. It reports a specific result from a cited co-authored paper that, counter to the intuition that reusing an existing solver should be more data-efficient, closure models actually fare worse than end-to-end Neural Operators in both data efficiency and training stability, because closure corrections are restricted to the same coarse grid as the underlying solver (an ill-posed learning target given only coarse-grid data) and cannot mix information nonlinearly across scales; a Neural Operator trained progressively, first on cheaper coarse-grid data, then fine-tuned with limited fine-grid data and physics constraints, is reported to achieve statistically optimal estimation of long-term statistics of chaotic systems, and closure-model systems are additionally described as typically non-differentiable, limiting their use for inverse design. The essay closes with a vision of a "foundation model for physics" built on Neural Operators, able to compositionally combine different physical systems, analogous to how language compositionally combines words and phrases, to simulate complex multiscale, multiphysics phenomena and generate novel designs.

## Learnings

Encoding the actual geometry and symmetry of a physical domain directly into a model's architecture, rather than only into its loss function, can be the specific difference between stable long-horizon extrapolation and catastrophic failure: a Spherical Fourier Neural Operator's built-in rotational equivariance on the sphere is credited with letting a weather model trained only on short autoregressive steps extrapolate stably to seasonal and multi-year timescales, while other architectures that implicitly assume a rectangular domain accumulate distortion near the poles and eventually blow up over long rollouts.

Augmenting an existing, trusted numerical solver with a learned correction term is not automatically the safer or more data-efficient design choice compared with replacing the solver end-to-end with a learned operator. A reported result found that closure-style corrections, restricted to the same coarse grid as the underlying solver, form an ill-posed learning target and cannot mix information nonlinearly across scales, making them less data-efficient and less stable to train than an end-to-end Neural Operator trained progressively across multiple resolutions, a counter-intuitive finding worth checking whenever "bolt a learned correction onto an existing trusted method" seems like the conservative choice.

Domain data that is small by modern AI standards, roughly fifty thousand samples for a global weather model versus web-scale text corpora, can be enough for a physically grounded model to generalize correctly to rare, high-impact events that are themselves scarce in the training data, when the underlying dataset is physics-informed and carefully curated (decades of reanalysis data combining raw observations with physical modeling) rather than simply large. This suggests physical structure and curation quality in training data can substitute for sheer volume for certain kinds of extrapolation, though the same essay is explicit that this does not extend to genuinely out-of-distribution "gray swan" events entirely absent from training data.

## Verification

Verification is a substantive and recurring theme throughout this essay, approached from several angles. The central proposal is physics-as-verifier: encoding governing laws and symmetries as constraints lets a model's proposed discovery be checked against what is physically valid, explicitly framed as a way to reduce or potentially eliminate the need for physical prototyping, a graded, physics-native verification argument. A second, more operational instance is FourCastNet3's use of large probabilistic ensembles with explicit filtering of physically implausible members, a concrete plausibility check applied at the ensemble level rather than to a single deterministic forecast. A third, categorically different mode is Lean-based formal proof verification, presented as a deterministic checker of every proof step that prevents catastrophic error accumulation over long reasoning chains, which the essay explicitly proposes combining with Neural-Operator-based physical modeling to guarantee the correctness of the PDEs and approximations used to generate training data and physics losses, a complementary rather than competing role for formal verification. Throughout, the essay treats physical experimentation itself as the ultimate but expensive verification step, framing AI's transformative potential specifically as reducing how often that expensive check is needed rather than eliminating the need for it. It also offers a cautionary counterexample against trusting discovery claims without this kind of verification: the widely publicized claim of millions of AI-discovered materials that follow-up experiments substantially failed to validate, cited as direct evidence for why unverified, pattern-matching-scale claims can mislead.

## Field context

The essay extends the physics-informed neural network literature (Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis, 2019) by contrasting PINNs' per-instance, test-time-optimization design, often facing an intractable optimization landscape and typically no faster than classical solvers even when it converges, against the Neural Operator framework the author's own group introduced (the Fourier Neural Operator at ICLR 2021, and the general Neural Operator theory published in JMLR), a supervised, trained-once, reusable, discretization-invariant approach. It positions its central argument, that textual AI alone is not enough for scientific discovery, against a wave of LLM-based "AI scientist" systems (cited examples include a fine-tuned-GPT-guided materials-design system and a multi-agent scientific-discovery system called Robin) as a genuine but partial contribution rather than a complete answer, and it engages directly with a live, contested empirical debate about AI-driven materials discovery rather than only citing successes. It connects the Neural Operator research program to the concurrent 2025-era wave of formal-mathematics theorem proving (Lean-verified AI systems reaching IMO gold-medal-level performance, and the author's own LeanDojo contribution) as a complementary capability rather than a competing one. Within this corpus, the essay is a strong counterpart to 25mcpsim's demonstration of an LLM-orchestrated agentic pipeline wrapped around a separate numerical solver, and to 25tpbench's finding that current LLMs' research-level physics performance is bottlenecked partly by unreliable symbolic execution: where those two papers work from the outside in (an LLM agent invoking or verifying against an external physics backend), this essay argues for building physical structure directly into the model's own architecture and training signal, offering a genuinely distinct design philosophy worth comparing directly against those approaches.

## Critical discussion

The essay earns real credit for citing, against its own broadly optimistic direction, a specific and well-documented failure case: the widely publicized claim of millions of AI-discovered materials that follow-up experiments substantially failed to validate, used explicitly as supporting evidence for the essay's own central argument that unverified discovery claims can mislead. Citing a failure that argues against the field's hype narrative, even when the specific failure is not the author's own system, is a genuine strength worth naming directly, especially in an advocacy-adjacent piece.

At the same time, nearly every specific positive technical claim in the essay, the Neural Operator speed and accuracy figures, FourCastNet's hurricane predictions and long-horizon stability, the catheter, lithography, and ultrasound application results, and the closure-versus-end-to-end data-efficiency comparison, is supported by citation to the author's own prior papers or her close collaborators' work, with no independent, third-party replication or comparison discussed in the essay itself. This is an expected and appropriate feature of a synthesis and position essay for a general scholarly-policy audience, and every specific claim is traceable to a named, peer-reviewed source rather than left unsourced, which is the right standard for this genre; but a reader should treat this essay as an advocacy synthesis by the technology's principal inventor rather than a neutral survey, and should follow the specific citations directly to weigh the underlying evidence rather than taking the essay's own confident framing as the final word.

Some specific claims are phrased with more categorical confidence than the underlying mechanism obviously supports as stated here. Describing Lean-verifier-checked theorem proving as ensuring "hallucination-free reasoning" is accurate in the narrow sense that an accepted Lean proof is guaranteed valid by construction, but the phrasing risks being read more broadly than that: what is guaranteed is that an accepted final proof's steps type-check, not that the search process or natural-language framing that produced candidate steps before verification filtered them was itself free of any confabulated intermediate reasoning (overclaim, in this specific, narrow sense). Similarly, the claim that physics constraints let AI discoveries be verified in a way that can "minimize or even eliminate the need for physical prototyping" is stated without qualifying how far it extends across domains with different degrees of physical-law completeness; a PDE-governed continuum system like fluid dynamics is a different verification target than a novel chemical compound's real-world synthesizability or an emergent biological behavior, where the governing "laws" are not fully known or tractable, a distinction the essay's own earlier discussion of protein-folding and materials-discovery failures implicitly supports but does not draw out at the point where the strongest version of the verification claim is made. The reported closure-versus-end-to-end result, that Neural Operators achieve "statistically optimal estimation of long-term statistics of chaotic systems," is presented as an established fact via a single cited paper without stating the precise conditions or scope of that optimality result in this essay, so a reader cannot judge from this essay alone how broadly that specific characterization should be trusted to generalize.

What remains well supported: the essay's central and least self-serving argument, that physical validation, not hypothesis generation, is the actual bottleneck in scientific discovery, and that purely textual or data-driven AI without physical grounding is unlikely to close that gap on its own, is well-argued and reinforced by a genuinely independent cautionary example rather than only by the author's own results. The description of Neural Operators' mathematical foundation, discretization-invariant operator learning, universal approximation in function spaces, and the linear-to-nonlinear-operator construction, is a clear and technically accurate account of published, peer-reviewed work, and the essay is explicit about real, named limitations of the approach (gray-swan events, out-of-distribution protein families) rather than presenting Neural Operators as a universal solution.

## Relevance to us

This essay is a first-person articulation of a design philosophy closely adjacent to our own B05 (intervention-capable physics world models) and B06 (physics-native JEPA) bets, from one of the field's most credentialed practitioners, and several of its specific points are worth engaging directly rather than only in general terms. Its central diagnosis, that experimentation and validation rather than idea generation is science's bottleneck, is an independently argued confirmation of our own emphasis on verification (B12) over pure hypothesis-generation throughput, and its proposed remedy, physics-grounded models verifiable against governing laws to reduce reliance on physical prototyping, is effectively a graded physics-native verification stack built into a model's training and architecture rather than added as a separate post-hoc checking layer; this is a genuinely different architectural bet from our own PVRE-line design (checking a model's output after the fact with an external, deterministic verifier) and worth comparing directly, since the essay's own reported closure-versus-end-to-end result, that bolting a learned correction onto an existing trusted method was less data-efficient and stable than training an end-to-end operator, is a specific, relevant data point for whether we should build verification-awareness as a separate bolt-on module or train it more deeply into a model itself. The Spherical Fourier Neural Operator finding, that encoding a physical domain's actual geometry and symmetry into the architecture, not just the loss, prevented long-horizon blowup, is a concrete, transferable design principle directly relevant to any physics world-model component we build, and worth studying in the underlying cited papers rather than only at the level of this essay's summary. The explicit distinction between PINNs (per-instance, test-time optimization, often not faster than classical solvers) and Neural Operators (trained once, reusable, discretization-invariant) is a useful technical clarification to apply whenever a future proposal in our own work reaches for the generic label "physics-informed neural network" without specifying which of these two substantially different design patterns is actually meant. The essay's framing of formal theorem-proving as complementary to physically grounded modeling, specifically proposing Lean-style formal verification to guarantee the correctness of the PDEs and approximations used to generate training data and physics losses for a learned model, is a genuinely specific and actionable idea worth considering for our own training-data and physics-loss construction pipeline, distinct from using formal verification only for end-user-facing proof tasks. Given the essay's advocacy framing, any specific quantitative claim we might want to build on, the closure-versus-end-to-end optimality result or the specific speedup figures, should be verified against the underlying cited technical papers directly rather than taken from this synthesis essay alone.
