Citation: Yu, X., Zhang, Y., Zhang, Y., Cui, Z. Grading the Unspoken: Evaluating Tacit Reasoning in Quantum Field Theory and String Theory with LLMs. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14188v1

## What this paper is about

Working physicists in quantum field theory and string theory routinely skip steps in their arguments because the community treats them as self-evident — this is "tacit knowledge," never written down but essential to genuine understanding. Standard benchmarks that just check a final answer cannot tell whether a model actually reconstructed this missing reasoning or merely produced the right conclusion some other way. The authors build a small, expert-curated set of twelve hard QFT/string-theory questions specifically designed so the omitted steps matter, grade model responses on a five-level rubric that separates surface correctness from deeper reconstruction, and find that current LLMs are strong at explicit derivation but collapse specifically on tasks requiring them to reconstruct suppressed reasoning or shift their conceptual framing.

## Extended summary

The dataset has twelve questions spanning field-theory foundations, symmetry/topology, conformal field theory, supersymmetry, and string dualities/D-branes (Table 1; full text in Appendix A), each targeting a widely-cited result whose derivation is conventionally compressed in textbooks and papers. The authors additionally place the twelve questions on a 2×2 "reasoning geometry": mode of inference (mechanism-driven, i.e. expanding local derivations, vs. consistency-driven, i.e. enforcing global constraints) crossed with conceptual organization (single-frame vs. cross-frame, requiring a representational shift before derivation can proceed), yielding four regimes — Local Derivation, Integration, Constraint-based, and Conceptual Hinge — and they position prior CoT/ToT/self-consistency-style reasoning methods within this same 2×2 space (Fig. 1, Appendix B) as an interpretive frame, not as baselines that are actually run.

The five-level rubric per question: L0 statement correctness (final claim is right); L1 key concept awareness (right concepts named); L2 reasoning chain presence (concepts linked into a causal argument, vs. just listed); L3 tacit step reconstruction (the conventionally-omitted step is explicitly supplied); L4 enrichment (bonus — validity regime, alternative perspectives, examples). Each level is illustrated with worked success/failure transcripts from specific models on specific questions (Section 3, Appendix C).

Ten models are evaluated (Gemini-2.5-Flash, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, GPT-5.2, GPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3.2 non-thinking and thinking, Kimi-K2-thinking, Qwen3.5-397B, Minimax-M2.7, Nemotron-3-Super) with average success rate per level over the 12 questions (Table 2). All models are near-ceiling at L0–L2 (statement, concept, reasoning-chain presence). A sharp, near-universal drop appears at L3 (tacit step reconstruction): Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview holds up (~0.92) while most other models fall to 0.17–0.50. Breaking this down by the four reasoning regimes (Table 3, Fig. 2): performance is consistently higher on mechanism-driven regimes (Local Derivation, Integration) and degrades most severely on Conceptual Hinge tasks (consistency-driven + cross-frame), where several models score near zero. A targeted case study on Q11 (an apparent contradiction about symmetry anomalies that requires distinguishing two different technical senses of "anomaly") perturbs the prompt with explicit hints pointing at the needed distinction (Versions B/C), without adding new technical content; nearly every model improves substantially and monotonically under the hint (e.g., Qwen3.5-397B: 0→4 out of 5 cumulative levels), while remaining sensitive to exact phrasing between the two hinted variants. The authors interpret this as evidence that the bottleneck is representation/framing selection, not missing domain knowledge — the capability exists but is not reliably self-triggered. No code, data-release statement, funding acknowledgment, or competing-interest statement is given in the extracted text.

## Learnings

The core reusable idea — a graded, level-structured rubric that separates "knows the concept," "has a reasoning chain," and "reconstructed the specific step that domain convention leaves unstated" — is a transferable evaluation pattern for any expert domain (not just theoretical physics) where practitioners systematically omit steps for a knowledgeable audience; it directly targets a blind spot that step-level PRM-style verifiers (which only judge stated steps) structurally cannot see, since a missing step is not a wrong step.

The reasoning-geometry framing (mechanism- vs. consistency-driven crossed with single- vs. cross-frame) is a useful lens even independent of physics: it predicts, and the data confirm, that current LLM failures concentrate specifically where a model must first recognize that its initial representation of the problem is wrong and switch frames, rather than where it must simply reason longer or more carefully within a frame it has already correctly set up. The Q11 prompt-perturbation result — that naming the needed distinction (without adding facts) unlocks capability that a differently-phrased identical-content prompt does not — is a compact, generalizable demonstration that some "reasoning failures" in LLMs are triggering/framing failures rather than missing-knowledge or missing-computation failures.

## Verification

The scoring itself is not black-box: each level is defined with explicit criteria per question (Appendix C.2) and illustrated with matched success/failure transcript pairs, which gives readers a way to audit the grading logic directly rather than trusting an aggregate number. However, the paper does not report who applied the rubric (the four authors themselves, an external annotator, or an LLM judge), how many graders scored each response, or any inter-rater agreement statistic — for a five-level rubric built specifically to distinguish subtle, human-judgment-dependent notions like "reasoning chain presence" versus "tacit step reconstruction," this is a meaningful gap, since the paper's central quantitative claims (the L2→L3 cliff, the regime-based degradation pattern) rest entirely on this scoring. The Q11 prompt-perturbation experiment is a genuine, if small (one question, three prompt variants), controlled manipulation that supports its specific causal claim about framing versus knowledge.

## Field context

This paper sits alongside recent research-level physics benchmarks it directly cites and distinguishes itself from: [reviews/25tpbench.md](25tpbench.md) (TPBench), which uses automatically code-checkable answers across undergraduate-to-research-level high-energy theory and cosmology problems, and [reviews/2507.04766.md](2507.04766.md) (ABench-Physics), which uses parametrically-varied numerical-answer problems to test whether correct answers generalize across problem restatements. Both of those benchmarks, like most prior physics-reasoning benchmarks, still require a single canonical correct final answer; this paper's explicit contribution, which it states directly, is that QFT/string-theory questions of the kind it curates often do not have that property — correctness is layered and the interesting evaluative signal is in the reconstructed reasoning, not the final claim — positioning it as a complementary, process-focused counterpart rather than a competing leaderboard. Within the reasoning-elicitation literature (Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thoughts, Self-Consistency, and related methods it surveys in Appendix B), this paper's contribution is diagnostic rather than a new elicitation method: it shows where those methods' implicit target (extending or verifying an already-well-framed derivation) breaks down, at the point where the frame itself must be changed.

## Critical discussion

The five-level rubric and the sharp L2→L3 stratification it reveals is a genuinely informative, well-illustrated result, and the reasoning-regime breakdown (mechanism- vs. consistency-driven, single- vs. cross-frame) gives a specific, falsifiable account of where the difficulty concentrates rather than a vague "models struggle with hard physics" claim. The Q11 perturbation experiment is a real controlled test of the framing-vs-knowledge hypothesis, and its result (near-monotonic improvement under hinting, but instability between hint phrasings) is reported honestly, including the caveat that the effect is not robustly triggered.

The chief limitation is scale and its consequences for how much weight the numbers should bear: twelve questions, one grading pass with no reported grader count or agreement statistic, and confidence bands are never given for any table despite Table 2's differences between adjacent models (e.g., 2.917 vs. 3.000, or 2.084 vs. 2.417) being reported to three decimal places on a 12-question, 5-level scale where such precision implies far more statistical resolution than a sample this small can support. The reasoning-geometry categorization of the twelve questions into four regimes (with three or four questions per regime, and explicit overlap across the topical categories in Table 1) is itself the authors' own construction; it is a reasonable and clearly motivated taxonomy, but Table 3's regime-level comparisons are consequently built on very small per-cell sample sizes (as few as two questions for "Conceptual hinge"), which the paper does not flag as a precision concern despite drawing a headline claim ("the most severe drop occurs in Conceptual Hinge tasks") from it. No baseline beyond the ten evaluated LLMs is used — there is no non-LLM reference point (e.g., human graduate-student performance on the same twelve questions) to calibrate how hard the tacit-reconstruction task actually is for a competent human non-expert, which would help distinguish "LLMs fail here" from "this is simply very hard for anyone without the specific tacit background." What remains solidly supported: within this specific twelve-question set and this rubric, there is a clear, qualitatively large and reproducible-looking gap between models' ability to state correct conclusions and their ability to reconstruct the specific intermediate reasoning conventionally omitted in the literature, concentrated in tasks that require a representational reframing rather than pure derivation-extension.
