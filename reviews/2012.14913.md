Citation: Geva, M., Schuster, R., Berant, J., Levy, O. Transformer Feed-Forward Layers Are Key-Value Memories. EMNLP 2021. arXiv:2012.14913.

## What this paper is about

Feed-forward sublayers make up two-thirds of a transformer language model's parameters, yet get far less analytical attention than self-attention. This paper argues, and provides direct evidence, that each feed-forward layer functions as a key-value memory: its first weight matrix acts as a bank of pattern detectors ("keys") that fire on specific recurring textual patterns, and its second weight matrix ("values") stores, for each such pattern, a distribution over which word is likely to come next. The paper shows these patterns are human-recognizable, get more abstract/semantic in upper layers and more surface-level in lower layers, and that the model's final prediction is built by combining hundreds of these small memories within each layer, then successively refining that combined guess layer by layer via the residual stream.

## Extended summary

The paper starts from an exact mathematical parallel: a feed-forward layer FF(x) = f(x·K^T)·V is almost identical to Sukhbaatar et al.'s (2015) key-value neural memory formulation, differing only in that the canonical transformer's non-linearity f is not a normalizing softmax. The paper's contribution is not the (previously noted) formal parallel, but empirically characterizing what the keys and values of a real, trained model actually store. All experiments use Baevski and Auli's (2019) 16-layer adaptive-input WikiText-103 language model (d=1024, dm=4096, 247M parameters), which has 65,536 total keys (4096 per layer x 16 layers).

Section 3 tests whether keys detect human-recognizable patterns. For 160 randomly sampled keys (10 per layer), the authors retrieve each key's top trigger examples (the training-set sentence prefixes whose hidden representation gives the highest inner product with that key) and have one NLP graduate-student annotator per key identify recurring patterns among the top-25 retrieved prefixes, classifying each as "shallow" (e.g., a repeated n-gram or shared final word) or "semantic" (a repeated topic without surface-form similarity). Annotators found at least one pattern for every key (average 3.6 patterns per key), and 65-80% of retrieved prefixes were covered by an identified pattern. Lower layers (1-9) were dominated by shallow patterns; upper layers (10-16) shifted toward semantic patterns, consistent with prior findings about lower/upper-layer specialization in contextualized models (cited: Peters et al. 2018, Jawahar et al. 2019, Liu et al. 2019). A follow-up perturbation experiment (1,600 keys, 100 per layer) removing the first, last, or a random token from trigger examples found that removing the last token affects the memory coefficient less in upper layers, supporting the shallow-to-semantic shift since shallow (often last-word) patterns matter less once removed from consideration in upper layers.

Section 4 tests whether values encode a next-token distribution matching their key's pattern. Projecting each value vector through the output embedding matrix (with adaptive softmax) yields a vocabulary distribution per value; the paper measures the agreement rate between a value's top-ranked token and the actual next token following its key's top-1 trigger example. Agreement is near zero for layers 1-10 but rises to about 3.5% from layer 11 onward, which the paper notes is orders of magnitude above a random baseline (~0.0004%), indicating real (if modest in absolute terms) predictive alignment concentrated in upper layers. Isolating the 100 highest-confidence values across the whole model (value's own top-prediction probability), 97 were in upper layers (11-16), and for 46 of these 100, at least one of the key's top-50 trigger examples matched the value's top prediction exactly (examples given in Table 2, with per-example precision@50 ranging from 4% to 92%). The paper explicitly flags this as a partial, not universal, correlation and notes it cannot fully explain why lower layers show weaker key-value alignment, offering the (untested) conjecture that lower layers may not yet operate in the same embedding space as the output vocabulary.

Section 5 studies how the model aggregates many memories into one prediction. Analyzing 4,000 random validation-set prefixes, a typical example activates 10-50% of a layer's 4096 memory cells (hundreds of active memories), with the number of active cells dropping around layer 10, coinciding with the shallow-to-semantic transition point identified in Section 3. Despite this potential compositionality, in about 68% of examples at any given layer, the layer's actual top prediction differs from every one of its individual memories' own top predictions, indicating genuine, non-trivial composition rather than one dominant cell driving the output; the cases where a single memory's prediction does survive intact skew heavily toward common stop words (60% of cases) and short prefixes (43% under 5 tokens), suggesting very frequent patterns may be "cached" directly in single cells without needing composition. Section 5.2 turns to the residual stream across layers: about a third of the model's final predictions are already fixed by the lower layers' residual value, a fraction that rises quickly from layer 10 onward (again aligning with the semantic-pattern transition), and both the identity and the confidence of the top prediction increase steadily through the layers (Figures 9-10). Breaking down how each layer changes the residual's running prediction (Figure 11), the paper finds that when a feed-forward layer does change the residual's top prediction, the new prediction is rarely the feed-forward layer's own top prediction; more often it is a "compromise" equal to neither the residual's nor the feed-forward layer's individual top choice, which the authors interpret as the feed-forward layer acting more like a veto/elimination mechanism on the residual's leading candidate than a direct override. A manual review of 100 cases where the very last layer changed the prediction found 66 shifted to a semantically distant word (e.g., "people" to "same") and 34 shifted to a closely related word (e.g., "later" to "earlier," "gastric" to "stomach"), suggesting fine-grained, varying-granularity tuning even at the final layer.

## Learnings

The paper's central, most transferable idea is treating a weight matrix pair not as an opaque linear transformation but as an explicit, addressable lookup table (keys as pattern detectors, values as output-distribution contributions), and then validating that framing empirically with human annotation rather than only asserting the mathematical analogy. This "which parameter matrix is the address and which is the content" framing has since become foundational to a substantial line of later work on locating and editing factual knowledge inside transformer feed-forward layers, and the paper's own trigger-example-retrieval-plus-human-annotation method (rather than only automatic proxy metrics) is a generalizable recipe for characterizing what any bank of learned "detector" units has actually specialized on.

A second, quieter but important finding: the model's residual stream does not simply accumulate a monotonically strengthening signal toward the final answer. When a feed-forward layer overrides the residual's leading prediction, it usually does not substitute its own top prediction directly, but produces a genuine three-way "compromise" distinct from either input, a compositional pattern that recurs both within a single layer (across its hundreds of active memories) and across layers (via the residual). Any mental model of "each layer either keeps or replaces the running answer" is too simple; the more accurate picture, at least for this model and task, is closer to competing candidates being reweighted rather than swapped.

## Verification

This paper is itself a verification exercise applied to a mechanistic claim (feed-forward layers behave as key-value memories) rather than a paper about verifying model outputs. Its central "verification" instrument is human expert annotation: NLP graduate students manually inspected the top-25 trigger examples per sampled key and identified recognizable patterns, a subjective but explicit and reported procedure; the paper requires each identified pattern to be grounded in at least 3 of the 25 examples specifically to guard against spurious pattern-finding in what could otherwise be a small, cherry-picked set. However, the paper reports only single-annotator labels per key (one expert per key, not multiple experts checked against each other), so no inter-annotator agreement or reliability check is reported for what is inherently a subjective judgment task (deciding whether a "pattern" is present and whether it counts as shallow or semantic), which is a real methodological limitation given how central this specific human-judgment step is to the paper's headline claim. Elsewhere, the paper favors quantitative, falsifiable checks over the annotation step: the value-key agreement rate is compared explicitly against a random baseline to establish non-triviality, and the token-removal perturbation experiment (Figure 3) is a genuine ablation-style test of the "shallow patterns are surface-level" hypothesis rather than an assertion. The composition analysis in Section 5 is also self-verifying in a useful sense: rather than assuming compositionality, the paper explicitly checks whether the layer's actual prediction differs from every one of its component memories' own predictions, and reports the 68% figure as the direct evidence for genuine (not single-cell-dominated) composition.

## Field context

This paper sits early in what became a substantial mechanistic-interpretability research program on transformer internals, following a wave of self-attention-focused analysis (Voita et al. 2019, Clark et al. 2019, Vig and Belinkov 2019) and inter-layer probing work (Tenney et al. 2019 "BERT rediscovers the classical NLP pipeline," Jawahar et al. 2019) that the paper explicitly cites and extends to the previously under-examined feed-forward sublayer, which the paper notes holds two-thirds of a transformer's parameters. It builds directly on the formal key-value-memory parallel proposed by Sukhbaatar et al. (2015, 2019) but goes beyond that prior work's architectural reparameterization to ask what the existing, canonical transformer's feed-forward memories actually store, which the paper frames as the genuinely new contribution. The paper's own discussion section explicitly flags unresolved questions (whether the same qualitative findings hold for encoder or translation models, whether embedding-space consistency changes across layers) as future work rather than settled claims. Within this corpus, its closest relative is [26nla]'s Natural Language Autoencoder, which also aims to expose what a model's internal computation represents in human-readable terms, but the two take structurally different approaches: this 2020 paper hand-annotates trigger examples for a fixed, architecturally-motivated unit of analysis (a single feed-forward key-value pair) to build a mechanistic account validated by human judgment, while 26nla trains a separate model to auto-generate natural-language descriptions of arbitrary activation vectors and validates itself via reconstruction accuracy rather than human pattern-matching; comparing the two is a useful illustration of how interpretability methodology has shifted from manually-annotated, architecture-specific case studies toward learned, general-purpose translation of internal state into language.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claims are supported by converging, independently-designed evidence rather than a single measurement: the human pattern-annotation result (Section 3), the value-projection agreement-rate result (Section 4), and the composition/refinement analysis (Section 5) each probe a different part of the key-value-memory hypothesis and each returns results consistent with it, which is a meaningfully stronger evidentiary structure than any one experiment alone would provide. The paper is also commendably explicit about the limits of its own evidence: it states plainly that lower layers show weak key-value agreement without a settled explanation, offers its embedding-space conjecture as exactly that, a conjecture, and frames generalization beyond this one language-modeling checkpoint as an open question left to future work rather than assuming it.

The clearest methodological gap is the single-annotator pattern-identification step underlying the Section 3 headline claim (every key has at least one human-recognizable pattern, 3.6 patterns per key on average): with one expert labeling each key's top-25 examples and no inter-annotator agreement reported, the paper cannot rule out that some fraction of "identified patterns" reflects one annotator's willingness to find structure in noisy, ambiguous text rather than a genuinely shared, recognizable regularity; a second annotator disagreeing on borderline cases would not necessarily overturn the qualitative conclusion but could meaningfully change the precise coverage numbers (65-80% of prefixes associated with a pattern) that the paper reports as if fully reliable. A second, smaller point: all experiments use one specific 247M-parameter, WikiText-103-trained language model; the paper's own "Beyond language modeling" discussion section is honest that generalization to encoder models (BERT-style) or translation models is an expectation, not a demonstrated result, so readers should not extend the specific quantitative figures (the 3.5% agreement rate, the ~68% composition rate, the layer-10 transition point) to other architectures or training regimes without independent verification. The agreement-rate finding (Section 4) is real and above chance by a wide margin, but the paper is transparent that the absolute magnitude (3.5% at best) is still small in absolute terms; describing this as showing "non-trivial predictive power" is an accurate, appropriately hedged characterization rather than an overclaim, since the paper does not claim values fully determine outputs, only that they correlate with next-token identity more than chance would predict.

What remains well supported: feed-forward layers in this model detect a broad range of human-identifiable input patterns that shift from shallow to semantic across depth, their value vectors carry a genuine, non-random correlation with the next-token distribution of the patterns their paired keys detect (strongest in upper layers), and the model's final output is constructed through real multi-cell, multi-layer composition rather than any single dominant memory driving the answer, a specific and mechanistically informative account of feed-forward layer function that later work in model editing and knowledge localization has built directly upon.
