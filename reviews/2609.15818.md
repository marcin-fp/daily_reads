Citation: Atria Team (Honglin Guo, Tao Gui, et al.). Atria Dawn: The Dawn of Agentic Superintelligence. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15818v1

## What this paper is about

This report introduces Atria Dawn Preview, a 744-billion-parameter agentic foundation model built for research and engineering work and trained by grounding every training task in a real, externally-verified execution environment. Beyond the model release, the paper's more distinctive contribution is a self-study of the team's own development process: a structured analysis of 769 recorded work tasks from 56 participants, examining exactly which parts of building this AI system were done by AI agents versus humans, and using that record to argue that current agentic AI has become a genuine research partner but that human judgment remains the scarce, load-bearing resource in AI-assisted AI development.

## Extended summary

**Model and training.** Atria Dawn is built on a 744B-parameter mixture-of-experts base model (cited as a Z.ai GLM-5.2 model card in the references) and trained via a "Verifiable Experience Pipeline": every training task is tied to a real execution environment where the model observes state, calls tools, produces artifacts, and receives feedback, and only trajectories whose final outcomes are checked against external signals (executable tests, experiment metrics, file/application state, geometric checks, source-evidence checks, or human-defined criteria) are curated into training data. Failure analysis (recurring problems like ineffective tool selection, incomplete verification, or unsuccessful recovery) feeds back into constructing new tasks and refining environments.

**Benchmark results.** Across 16 benchmarks spanning tool use, deep search, workspace productivity, professional deliverables, software engineering, terminal tasks, ML engineering, and cybersecurity (Table 1, scores as published on the release website), Atria Dawn attains the highest reported score on 5 (AutomationBench 53.8, BFCL v4 77.0, DeepSearchQA 96.0, BrowseComp 92.5, CyberGym 86.5) and second-highest on 3 more, with margins on the wins ranging from close (0.3-1.9 points behind the leader on three near-misses) to substantial (4.1 points on AutomationBench, 2.9 on BFCL v4, 2.0 on CyberGym). It trails noticeably on several coding/professional benchmarks: SWE-bench Pro (59.6 vs. Claude Opus 5's 74.7), JobBench (50.3 vs. 68.0), Terminal-Bench 2.1 (78.3 vs. 90.2), and GDPval (1583 vs. 1768), all led by Claude Opus 5. Selected qualitative case studies (weather-forecasting model training, GDN decode-latency optimization, an OS-from-scratch build with persistence verified across QEMU reboots, CAD assemblies, professional report generation, and an authorized cybersecurity repair-and-reverify task) illustrate tool-mediated iteration but are explicitly presented as demonstrations, not success-rate estimates.

**The human-AI collaboration case study.** The paper analyzes 769 task records and accompanying agent logs from 56 participants who worked on Atria Dawn's own development. AI was used in 96.5% of the 739 tasks with a clear response on AI involvement. The median ratio of agent actions to human prompts, tracked daily for the same 22-participant cohort over four weeks, rose from 11.0 to 28.5, with the spread across participants widening over the same period. Delegated work concentrated in execution and reporting (run-and-evaluate cycles, debugging, data preparation, resource scaling, summarizing changes) rather than in deciding what to study.

Three specific analyses probe what this delegation actually means. First, feasibility: among 455 completed AI-assisted tasks with a usable response, 151 (33.2%), drawn from 27 of the 56 participants, were rated by their own executor as infeasible without AI under matched scope and resources, distinguishing tasks AI made possible from tasks AI merely sped up. Second, decision attribution: separating who proposed an option from who selected it across goals/scope, methods/parameters, and acceptance-criteria decisions, "AI proposes, human selects" was the single most common pattern for methods/parameters (55.4%), but humans made the final choice in 85.5% of method/parameter decisions overall, 93.4% for goals/scope, and 81.9% for acceptance criteria; AI's proposal share varied more than threefold across decision types (16.9% to 55.4%) while its final-decision share stayed narrow (6.1% to 9.2%). On the infeasible-without-AI subset specifically, humans still selected the final goal in 95.4% of cases. Third, feedback and revision: among 588 tasks with a recorded difficulty, 76.0% moved forward via human intervention, 23.0% were resolved by the agent alone, and only 1.0% were left unresolved; of the human-intervention modes, informational help (adding context, or diagnosing/redirecting the approach) accounted for about 70% combined, while direct human editing or takeover of the work itself was rare (3.9% combined). Output-revision data followed the same pattern: of 627 tasks with a known output disposition, 56.5% received substantive revision, and within that subset the AI (not a human) performed 75.4% of the revisions, typically after human feedback.

**Open challenges.** The paper frames its findings through a three-stage account of evolving human-AI roles in AI R&D (research object, task-level runner, project-level coworker) and poses four open challenges toward sustained recursive self-improvement (RSI): generating and valuing diverse research directions before results exist (observing that in their own project, agents mostly proposed variations within a direction a human had already fixed, and new pipelines still originated from human prompting); converting accumulated experience into durable research capability rather than session-bound context (the paper notes agents repeatedly needed the same diagnosis supplied again in later tasks, while the human who supplied it carried the lesson forward); maintaining meaningful human oversight as the volume of agent-generated work grows faster than any one person can inspect; and establishing authority and safety protocols for autonomous execution modes, noting that in practice broad execution permissions (Codex's "yolo mode," Claude Code's "skip-permissions mode") were adopted for convenience rather than deliberate authority allocation.

## Learnings

The paper's central methodological move, decomposing "AI did X% of the work" into who proposed an option versus who selected among proposals, and further into which specific project element (goals/scope, methods/parameters, acceptance criteria) the decision concerns, is a reusable template for characterizing human-AI collaboration with more granularity than a single delegation percentage: the same underlying data (AI does most of the proposing for method/parameter decisions but a small fraction of the proposing for goals/scope, while final selection stays overwhelmingly human across all three) would be substantially flattened and less informative if collapsed into one aggregate "AI autonomy" number.

Separating "hours saved" from "work made possible" via a direct feasibility question (would this task have been completed at all without AI, holding scope and resources fixed) is a specific, transferable survey design for measuring AI impact that avoids a common blind spot in productivity studies that only measure time saved on tasks that would have happened anyway; the paper's finding that a third of completed tasks were rated infeasible without AI, spread across roughly half the participant cohort rather than concentrated in a few heavy users, is a concrete illustration of why this distinction matters empirically, not just conceptually.

The observation that a rising ratio of agent actions to human prompts should not, by itself, be read as rising AI autonomy, because each additional human decision now propagates through more agent-executed actions rather than being replaced by fewer human decisions, is a specific, useful corrective against a common but potentially misleading way of operationalizing "autonomy" purely through action-count ratios without checking who actually retains decision authority over those actions.

## Verification

The model's own training pipeline is explicitly verification-centered: the paper states that only experience linking a task to its trajectory, artifacts, and independently-checked verification evidence (executable tests, experiment metrics, file/application state, geometric checks, source support, or human-defined criteria) is incorporated into training, and that trajectory curation removes incomplete, contradictory, duplicate, or behaviorally invalid examples. The benchmark results themselves, however, largely rely on LLM-judge scoring for several of the 16 evaluations (Appendix B): DeepResearch Bench II is scored by GPT-5.5 averaged over three passes; Workspace-Bench and Workspace-Bench-Lite use Doubao Seed 2.0 Lite as judge; GDPval uses GLM-5.3 for pairwise comparison and rubric scoring; JobBench uses Grok-4.3. None of these judges are disclosed as independently validated against human ratings in the extracted text, and several use a different model family than any of the models being compared, which at least avoids same-family judge bias but does not establish the judges' own reliability. The human-AI collaboration case study's verification is procedural rather than statistical: task records and agent logs are the primary data source, decision attribution is based on participant self-report during task review rather than independent, blinded coding, and no inter-rater reliability check (comparable to a second reviewer or coder cross-checking a sample of attributions) is reported for how tasks were classified into proposal/selection or difficulty-resolution categories.

## Field context

Atria Dawn sits alongside a small but growing set of reports (the paper cites Anthropic's and OpenAI's own recent internal accounts of AI-assisted research acceleration) that use a lab's own model-development process as an empirical case study of human-AI collaboration in AI R&D, rather than only reporting benchmark scores for the resulting model. Within this same batch, "[ZGCM-1](2609.13356.md)" (Zhongguancun Academy) reports a structurally similar but far less granular self-study: nine core contributors rated their team's AI-agent autonomy across 11 R&D task categories on a five-level ordinal rubric (99 total ratings), finding operational tasks (experimentation, deployment) reach higher autonomy than architecture or learning-algorithm design. Atria Dawn's analysis is methodologically more detailed on the same underlying question, using 769 logged task records from 56 participants rather than a smaller group's retrospective self-ratings, and specifically separates proposal from selection and tracks feedback/revision patterns rather than assigning a single autonomy level per task category; both papers independently converge on the same qualitative pattern (agents handle more of the execution and even more of the option-generation, while humans concentrate on selection, evaluation, and higher-level judgment), which is a genuine, non-coincidental corroboration given the two papers were produced independently by different teams studying their own separate model-development efforts around the same time. The report's speculative discussion of recursive self-improvement pathways also connects to "[Artificial General Intelligence, Preparedness, and the Path to Recursive Self-Improvement](2606.12683.md)" (reviewed earlier in this corpus), which lays out sociogenic (division-of-labor), memetic (self-generated training data), genotypic, and hardware-level RSI mechanisms as an open forecasting question without direct empirical grounding; Atria Dawn's task-record study is a concrete, present-tense data point specifically relevant to that report's sociogenic pathway and to its "research gets harder" bottleneck discussion, showing that even a heavily agent-assisted real project currently bottlenecks on exactly the two capabilities that report flags as unresolved (generating genuinely novel research directions rather than variations on a human-set direction, and converting session-bound agent experience into durable capability).

## Critical discussion

The human-AI collaboration case study is the paper's most rigorous contribution: it draws on a real, sizable dataset (769 task records, 56 participants, four weeks of tracked agent-action ratios) rather than a handful of anecdotes, uses a genuinely informative decomposition (proposal vs. selection, across three distinct decision types) instead of one aggregate delegation number, and directly tests the feasibility-vs-speedup distinction with a targeted survey question rather than assuming higher agent-action counts imply reduced human necessity. The finding that AI's proposal share swings more than threefold across decision types while its final-decision share stays narrow throughout is a specific, well-evidenced pattern rather than a single top-line percentage that could hide this structure.

Some caution is warranted on both halves of the paper. On the model-evaluation side, several of the 16 benchmarks are scored by an LLM judge (DeepResearch Bench II, Workspace-Bench/Lite, GDPval, JobBench) with no reported validation of that judge's reliability against human raters, and Table 1's benchmark scores are described as "reported on the official release website" rather than independently reproduced or audited by this review, so the headline "highest reported score on five of them" framing rests on evaluation protocols and scoring the paper documents in an appendix but does not independently corroborate through, for instance, a third-party leaderboard (`missing-control`). Given the report's own framing emphasizes leading results, it is also worth noting plainly that Atria Dawn trails the strongest comparison model (Claude Opus 5) by large margins on several of the remaining benchmarks (SWE-bench Pro, JobBench, Terminal-Bench 2.1, GDPval), a genuinely mixed picture that the paper's Table 1 discloses in full but that the surrounding prose foregrounds less than the wins. On the collaboration case study, the underlying data (task classification into proposal/selection categories, difficulty resolution modes, and infeasibility ratings) comes from participant self-report during task review rather than an independently coded or inter-rater-checked process, so some of the finer distinctions (for instance, exactly what counts as "AI proposes, human selects" versus "human proposes and selects") may carry participant-level interpretation variance that the paper does not quantify (`incomplete-reporting`, since no coding-reliability check is reported for what is otherwise a carefully designed survey instrument). The paper is itself an interested party in both halves of its own claims (evaluating its own model against competitors, and characterizing its own team's AI usage as evidence for a broader RSI narrative), which does not invalidate the findings but is a relevant context for how confidently the framing (rather than the underlying numbers) should be read.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the specific, decomposed finding that AI's role in this project varied sharply by decision type (heavy proposal involvement in methods/parameters, much lighter involvement in setting goals/scope, and consistently light involvement in final decisions across all types) is backed by a real, sizable, granular dataset rather than an aggregate impression; the feasibility-vs-speedup distinction is directly measured rather than assumed; and the paper's own self-identified open challenges (shared research-direction homogeneity across agents, session-bound rather than accumulated experience, growing oversight burden, and ad hoc rather than deliberate authority allocation) are each grounded in a specific observation from the reported data rather than asserted as generic concerns.

## Relevance to us

The paper's task-record methodology (decomposing delegation into proposal versus selection, tracked across distinct project-decision types, plus a direct feasibility-vs-speedup survey question) is a concrete, reusable instrument for characterizing human-AI collaboration in a research setting, directly applicable to how we might evaluate or design our own collaborator/co-scientist tooling: it operationalizes exactly the distinction our own framing already emphasizes, that validation and pushback matter more than autonomous solving, and that intent and decision authority should be treated as inspectable objects rather than inferred from raw action counts. The specific finding that agents mostly generate variations within a direction a human has already fixed, rather than genuinely novel research directions, and that agents' session-bound experience does not currently accumulate into durable capability the way a human researcher's does, are both concrete, empirically-grounded data points relevant to our own problem-formulation and long-lived-session arcs (agents proposing testable programs from a hunch; multi-day DAG memory that survives across sessions rather than resetting), and they corroborate rather than merely assert the specific gaps those arcs are meant to address. As a physics-irrelevant systems and organizational case study, the paper says nothing about domain-specific scientific reasoning, but its core measurement approach and its concrete characterization of current-frontier collaboration limits are transferable independent of that.
