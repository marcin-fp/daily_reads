Citation: Subramanyam, A., Chen, Y., and Grossman, R. L. Scaling Laws Revisited: Modeling the Role of Data Quality in Language Model Pretraining. arXiv preprint arXiv:2510.03313, 2025.

## What this paper is about

Classic scaling laws for language models (most famously the Chinchilla law) predict pretraining loss from model size and the amount of training data, treating all tokens as equally useful. This paper asks what happens if the data itself is not uniformly clean, and proposes folding a single new number, a "data quality" parameter Q between 0 and 1, directly into the scaling-law formula, so that loss is predicted as a joint function of model size, data volume, and quality. The authors derive this extended law from a general "effective sample size" argument (corrupted or redundant data is worth fewer effective training examples than its raw token count suggests) and test it by deliberately injecting controlled amounts of synthetic noise into two different training setups (machine translation and next-token prediction), fitting the same kind of power-law curve Chinchilla used, and checking whether the resulting fit predicts loss on held-out data and different noise levels.

## Extended summary

The paper first proposes two ways to turn "data quality" into a single scalar Q in (0, 1]: a simple corruption rate (Q = 1 minus the fraction of corrupted tokens or samples), and a more general "data deficiency" measure ∆ satisfying positivity, continuity, and additivity across independent datasets, illustrated with an additive-noise model where each observation is a clean value plus a nonnegative noise term. It then derives, from an "effective sample size" assumption (that D noisy samples carry the same statistical information as some smaller number of clean samples, D_eff = D·g(Q)), two worked special cases: under i.i.d. additive Gaussian noise, effective sample size scales with the signal-to-noise ratio, giving an exponent γ≈1 (Lemma 1); under symmetric random classification-label noise with flip rate η, the variance-inflation factor from standard noise-corrected loss estimators gives γ≈2 (Lemma 2). Both are shown, via a regular-variation argument, to justify approximating the quality term's effect on effective data as a power law Q^γ, yielding the proposed quality-aware scaling law L(N,D,Q) = A/N^α + B/(D^β Q^γ) + E, which reduces to the ordinary Chinchilla law when Q=1. A parallel information-theoretic derivation (via mutual information and the Information Bottleneck framing) is offered as a second route to the same functional form. The paper also shows, algebraically, that three recent, independently proposed "quality-aware" scaling laws in the literature (Chen et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2024; Goyal et al., 2024) can each be recovered as special cases of its own more general deficiency decomposition (∆ as a weighted sum of a noise term, an inverse-coverage term, a repeated-data term, and a synthetic-data term), positioning the paper's contribution as a unifying generalization of this specific recent literature rather than a fourth independent proposal.

Experiments train small decoder-only models from scratch on two tasks: English-to-German neural machine translation (NMT) on a filtered ~101M-sentence-pair subset of Paracrawl v8, using an 8-layer GPT-Neo model (~133M parameters) with a custom-trained BPE tokenizer; and causal language modeling (CLM) on a subset of C4 (English), using an 8-layer Llama-3-style model (hidden size 512) with the Llama-3.2-1B tokenizer. For each task, the authors construct 3 dataset sizes (500K/1M/2M sentence pairs for NMT; 0.1B/1B/10B tokens for CLM) and, at each size, 7 quality levels created by synthetically corrupting a controlled fraction (0% to 50%, in the paper's stated increments) of samples: for NMT, corrupting a sample means replacing 50% of its non-special tokens with pad tokens; for CLM, it means randomly swapping 50% of a sample's non-special tokens with other random vocabulary tokens. Each size/quality combination is run with 3 replicate seeds, giving 63 runs per task (126 total). The resulting loss values are fit to the proposed law using the same parametric fitting approach as the original Chinchilla paper, with two fitting methods (ordinary least squares and Huber loss) as a basic robustness cross-check.

The central empirical finding is that the fitted quality exponent γ is well below 1 for both tasks (≈0.17-0.19 for NMT, ≈0.39-0.40 for CLM, with the two fitting methods agreeing closely), meaning effective data decays sublinearly as quality drops — models lose accuracy more slowly than classical noise-robustness theory (PAC learning, channel capacity, both of which motivate γ≥1) would predict, which the authors attribute to natural-language redundancy letting even partially corrupted samples retain some useful signal. CLM's higher γ than NMT is interpreted as CLM's token-swap corruption being harsher for distributional/local-dependency modeling than NMT's token-padding corruption, which still preserves alignment and some context. A secondary check plots the isolated quality-driven loss increase ΔL(Q) against the predicted Q^(-γ)-1 term and finds a near-linear relationship across quality levels and dataset sizes, taken as support for the multiplicative Q^(-γ) functional form specifically (rather than the whole law being an unconstrained curve fit). An out-of-sample generalization check refits the same functional form to a held-out slice of unseen CLM data and finds similar parameter estimates (Table 3), though the equivalent check is not reported for NMT. Two further appendix validations are offered: a monotonic decrease in embedding cosine similarity between clean and corrupted samples as injected noise increases (offered as evidence the synthetic noise manipulation genuinely degrades semantic content, not just superficial token identity); and a learning-rate sensitivity sweep (5 learning rates from 1e-5 to 5e-3) reporting the estimated γ at each rate. The paper closes with a 6-step practitioner's recipe for estimating Q on a real corpus (pick a clean reference subset, choose measurable deficiency proxies, combine them into a weighted deficiency score, map to Q via Q = e^(-Δ), sanity-check against a semantic-drift or downstream-performance signal, then fit the scaling law).

## Learnings

Folding a data-quality nuisance factor into a scaling law's exponent, motivated by an "effective sample size" argument, is a specific, checkable modeling move that this paper derives from two different classical noise-robustness results (PAC learning under random classification noise; Fisher-information/signal-to-noise-ratio arguments under additive Gaussian noise) and validates against controlled synthetic experiments — a reusable template for turning any well-defined data-degradation mechanism into a scaling-law-compatible exponent, provided a comparable statistical argument for how it reduces effective sample size can be constructed.

A parameter estimated as an "intrinsic," stable property of a task deserves an explicit sensitivity sweep over the training hyperparameters used to estimate it, not just a claim of stability from a single run; this paper's own appendix (a learning-rate sweep, Table 9) is a good instance of running exactly this check, even though (see Critical discussion) the resulting data does not fully support the specific claim of intrinsic stability made about it in the main text.

Validating that a synthetic experimental manipulation (here, injected token noise) actually moves the intended underlying construct (semantic/informational content, not just surface token identity) via an independent measurement (embedding similarity between clean and corrupted samples) is a lightweight, reusable sanity check worth applying whenever an experiment uses an artificial proxy for a real-world phenomenon (here, "low-quality data") that cannot be directly and cleanly manipulated.

## Verification

The paper's most relevant verification step for its own experimental design is the embedding-similarity check (Appendix E.1): before trusting that "50% of tokens replaced with pad tokens" or "50% of tokens randomly swapped" is a meaningful stand-in for the broader concept of "data quality," the authors independently measure that increasing injected noise produces a monotonic, near-linear decrease in cosine similarity between clean and corrupted sample embeddings, confirming the manipulation does track semantic degradation rather than an arbitrary transformation. This is a genuine, specific verification of the experimental construct itself, separate from verifying the model's predictions. On the harder question of whether the central fitted parameter (γ) is a reliable, reproducible estimate, the paper does run a relevant check (a learning-rate sensitivity sweep, Table 9) but the paper's own reported numbers there are more equivocal than the main text's framing suggests — see Critical discussion. No confidence intervals, standard errors, or bootstrap estimates are reported for any of the fitted scaling-law parameters (B, β, γ, E) in Table 2 or Table 3; the only robustness signal for these central estimates is agreement between two point-estimate fitting methods (least squares and Huber), which is a much weaker form of verification than an interval or resampling-based estimate of fit uncertainty.

## Field context

The paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) into a specific, recently active subfield: several 2024-2025 papers (Chen et al., 2025, ACL; Chang et al., 2024, EMNLP Industry Track; Goyal et al., 2024, CVPR) have each proposed their own way of folding a data-quality-like signal (density-based measures, compressibility-based diversity, or repeated-data value decay under multiple epochs) into a Chinchilla-style law. Rather than simply citing these as related, competing proposals, the paper's Appendix D shows each can be recovered as a special case of its own more general additive deficiency decomposition, framing itself as a unifying generalization of this specific, contemporaneous literature. It also draws on considerably older statistical learning theory (PAC noise-tolerant learning results going back to Angluin and Laird, 1988; classical channel-capacity and Fisher-information arguments) for the theoretical grounding of its two effective-sample-size lemmas, connecting a very recent empirical trend (quality-aware scaling laws) to decades-old noise-robustness theory. None of Hoffmann et al. (2022), Chen et al. (2025), Chang et al. (2024), or Goyal et al. (2024) are yet reviewed in this corpus.

## Critical discussion

The paper's own appendix data complicates one of its central claims. In the main text, the authors write that the stability of the ΔL(Q) linear-fit plots across dataset sizes "further suggests that γ is an intrinsic task-dependent parameter rather than an artifact of optimization or dataset sampling," and separately describe γ as "highly stable" across "practical learning rate regimes" based on a learning-rate sensitivity sweep. Reading that sweep's own reported numbers (Appendix E.2, Table 9), however: γ = 0.037 at learning rate 1e-5 (explicitly labeled "Undertrained" in the table), 0.394 at 3e-4, 0.396 at 1e-3 (the paper's own chosen rate, labeled "Ours"), and 0.177 at 5e-3 (labeled "Unstable") — more than a tenfold swing across the five tested learning rates, with genuine stability confirmed only in a narrow window immediately around the specific learning rate the authors happened to choose for their main experiments. "Stable near the hyperparameters we already picked" and "an intrinsic, optimization-independent property of the task" are meaningfully different claims, and only the narrower one is actually supported by the reported data; the main text's stronger framing is not fully earned by its own appendix evidence.

The experimental scale is well below both real pretraining runs and even the range the original Chinchilla law was itself fit on (70M-16B parameters, 5B-400B tokens): the NMT model has ~133M parameters and the CLM model is an 8-layer, 512-hidden-size architecture, smaller than the smallest Chinchilla-fit model in several cases, and the largest CLM training run uses 10B tokens. Whether the specific fitted γ values, or even the qualitative "sublinear decay" pattern the paper's main finding rests on, extrapolate to frontier-scale pretraining recipes and real (non-synthetic) data-quality variation is untested, and should be read as a hypothesis motivated by carefully controlled small-scale evidence rather than a validated large-scale property.

The synthetic noise model itself, while cleanly controllable and validated as genuinely semantically degrading (via the embedding-similarity check), is a fairly narrow proxy for what actually makes real pretraining corpora low-quality: near-duplicate boilerplate, template spam, off-topic or wrong-language content, and low-information filler text typically look nothing like locally token-scrambled or token-padded text. The paper does not attempt a more ecologically direct validation (for example, comparing the fitted law's predictions against a real quality-filtered versus unfiltered corpus pair, as several of the cited prior works do use), so the quantitative γ values reported here are specific to this paper's particular synthetic corruption mechanisms and should not be treated as general-purpose constants.

Finally, the out-of-sample generalization check (Table 3) is reported only for the CLM task, with no NMT equivalent and no explanation for the omission, which narrows how much the paper's "scaling generalization" claim can be said to cover both of its own experimental settings rather than just one.

## Relevance to us

This paper's core methodological pattern — fold a data-quality nuisance factor into a scaling-law exponent via an effective-sample-size argument, then independently validate that the quality proxy actually manipulates the intended semantic construct before trusting the fitted law — is directly applicable to our own data quantity vs. quality tension: if we ever need to decide, for a data-limited fine-tuning or pretraining problem in a specialized domain (e.g., a synthetic-physics-problem generation pipeline), whether cleaning existing data or collecting more of it is the better lever, this paper's practitioner's recipe (establish a clean reference subset, define measurable deficiency proxies, combine into a weighted score, map to Q, sanity-check against an independent semantic-drift signal, then fit the scaling law) is a concretely reusable checklist rather than an abstract suggestion. The specific numeric findings (γ≈0.17-0.40, meaning tested models were more robust to bulk synthetic corruption than classical noise theory predicts) should not be treated as domain-general constants given the paper's small model scale and narrow, non-ecological noise model; the more durable takeaway for us is the verification pattern itself (validate the proxy, then stress-test the estimated exponent against training-hyperparameter choices) rather than any of its specific reported numbers, and the appendix's own learning-rate sweep is a useful cautionary example of a paper's main-text claim outrunning what its own supporting table actually shows.
