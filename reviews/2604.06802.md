## What this paper is about

Frontier AI models now win gold at the International Mathematical Olympiad, but IMO problems are deliberately restricted to four elementary toolkits (algebra, combinatorics, geometry, number theory excluding calculus) specifically so that a clever human can solve them in a few hours using known tricks. This paper argues that gold-medal IMO performance says almost nothing about whether a model can do the kind of mathematics a PhD student or professional researcher actually does: sustained, weeks-long reasoning chains that draw on specialized theory from multiple advanced subfields at once. To measure this gap directly, the authors (from Surge AI, the company behind GSM8K) built Riemann-Bench, a private 25-problem benchmark authored by working research mathematicians, each problem verified independently by two other experts solving it from scratch, and evaluated frontier models as fully unconstrained research agents (Python, search, no token limits) over 100 independent attempts per problem. Every frontier model tested scores below 10%.

## Extended summary

Each of the 25 problems was contributed by an Ivy League mathematics professor, graduate student, or PhD-holding IMO medalist, drawn directly from problems the contributor encountered in their own research and that "routinely took their authors weeks to solve independently"; contributors reported their own graduate students and colleagues would likely struggle with the problems too. Every problem is required to have a single unique closed-form answer (no partial credit, no subjective grading), verified where multiple equivalent representations are possible via a programmatic checker. The construction protocol is double-blind: two domain experts, without seeing the author's solution, must independently solve each problem from scratch and arrive at the correct answer before the problem is accepted, and they additionally screen for ambiguity, underspecification, and appropriate difficulty; problems failing this bar were revised or dropped. The dataset itself is never released publicly — labs may only submit models through a controlled evaluation service — explicitly to prevent the kind of benchmark leakage and memorization the paper argues has already compromised several public math benchmarks.

Models are evaluated as "unconstrained research agents": full API access with a Python interpreter, search tools, and open-ended reasoning with no imposed token or interaction-format limits, on the stated rationale that a model's true research-relevant capability should be measured under conditions resembling how it would actually be deployed for research assistance, not squeezed into a rigid single-shot QA format. Each model is run 100 independent times per problem, and pass rates are computed via the standard unbiased pass@k estimator (Chen et al. 2021) rather than reported from a small or cherry-picked number of attempts. A detailed worked example (Section 4) — classifying isomorphism classes of "multibasic A-modules" over a ring of Hahn series with real-valued valuation, a problem one domain expert estimated would take 40-50 hours from scratch — illustrates the intended difficulty level and is accompanied by a specific failure-mode analysis: rather than working within the Hahn-series/A-module framework the problem specifies, the observed model reinterpreted the problem using an unrelated, self-invented "theory of generalized scales," misidentified two of the three given structural conditions, and fabricated a nonexistent supporting theorem ("M. Getz, Theorem 4.14 on Generalized Scales") to justify a final numeric answer that was off by orders of magnitude — while the overall response remained fluent and structurally coherent (correctly framed as a classification problem, complete with an invoked theorem and a computed number) despite resting on a misidentified mathematical foundation throughout.

Headline results (Table 1): across seven frontier models (Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3 Pro, Kimi K2.5, DeepSeek V3.2, GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5), pass@1 ranges from 6% (the two best, Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6) down to 2% (GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5), with every model below 10%. The paper contrasts this directly with the same model generations' near-ceiling performance on AIME (near 100% pass rates with tool access) and gold-medal-level IMO performance, framing the roughly 90-plus-point drop from competition to research-level mathematics as the paper's central empirical finding, and arguing this shows competition-benchmark saturation reflects mastery of "a particular style of mathematical reasoning" (lateral-thinking tricks with elementary tools) rather than research-level mathematical capability more broadly. The Discussion section frames a more modest near-term conclusion from this: current systems are unreliable as autonomous research mathematicians and are better positioned as assistants for specific subtasks under human verification, and explicitly distinguishes Riemann-Bench's remit (hard problems with a known, verifiable answer) from the harder, further "moonshot" of open conjecture formation and novel-framework construction where the existence of an answer is itself unknown.

## Learnings

The most transferable idea is the specific, mechanistic characterization of the failure mode observed on the worked example: rather than simply getting the answer wrong through a calculation slip, the model substituted an inapplicable but superficially plausible theoretical framework for the one the problem actually specifies, misassigned the meaning of two of the three given conditions to fit that substituted framework, and then fabricated a specific, precisely-named nonexistent theorem to bridge the resulting reasoning gap — producing an answer that "reads as structurally coherent" throughout. This is a more diagnostically useful description of a hallucination-under-specialized-reasoning failure than a generic "the model hallucinated" claim, since it names the specific mechanism (framework substitution plus citation fabrication) rather than only the symptom, and is a pattern worth checking for in any evaluation of LLM reasoning on unfamiliar specialized-theory problems.

A second learning is the paper's explicit argument for why benchmark saturation on one difficulty axis (competition mathematics) should not be read as progress on a qualitatively different axis (sustained, multi-domain research reasoning): the paper's framing that IMO-style problems are "designed to be solved in a few hours using known tools" via "a single key insight," whereas the problems here draw on specialized theory across multiple subfields simultaneously and take domain experts weeks, is a specific, falsifiable distinction (not just an assertion of greater difficulty) that other evaluation work assessing "has mathematical reasoning been solved" claims could usefully adopt as a check before generalizing from competition-benchmark results to broader claims about mathematical capability.

## Verification

The paper's verification effort is concentrated on problem quality rather than on independently re-checking its own headline capability claims, which is the right place to invest verification effort for a benchmark-construction paper specifically: the double-blind, from-scratch, two-independent-expert verification protocol is a stronger standard than the more common practice of trusting problem authors to self-certify their own solutions, and the paper reports that problems failing this bar (for ambiguity, error, or insufficient difficulty) were revised or excluded rather than kept. The evaluation methodology for measuring model capability is also more rigorous than a single-attempt or small-sample report: 100 independent runs per problem with the standard unbiased pass@k estimator gives stable per-problem pass-rate estimates rather than point estimates vulnerable to sampling noise at the level of an individual attempt. What the paper does not report, and what would strengthen the aggregate <10% headline claim specifically, is any measure of uncertainty at the level of the 25-problem sample itself — for instance, a bootstrap or jackknife confidence interval across problems for each model's overall pass rate — which would clarify how much the specific ranking and magnitude of the reported 2%-6% pass rates (a narrow 4-percentage-point band across seven frontier models) might shift under a different, equally rigorously-constructed sample of 25 comparably-hard problems. Because privacy is a design requirement, this uncertainty cannot be resolved by external replication either, making within-paper uncertainty quantification at the sample level the main available check, and it is the one verification step the paper does not provide.

## Field context

The paper positions itself carefully within an explicit, well-documented progression of increasingly difficult mathematical-reasoning benchmarks it surveys directly: elementary/competition-level (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU), olympiad-level (Omni-MATH, OlympiadBench, OlymMATH, JEEBench, MathOdyssey, MathArena), graduate/research-adjacent (GPQA, Humanity's Last Exam, GHOSTS, TheoremQA, ARB, SciBench, MathBench), and formal-mathematics benchmarks (MiniF2F, ProofNet, PutnamBench, DeepSeek-Prover-V2). Its most direct comparator is FrontierMath (Epoch AI), which the paper credits with a similar research-level ambition but explicitly differentiates on privacy and verification grounds — noting that FrontierMath's own reported near-0% frontier performance has since risen substantially (Google's AI co-mathematician reportedly reaching 48% on FrontierMath Tier 4), which the paper implicitly uses to motivate why an even harder, still-fully-private benchmark is needed to keep measuring genuine headroom rather than measuring benchmark-specific adaptation. Within this corpus, the paper's central empirical contrast (near-ceiling competition-math performance vs. near-floor research-math performance on the same model generation) is a data point directly relevant to 2605.06651's AI co-mathematician (reviewed in a prior session of this corpus) and to 2605.18661's broader AI-for-research survey (also reviewed previously), both of which grapple with the same underlying question of how far current systems' demonstrated mathematical competence actually extends into genuine research assistance versus pattern-matched competition-style problem solving; Riemann-Bench's specific, quantified answer (<10% even under unconstrained agentic conditions) is a concrete data point those broader surveys' more qualitative "research mathematics remains hard" claims can be checked against.

## Critical discussion

Because the dataset is deliberately kept private, no external reader can independently inspect problem quality, verify the double-blind protocol was executed as described, or check whether the reported pass rates reflect the benchmark's actual difficulty rather than, for instance, ambiguity in a subset of problems that the internal verification process did not catch. This is an inherent, disclosed tradeoff of any privacy-by-design contamination-resistant benchmark rather than a flaw specific to this paper, but it does mean the paper's empirical claims rest more heavily on trust in the authors' described process than a benchmark a reader could independently audit, and a reader should weigh the headline percentages accordingly.

A more specific concern is sample scope and representativeness. The 25 problems were explicitly solicited as ones that "routinely took their authors weeks to solve independently" and that the authors' own colleagues and graduate students "would struggle to solve," meaning the benchmark is constructed to select for the hardest tail of problems its contributors encountered, not a representative cross-section of research-level mathematics as a discipline. This is consistent with the paper's stated "moonshot" framing and is not misrepresented, but it means the Discussion's broader claim that "the broader and deeper domain of research mathematics largely still remains beyond current capabilities" generalizes past what a deliberately adversarially-selected 25-problem sample, drawn from an unreported and possibly narrow mix of subfields (the one detailed example concerns a fairly specialized corner of module theory and valuation theory), can rigorously establish on its own; the paper does not report a per-problem domain breakdown or per-domain performance breakdown that would let a reader assess whether the reported <10% figure holds evenly across the "diverse mathematical domains" the introduction lists, or is concentrated in a narrower subset. Relatedly, the qualitative failure-mode analysis in Section 6.3 is presented as illustrating "a recurring pattern observed across Riemann-Bench evaluations," but only one worked example is analyzed in this level of detail, and no quantified breakdown (e.g., what fraction of the other 24 problems' failures also involve fabricated-theorem citation specifically, as opposed to more mundane calculation or setup errors) is provided to substantiate how representative this single case actually is of the broader failure distribution.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the qualitative core claim — that current frontier models, even under maximally permissive agentic conditions with tools, search, and unlimited reasoning budget, fail on the substantial majority of problems drawn from genuine, expert-verified research mathematics, in sharp contrast to their near-ceiling performance on competition mathematics under comparable or more constrained conditions — is a real and striking result given the magnitude of the gap (roughly 90+ percentage points) and the rigor of the underlying problem-verification protocol, and does not depend on precisely how narrow or broad the specific 25-problem sample turns out to be; a benchmark drawing from a genuinely different but comparably-difficult set of research-level problems would very plausibly show a similarly large gap even if the exact percentages shifted. The specific illustrated failure mode (framework substitution plus citation fabrication under a fluent, structurally coherent surface) is a concrete, well-documented instance of a hallucination pattern distinct from simple arithmetic error, and stands on its own as a useful qualitative finding independent of how representative it turns out to be of the other 24 problems.
