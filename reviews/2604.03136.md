## What this paper is about

Most AI-text detectors work by spotting surface style — telltale words, sentence rhythm, an overused em-dash — but style is exactly the thing a model update or a light editing pass can erase, and indeed detection rates on stylistically-humanized creative writing have already been shown to collapse. This paper asks a different question: even after you throw away every stylistic cue, do AI-written stories still make systematically different narrative choices than human-written ones — what happens in the plot, how time is handled, whether the ending is morally tidy or ambiguous — the kind of decisions that would require rewriting the story's structure, not just its prose, to disguise? To test this at scale without hand-annotating tens of thousands of stories, the authors build StoryScope, a pipeline that uses LLMs to convert stories into structured representations, compare them across sources writing to the same prompt, and automatically discover a taxonomy of 304 interpretable narrative features grounded in an existing narratology framework (NarraBench). They then use these features — with all stylistic ones explicitly excluded — to detect AI authorship and attribute stories to specific models across a parallel corpus of over 61,000 stories from one human author and five frontier LLMs.

## Extended summary

The dataset pairs 10,272 human-written short stories extracted from Books3 with mirrored AI versions: for each human story, Gemini 2.5 Flash reverse-engineers the underlying writing prompt, and five LLMs (Gemini 3 Flash, Kimi K2.5, DeepSeek V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4) each generate a story from that same prompt, yielding six "sources" and 61,608 stories averaging ~4,750 words each (24 generation refusals aside). The StoryScope pipeline has three stages. First, each story is converted by GPT-5.1 into a structured JSON template spanning 10 of NarraBench's 12 narratological dimensions (Agent, Social Network, Event, Plot, Structure, Setting, Time, Revelation, Perspective, Style — excluding Paratext and Motivation as context-dependent), explicitly to force downstream stages to reason over narrative content rather than prose surface; an ablation confirms this matters, since running the pipeline directly on raw text instead of templates discovers a largely different, style-heavy set of top features (only 6 of 20 overlap with the template-based discovery). Second, a held-out discovery pool of 600 stories (100 prompts × 6 sources) is fed to GPT-5.1 for pairwise cross-source comparative analysis, distilling ~2.7M tokens of structured representations into ~686K tokens of cross-source observations. Third, GPT-5.1 is prompted (via 10 dimension-specific expert prompts, run three times for coverage) to propose discriminative features as closed-form questions with discrete answer types, yielding 408 candidates that are deduplicated via embedding-based clustering (cosine threshold 0.85, chosen after testing that 0.90 left obvious duplicates unmerged) down to 304 final features spanning five response types (categorical, ordinal, scale, binary, multi-select).

Every one of the 61,608 stories is then scored on all 304 features by Gemini 3 Flash (minimal thinking mode, chosen because full thinking caused pathologically long reasoning on this simple task while only marginally changing accuracy). Reliability of this production extractor is checked directly: a repeated-measures design over 300 extraction runs gives Krippendorff's α=0.90 and mean pairwise Cohen's κ=0.89 across five independent runs, and a small human-validation study (two annotators, 240 story-feature items across 12 stories) finds mean human-model Cohen's κ=0.84 — actually higher than the human-human agreement baseline on the same items (κ=0.74), suggesting the model's feature assignments are at least as internally consistent as independent human annotators' would be. XGBoost classifiers (chosen after comparing against linear models and random forests) are trained on encoded feature vectors for two tasks — binary human-vs-AI detection and six-way authorship attribution — using a proper prompt-level train/val/test split (7,383/1,405/1,384 prompts) to prevent leakage across paired stories from the same prompt.

The central quantitative result: a "Narrative" feature set (257 features, with 47 features flagged by an LLM style-dependence audit as style-related excluded) achieves 93.2% macro-F1 for binary detection — retaining over 97% of the 96.0% macro-F1 achieved when style features are included, and losing only 2.8 points (95% bootstrap CI 2.09-3.54) relative to the full model. For six-way attribution, narrative-only features reach 68.4% macro-F1 versus 77.3% with style added, still far above the 16.7% chance baseline. A compact 30-feature "core" subset (identified via bootstrap SHAP stability analysis, B=50 iterations with prompt-level resampling) retains 84.8% macro-F1 on its own; adding 71 model-specific "fingerprint" features (identified from the multiclass SHAP analysis as concentrated in one source) brings this to 91.1%. Text-based baselines operating on raw prose (ModernBERT fine-tuned on the training set, a stylometric-feature XGBoost, a TF-IDF XGBoost) all achieve near-ceiling ≥99.5% macro-F1 on both tasks, while zero-shot Binoculars performs near chance (55.9%) — establishing that the narrative-feature approach substantially closes the gap to supervised text classifiers using a fully interpretable, style-independent signal, at some real cost in raw accuracy relative to the best black-box text classifiers.

The specific discovered narrative differences (Section 4.1) are extensive and mostly effect-size-quantified: AI stories over-explain themes (narrators state the theme explicitly 77% of the time vs. 52% for humans), favor protagonist-driven, causally tidy single-track plots with far fewer subplots (79% "no subplots" vs. 57% for humans) and internal-acceptance endings (47% vs. 27%) over human authors' greater comfort with moral ambiguity and nonlinear/flashback-heavy structure; AI conveys emotion overwhelmingly through bodily sensation and imagery (81% vs. 38% for explicit emotion labeling patterns) rather than naming feelings directly; and humans reference real external texts/authors nearly twice as often (47% vs. 24%) and break the fourth wall to address the reader far more often (67% vs. 39%). A robustness check applies a professional-writer-informed span-level artifact-removal rewriting framework (LAMP) to 278 Gemini-generated stories, using Gemini itself as the rewriter; narrative-feature detection on the edited stories drops only 1.6 points (95.5%→93.9% macro-F1), interpreted as evidence the narrative signal is largely orthogonal to the surface prose artifacts LAMP specifically targets (clichés, purple prose, redundant exposition).

On attribution structure: projecting narrative feature vectors onto their first two linear discriminant components shows the five AI models forming a shared, overlapping cluster clearly separated from human stories (Figure 2), with the six most-confused source pairs in the six-way classifier being exclusively AI↔AI (dominated by Gemini↔DeepSeek confusion) rather than any human↔AI pair; quantitatively, mean human-AI centroid distance (6.6) is 1.6× the mean AI-AI centroid distance (4.3) in the encoded feature space, and even the closest human-AI centroid pair is farther apart than the most distant AI-AI pair. Human stories are also measurably rarer: mean per-story rarity percentile (distance to 25 nearest neighbors, computed against the train+val distribution) is 0.71 for humans versus 0.49 for AI (Cohen's d=0.83), with humans overrepresented nearly 3.5× in the corpus-wide top-10%-rarest tail (24.7% vs. 7.1%). Per-model "fingerprints" are qualitatively distinct: Claude shows the flattest event-intensity escalation and the most consistent, tradition-honoring voice; GPT relies heavily on gossip/rumor as a plot device and subverts expectations more than other AI models; Gemini produces the tidiest endings and bleakest settings; DeepSeek front-loads context; and Kimi is described as having the fewest distinctive fingerprints, sitting closest to the generic center of the AI distribution. Extensive appendix analyses address potential confounds directly: a length-matched re-evaluation shows narrative-model accuracy is essentially unchanged (93.2% before and after matching median story length between human and AI subsets), a topic-based Kruskal-Wallis test across six genre categories finds no significant detectability difference by topic (H=4.69, p=0.46), and a conservative memorization-risk audit (exact/near-verbatim n-gram overlap against shuffled-human controls) flags only 0.70% of story pairs as high-risk, with removing all such prompts changing the headline macro-F1 numbers by at most 0.27 points in either direction.

## Learnings

The most transferable methodological idea is the template-mediation design choice itself, directly validated by the paper's own ablation: forcing an intermediate structured representation between raw prose and comparative feature discovery changes which features get discovered (structure-heavy vs. style-heavy, with only 6/20 top-feature overlap between the two pipeline variants), demonstrating that the choice to abstract away surface wording before searching for discriminative differences is not a cosmetic step but actively determines what kind of signal the discovery process can find. This is a specific, generalizable design lesson for anyone building an LLM-based feature-discovery pipeline over any kind of text: what representation you compare at (raw text vs. a structured abstraction) is itself a strong prior on what differences you will and won't be able to detect.

A second learning is the specific technique of validating an LLM feature extractor not just against human judgment in the abstract, but against the human-human agreement baseline on the identical items — finding that model-human agreement (κ=0.84) exceeds human-human agreement (κ=0.74) on the same 240 items is a much stronger and more informative validation claim than a bare "the model agrees with humans reasonably well," since it directly addresses whether the automated pipeline is meaningfully worse than the human alternative it's replacing, rather than only whether it clears an arbitrary agreement threshold.

A third learning, demonstrated rather than merely argued, is that structural/narrative-level differences between human and AI writing survive an editing intervention specifically designed to remove the surface artifacts most existing detectors target: applying LAMP's professional-writer-informed span-level rewriting to Gemini stories costs only 1.6 points of detection accuracy, which is a concrete, mechanistically interpretable demonstration (not just a claim) that narrative choices and prose-surface artifacts are largely separable properties of a text, with direct practical implications for detection robustness as models continue to reduce surface-level "AI-isms."

## Verification

This paper's verification practices are unusually extensive for a paper of this kind, addressing several distinct threats to validity with dedicated, quantified checks rather than narrative reassurance. Extractor reliability is checked via a genuine repeated-measures design (five independent runs, Krippendorff's α=0.90) rather than a single run assumed to be representative. Ground-truth quality is checked against independent human annotation on a held-out sample, with the specific and informative comparison against human-human agreement on the same items. Three distinct confound hypotheses are tested directly rather than dismissed: a length confound (addressed via a decile-stratified length-matched re-evaluation, further broken into short/medium/long bands, all showing stable accuracy), a topic confound (addressed via a genre-stratified Kruskal-Wallis omnibus test finding no significant effect), and a memorization/contamination confound specific to using publicly-known human-authored source texts (addressed via an explicit n-gram overlap audit against shuffled-human controls, with the audit's own limits disclosed: "we measure output overlap rather than formal training-set membership, so we treat it as a sensitivity analysis rather than proof"). The one verification gap worth naming, because it is more subtle than the others and not directly addressed by the paper's existing checks: Gemini 3 Flash is used as the production feature-assignment extractor for the entire 61,608-story corpus, and Gemini 3 Flash is simultaneously one of the five AI sources being classified. The paper's human-validation study establishes that the extractor's feature assignments generally track independent human judgment well, but that validation sample (12 stories) is not reported as being specifically representative of Gemini-authored stories, so it does not directly rule out a narrower possibility: that Gemini's feature assignments for stories written by Gemini itself could differ systematically (in either direction) from its assignments for other sources' stories in a way unrelated to genuine narrative content — a same-family-judge concern distinct from, and not resolved by, the general reliability and human-agreement checks the paper does report.

## Field context

The paper positions itself explicitly against the dominant AI-text-detection paradigm, which it credits with being "highly discriminatory" via surface stylistic cues (word choice, em-dashes, specific vocabulary like "delve" and "tapestry") while noting this exact strength is also the paradigm's central weakness: it cites a concrete, dated example (GPT-5.4 significantly reducing em-dash usage) and a specific published finding (fine-tuning to mimic human style dropping creative-writing AI-detection rates from 97% to 3%) as evidence that stylistic detection is becoming increasingly "fleeting," motivating the paper's choice to investigate a structurally different, harder-to-erase signal. It grounds its feature taxonomy directly in NarraBench (Hamilton et al. 2025), an existing narratological framework rooted in literary theory (citing specific narratological lineages, e.g. Genette 1980 on narrative order/duration/frequency for the temporal dimension), rather than inventing an ad hoc feature space, which gives the discovered features an independent theoretical grounding beyond "whatever an LLM happened to notice." Within the broader literature on LLM creative-writing homogenization this batch has also covered (2605.27878's "Narrative Flattening" study of post-training's effect on thematic/affective/stylistic variation, reviewed in a prior session), this paper's finding that the five AI models cluster tightly together in narrative-feature space while human stories occupy a more dispersed, rarer region is a complementary, cross-sectional (across-model) demonstration of a similar underlying phenomenon that the other paper demonstrates longitudinally (across post-training stages within one model lineage) — together the two papers corroborate, from independent angles and independent research groups, that convergence toward a narrower narrative/stylistic attractor is a robust, model-general property of current LLM creative writing rather than an artifact specific to one model family or one measurement approach.

## Critical discussion

The paper's experimental design necessarily fixes one important variable that a fully open-ended comparison of human versus AI creative writing would not: every AI story is generated from a premise that was itself reverse-engineered from the paired human story, meaning the comparison measures how differently sources develop a given premise into a full narrative, not how differently they would originate a premise in the first place. This is a reasonable and clearly-motivated design choice for enabling a controlled, paired comparison (the alternative — letting each source choose its own premise — would confound narrative-construction differences with premise-selection differences), and the paper does not overclaim beyond this scope, but a reader should understand the reported narrative differences (tidier plots, more explicit themes, fewer subplots, etc.) as differences in narrative execution given a shared starting point, which is a narrower and more specific claim than "AI and humans conceive of different kinds of stories" in the fully open-ended sense the paper's framing around originality and authorship sometimes gestures toward.

A second, more specific point concerns the same-family relationship between the feature-assignment model (Gemini 3 Flash) and one of the five classified AI sources (also Gemini 3 Flash generating stories). While the paper's overall reliability and human-validation checks are genuinely strong, neither directly tests whether Gemini's feature assignments are equally well-calibrated when scoring its own generations as when scoring other sources' — a question the six-way attribution results indirectly bear on (Gemini is one of the more confused, less-distinctive sources in that task, per Table 12 and the "triplets" discussion), which is at least consistent with the extractor not obviously inflating Gemini's distinctiveness, but this is an indirect observation rather than a targeted check of extractor self-consistency.

Beyond these two points, the paper's own appendix work substantially pre-empts the most common objections a critical reader would otherwise raise: the length confound, the topic confound, and the memorization/contamination risk from using a corpus of publicly-known literary texts are each addressed with a dedicated, quantified analysis rather than an assumption or a dismissive aside, and in every case the reported sensitivity is small (a few tenths of a macro-F1 point at most) relative to the headline effects. The paper is also appropriately calibrated in how it frames its own numbers: it explicitly reports that narrative features alone are a weaker detector than the best supervised text-based baselines (93.2% vs. ≥99.5% macro-F1 for ModernBERT/stylometric/TF-IDF classifiers), rather than claiming narrative features are simply "better," and instead argues for their value on durability and interpretability grounds specifically — a claim the LAMP robustness check (Section 4.2) directly substantiates rather than merely asserts.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the central finding that narrative-level, style-independent features carry substantial and durable human-vs-AI discriminative signal (93.2% macro-F1, retaining >97% of the full model's performance, essentially unaffected by a targeted style-removal editing pass) is demonstrated with multiple converging and mutually reinforcing lines of evidence — direct classification performance, a dedicated robustness intervention (LAMP editing), an independent geometric analysis of feature-space clustering and rarity, and confound audits for the three most obvious alternative explanations (length, topic, memorization) — rather than resting on any single result; the qualitative characterization of specific discovered differences (thematic over-explicitness, causal tidiness, bodily-rather-than-named emotional expression, reduced intertextual and fourth-wall engagement) is grounded in concrete, quantified per-feature percentages rather than impressionistic description, and the per-model "fingerprint" findings are corroborated by the independent, structurally-distinct six-way confusion-matrix analysis (AI-AI confusions dominating, with Gemini/DeepSeek/Kimi forming a genuinely more confusable cluster than any of them is with Claude, GPT, or human).
