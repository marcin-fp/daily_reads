Citation: Bai, Y., Dong, Q., Jiang, T., Lv, X., Du, Z., Zeng, A., Tang, J., Li, J. IndexCache: Accelerating Sparse Attention via Cross-Layer Index Reuse. arXiv:2603.12201, 2026.

## What this paper is about

DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) speeds up long-context inference by having each layer's core attention only look at the top-k most relevant preceding tokens, chosen by a cheap "lightning indexer." The problem this paper identifies is that the indexer itself still has to scan the entire context at every single layer to pick those tokens, so its total cost across all layers still grows quadratically with context length and increasingly dominates inference time as context gets longer. The authors show that consecutive layers' indexers pick nearly the same tokens anyway, so most of that indexer computation is redundant. IndexCache exploits this by having only a small subset of layers run their own indexer and letting the rest simply reuse the nearest such layer's token selection — and the paper's main technical contribution is figuring out, both without and with retraining, which layers can safely skip their own indexer without hurting quality.

## Extended summary

DSA scores all preceding tokens per query with a lightweight multi-head ReLU-gated dot-product indexer, selects the top-k=2048, and runs full attention only over that subset — reducing per-layer core attention from O(L²) to O(Lk), but the indexer itself remains O(L²) and runs at every layer, so total indexer cost is O(NL²) across N layers. Profiling a 30B DSA model shows the indexer's share of total latency rising sharply with context length (up to 81% of prefill time at 200K tokens). The paper's motivating empirical observation (validated via a full pairwise top-k overlap heatmap across all 47 layers, Appendix A) is that adjacent layers' indexer selections overlap 70-100%, with the heatmap revealing distinct layer clusters of mutually high overlap and sharp transitions at cluster boundaries.

IndexCache partitions the N layers into a binary pattern of Full (F) layers, which retain their own indexer and compute fresh top-k sets, and Shared (S) layers, which have no indexer and simply inherit the nearest preceding F layer's index set — implemented as a single conditional branch added to the per-layer inference loop, requiring no additional GPU memory. Two complementary approaches determine the F/S pattern. Training-free IndexCache uses a greedy search: starting from all-F, at each step it tentatively converts every remaining F layer (except layer 1) to S, evaluates the resulting language-modeling loss on a fixed calibration set, and commits whichever single flip minimizes that loss, repeating until the target retention ratio is reached; a pipeline-parallelism-aware variant reduces the O(N²) search cost by roughly a factor of P (the number of pipeline stages) by searching layer blocks sequentially. The paper explicitly shows (Appendix C) that a more obvious, theoretically motivated, and computationally cheaper alternative — choosing the pattern via a dynamic-programming optimization over a cross-layer cosine-similarity matrix of attention outputs — fails to outperform naive uniform interleaving, because per-layer output similarity is a local metric that cannot capture how small token-selection mismatches cascade through downstream layers; the loss-based greedy search works because LM loss is a global, end-to-end proxy. Training-aware IndexCache instead retrains the retained indexers with a multi-layer distillation loss, where each F layer's indexer is trained against the attention distributions of all S layers it serves; the paper proves this multi-layer loss has an identical gradient to distilling against the single averaged (centroid) target distribution across served layers (Proposition 1), and adopts the multi-layer formulation only for implementation efficiency.

Experiments use a 30B-parameter MoE DSA model built from GLM-4.7-Flash (47 layers, MLA) and, for a scaling check, the 744B-parameter (40B active) GLM-5. Evaluation spans five long-context benchmarks (MRCR v2, GraphWalks, LongBench v2, RULER, AA-LCR) and four general/reasoning benchmarks (AIME 2025, GPQA-Diamond, LiveCodeBench v6, IFBench). End-to-end inference on the 30B model (SGLang, H100, dp attention) shows IndexCache at 1/4 indexer retention delivering up to 1.82× prefill speedup and 1.48× per-request decode speedup at 200K context, with speedups growing with context length as expected since the indexer's relative cost share grows with length; total decode throughput under full KV-cache saturation improves 22-51%. For training-free IndexCache, naive uniform interleaving degrades quality substantially at aggressive retention (Long-context average drops from 50.2 to 43.0 at 1/4 retention, to 35.3 at 1/8), while the greedy-searched pattern nearly eliminates this gap at 1/2 and 1/4 retention (49.9-50.3 vs. the 50.2 baseline) though a non-negligible gap remains at the more extreme 1/8 retention (46.1 vs. 50.2 even with search). General/reasoning performance stays within about 1 point of baseline across nearly all configurations, with the 1/4 searched pattern slightly exceeding baseline on AIME 2025 and GPQA-Diamond, which the authors tentatively interpret as removing redundant indexer computation acting as a mild regularizer. For training-aware IndexCache, the layer-pattern sensitivity seen in the training-free setting largely disappears: uniform interleaving at 1/2 retention (51.6 Long-avg) matches or slightly exceeds the searched pattern (50.6) and the shortened-training-pipeline baseline (51.0), and an ablation removing the cross-layer distillation loss (training each retained indexer only against its own layer) drops Long-avg from 51.6 to 49.8, isolating the multi-layer distillation objective's specific contribution. A preliminary scaling check on the 744B GLM-5 shows broadly consistent trends (searched pattern at 1/2 retention slightly exceeding baseline, 78.7 vs. 78.4; at 1/4 retention within 0.4 points), with the paper explicitly noting that uniform interleaving happening to preserve quality at 1/2 retention on GLM-5 is "likely coincidental" rather than a robust property of uniform patterns — a self-aware caveat about not over-crediting one favorable uniform-interleaving result.

## Learnings

The most transferable idea is the paper's own explicitly reported negative result (Appendix C): a theoretically well-motivated, cheaper alternative to the greedy loss-based search (choosing which layers can share indices by directly measuring cross-layer output-similarity via a globally-optimal dynamic program) turns out to perform no better than the naive uniform baseline, because a local similarity metric cannot see how small token-selection errors compound through later layers. This is a specific, mechanistically-explained cautionary lesson for anyone building similar layer-pruning or layer-sharing search procedures: a locally-measured similarity or overlap score, however precisely optimized, is not automatically a good proxy for global end-to-end quality, and the paper's explanation (small mismatched "critical" tokens can matter disproportionately for downstream reasoning even when aggregate overlap is high) generalizes beyond this specific sparse-attention setting.

A second learning is the clean theoretical result (Proposition 1) that training an indexer against a sum of per-layer KL-divergence terms produces exactly the same gradient as training it against a single averaged target distribution — a small but genuinely useful piece of applied math that justifies an implementation choice (using the multi-layer sum formulation for memory/compute efficiency, since it avoids needing to materialize both the predicted and each served layer's full attention distribution simultaneously) without sacrificing correctness, and is a reusable pattern for anyone designing a "one model serves several downstream consumers" distillation objective.

A third learning is the qualitative finding, consistent across both the training-free and training-aware settings, that which layers retain their indexer matters far more than how many do: uniform interleaving at even a moderate 1/2 retention ratio degrades quality meaningfully in the training-free setting, while a carefully chosen (searched) 1/4-retention pattern nearly matches full-indexer quality — suggesting indexer "importance" is a genuine, non-uniform, intrinsic property of specific layers (the paper shows this ranking is stable across different calibration sets) rather than something a fixed-interval heuristic can be expected to capture.

## Verification

The paper's verification strategy for its central efficiency claims is unusually thorough for a systems paper of this kind. The motivating premise (cross-layer top-k overlap) is verified directly via a full pairwise overlap heatmap across all layer pairs on a real calibration set (Appendix A), not merely asserted from prior work on full-attention models. The greedy search's stability is checked by confirming the discovered layer-importance ordering is consistent across different calibration sets (an intrinsic-model-property claim, not a data-artifact claim) and that lower calibration LM loss correlates with better downstream task performance, giving the search's proxy objective some independent grounding. The paper also verifies its design choice against a plausible, cheaper alternative (similarity-based dynamic-programming search) and reports that alternative's failure explicitly rather than omitting it, which is a genuine strength: a reader can see why the more expensive greedy loss-based search is necessary rather than taking the authors' word for it. That said, none of the reported LM-evaluation-benchmark results (Tables 2, 3, 4) include variance, confidence intervals, or a stated number of repeated samples per problem, despite several of the constituent benchmarks (AIME 2025 in particular, with a small problem count, and any benchmark scored via sampling-based generation at temperature 1.0 per Appendix D) being known to carry meaningful run-to-run variance; several of the paper's more marginal claimed improvements (e.g., the 1/4 searched pattern's AIME 2025 score of 92.6 vs. the 91.0 baseline, or GPQA-Diamond's 78.6 vs. 77.6) are within a range that could plausibly reflect sampling noise rather than a reliable effect, and the paper does not report the statistics needed to distinguish the two.

## Field context

The paper explicitly builds on and differentiates itself from two adjacent literatures it names directly: cross-layer sharing methods for full-attention models (TidalDecode, LessIsMore, OmniKV, DELTA, and Kascade, which formalizes anchor-layer selection via dynamic programming over a cross-layer similarity matrix) that all rely on full attention as an oracle for identifying important tokens at designated anchor layers; and cross-layer KV-cache sharing methods (You Only Cache Once, cross-layer attention, MiniCache, MLKV) that reduce memory rather than compute by sharing key/value tensors across layers. IndexCache's explicitly stated differentiator from the first group is that DSA has already eliminated full attention entirely, so there is no oracle left to anchor to — the paper's contribution is showing the sharing principle still applies when the "oracle" itself is the cheap lightning indexer rather than full attention, and that the specific search/optimization techniques needed (greedy loss-based search; multi-layer distillation) differ from what full-attention-oracle methods require, since Kascade's dynamic-programming-over-similarity approach is shown (via this paper's own similarity-based ablation, Appendix C) not to transfer directly to the indexer-sharing setting. The paper also explicitly names a generalization claim it does not itself test: that the cross-layer-reuse principle should extend to any sparse attention method with a dynamic (non-fixed) token-selection step, citing MoBA's and NSA's block-level selection as candidate targets — a plausible but unverified extrapolation the paper is careful to phrase as expectation rather than demonstrated fact. Within the broader efficient-inference literature this batch has touched (e.g., 2602.11451's elastic-depth looped Transformers, reviewed in a prior session), IndexCache shares a structural theme with that paper's cross-layer redundancy exploitation, though IndexCache targets attention-computation redundancy specifically and at production model scale (744B parameters), whereas the looped-Transformer paper targeted representational redundancy at much smaller (~1B parameter) research scale.

## Critical discussion

The paper's empirical claims are backed by an unusually complete set of internal checks for a systems paper — a direct empirical validation of the motivating overlap premise, an explicitly reported negative result for a plausible alternative method, a proposition proving the training-aware loss's gradient equivalence, cross-calibration-set stability checks, and a scaling validation on a 744B production model — which collectively make the paper's central mechanism (indexer redundancy exists, is layer-specific rather than uniform, and can be found via a global loss-based search) considerably better supported than a typical efficiency paper reporting only end-to-end speedup numbers. The main gap is statistical: none of the downstream quality tables report variance or repeated-sample statistics, so the paper's more marginal claimed wins (a few of the individual reasoning-benchmark deltas in Tables 2-4 are on the order of 1-2 points on benchmarks with limited problem counts or sampling-based scoring) cannot be distinguished from noise using the information the paper provides, even though the paper's headline results (the large end-to-end speedups, and the qualitative training-free-vs-training-aware pattern-sensitivity contrast) are large enough in magnitude that this concern does not undermine them.

The evaluation is also narrow along two axes the paper itself is not entirely silent about but does not fully address. First, all downstream evaluation is confined to the GLM/DSA model family (the 30B GLM-4.7-Flash-derived DSA model and the 744B GLM-5); no other DSA-style production model or other sparse-attention architecture (e.g., NSA, MoBA) is tested, so the generalization claim in Related Work — that the cross-layer-reuse principle should transfer to any dynamic token-selection sparse-attention method — remains an untested extrapolation rather than an evaluated claim. Second, the training-aware results (Table 3) use a deliberately shortened DSA training pipeline (1,000-step warm-up plus 4,000-step sparse training from an already-pretrained base model) explicitly acknowledged to produce "a small performance difference compared to the original DSA reported in Table 2," meaning the training-aware and training-free result tables are not on a strictly comparable baseline, and the paper does not report whether the training-aware benefits (particularly the disappearance of pattern-sensitivity) would hold under a full from-scratch DSA training pipeline of the kind actually used in production.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the core mechanistic claim — that indexer redundancy across layers is real, substantial, and non-uniformly distributed across layers (rather than a simple function of retention ratio alone) — is demonstrated through multiple independent and mutually corroborating pieces of evidence (the overlap heatmap, the greedy search's stable cross-calibration-set layer ranking, and the sharp quality gap between uniform interleaving and searched patterns at matched retention ratios); the large end-to-end inference speedups (1.82× prefill, 1.48× decode at 200K context) are measured on a realistic serving stack (SGLang on H100 with data-parallel attention) rather than a synthetic FLOP-counting estimate, giving them practical credibility; and the paper's willingness to report its own negative result and to flag a "likely coincidental" favorable outcome for uniform interleaving on GLM-5, rather than omitting or over-interpreting it, is a genuine mark of methodological honesty that increases confidence in the reliability of the paper's other, more favorable claims.
