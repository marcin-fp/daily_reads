Citation: Zheyuan Zhang, Alvin Zhang, Daniel Khashabi, and Tianmin Shu. Continual Learning Mechanisms Compose for Long-Horizon Memorization. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06986v1

## What this paper is about

If a language model is meant to build up memory by repeatedly fine-tuning on new information over time, it needs to still remember task 1 after being updated on tasks 2 through 100. Sequential fine-tuning is well known to overwrite earlier updates (catastrophic forgetting), and many individual mitigation mechanisms exist. This paper asks a more specific question: does combining several complementary anti-forgetting mechanisms actually work better than the best single one at a genuinely long horizon (100 sequential tasks), and if so, which combinations and why. It builds three purpose-built 100-task memorization datasets, searches the combinatorial space of mechanism combinations efficiently, and runs a full factorial experiment to measure which mechanisms help, by how much, and whether they interact.

## Extended summary

**Setup.** The model (Qwen3-4B-Base) learns 100 query-answer tasks in sequence via continual supervised fine-tuning, domain-incremental (no task identifier at inference), with no rehearsal of raw earlier examples. After each task the model is evaluated on every task learned so far, producing a full temporal accuracy matrix from which the paper reports final retention (mean accuracy over all tasks after the last update), diagonal accuracy ("Diag," accuracy on each task immediately after it is learned, i.e. immediate acquisition), and forgetting (drop from each task's peak accuracy to its final accuracy).

**Three datasets of increasing naturalism.** Symbol-QA: 10,000 random six-character-key to four-character-value associations (100 tasks x 100 items) with no learnable structure connecting key to value, so nothing but rote memorization can succeed. LLM-QA: 10,000 query-answer pairs about invented fictional entities, generated by Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 across 100 fixed topics (one per task), using natural language and familiar entity types but facts that do not exist. Real-QA: 5,000 items drawn evenly from ten public QA datasets (TriviaQA, NQ-Open, PopQA, SQuAD, WebQuestions, OpenBookQA, SciQ, ARC-Easy/Challenge, MedMCQA), converted to free-form recall and filtered per-base-model so that only items the base model fails to answer correctly across five sampled completions are retained, avoiding "retention" credit for facts the model already knew before training.

**Design space.** Two axes are crossed. Anchors specify what an update should preserve: a data anchor (generative replay, using the frozen previous-checkpoint model to synthesize pseudo-sequences from a task-agnostic replay token, paired against the current task's minibatch, with soft next-token targets from the frozen model), a function anchor (self-distillation against the previous model's output distribution on current-task inputs, following Learning without Forgetting), and a weight anchor (importance-weighted regularization; the main experiments use Synaptic Intelligence, SI). Low-rank allocation rules specify where LoRA updates are retained across tasks: shared LoRA (one adapter continuously updated across all 100 tasks) versus merged LoRA (each task gets a fresh LoRA pair, folded into the dense weights after training, adapting ReLoRA's merge-and-reinitialize pattern to the continual setting); both keep constant-size retained state as tasks accumulate, unlike task-growing alternatives (O-LoRA, sequential OSRM) evaluated separately.

**Search procedure.** Testing all 90 candidate configurations (anchor combinations x hyperparameter variants) to 100 tasks on all three datasets would be expensive, so the paper introduces task-level successive halving (TSH): rank all 90 configurations by mean final retention (three seeds) after 10 tasks, keep the top 45; after 20 tasks keep the top 23; after 50 tasks keep the top 10; only those ten continue to 100 tasks. TSH is explicitly acknowledged not to guarantee retaining the true best configuration, but a validation check (Appendix D.5) reports Spearman correlations of 0.90-0.95 between the 10-task search ranking and the eventual 100-task final ranking (mean absolute rank displacement 1.3-1.5 positions across the three datasets), despite the two phases using different task orders.

**Main results.** No standalone mechanism survives to the 50-task TSH stage on any dataset (Figure 2); every configuration that reaches 100 tasks combines a data anchor with merged LoRA. The full 2^4 factorial (16 anchor combinations x shared/merged LoRA, 21 methods total per dataset counting the O-LoRA/OSRM comparisons, three seeds each) confirms this: naive sequential fine-tuning reaches 1.2% average final retention across the three datasets, the strongest standalone mechanism reaches only 4.2% (Symbol-QA), 7.5% (LLM-QA), or 12.5% (Real-QA), while the strongest per-dataset composition reaches 23.2%, 41.8%, and 54.8% respectively. The single method that combines all three anchors with merged LoRA is the only composition ranking in the top 3 on every dataset, averaging 34.9% final retention, a 28-fold improvement over naive fine-tuning. Immediate acquisition ("Diag") is reported as nearly perfect across methods, so the paper attributes final-retention differences to forgetting rather than to difficulty learning the current task.

**Factorial effects (Table 1, significance at p<0.05).** Replay (data anchor) and merged LoRA have the two largest main effects on every dataset (replay: +9.5 to +19.3 points; merged LoRA: +5.9 to +20.5 points) and a significant positive (super-additive) interaction on all three: their standalone contributions sum to only 3.9-13.9 points, but combined they raise retention over naive fine-tuning by 15.6-46.9 points. SI (weight anchor) and self-distillation (function anchor) have smaller, more dataset-dependent effects; self-distillation's interaction with replay is significantly negative on LLM-QA and Real-QA (smaller marginal benefit once replay is present), and SI's interaction with merged LoRA is significantly negative only on Symbol-QA, which the authors attribute to SI's importance values and parameter references becoming stale once merged LoRA periodically reinitializes the LoRA factors those values were computed against.

**Memory half-life and general capability.** Naive fine-tuning has a memory half-life (age at which retention on a given task falls below half its initial value) of 1-2 tasks; the best standalone mechanism extends this to 4-11 tasks; the best composition extends it to 19-44 tasks, though every curve continues declining with memory age, so composition changes the timescale of forgetting rather than eliminating it. Replacing merged LoRA with the task-growing-state alternatives O-LoRA or sequential OSRM does not consistently improve retention (O-LoRA: small gains on the two natural-language datasets, a decrease on Symbol-QA; OSRM: worse on all three), but O-LoRA preserves substantially more general capability (GSM8K, MATH, MGSM, MMLU-Redux, four-benchmark base-model average 69.4%) than merged LoRA after training on LLM-QA and Real-QA (O-LoRA four-benchmark averages 26.8% and 28.8% vs. merged LoRA's 13.0% and 13.4%), despite only modest memorization gains; all methods still lose more than 40 points of general capability relative to the untrained base model.

## Learnings

The strongest pairwise result in the paper, that replay and merged LoRA interact super-additively (their combined gain far exceeds the sum of their individual gains, consistently across three datasets built on different content types), is a specific, reusable finding about which two mechanism categories to combine first when designing a continual-memorization system, rather than a general claim that "more mechanisms is always better" — the weight anchor (SI) and function anchor (self-distillation) contribute real but smaller, more dataset- and combination-dependent gains, and in one case (SI with merged LoRA) the interaction is actively negative because SI's accumulated importance estimates reference LoRA coordinates that merged LoRA periodically discards and reinitializes.

Task-level successive halving (ranking survivors by outcome at an early point in a long sequential process, rather than by early training-iteration counts as in standard successive halving) is a specific, transferable search strategy for any expensive, long-horizon sequential evaluation where full-length runs are too costly to try on every candidate; the paper's own validation (10-task rankings correlating at 0.90-0.95 with 100-task final rankings, despite a different task order between the two phases) is a useful template for checking whether an early-horizon proxy is trustworthy before relying on it, rather than simply assuming it.

Methods with constant retained state (shared or merged LoRA) versus methods whose retained state grows with the number of tasks (O-LoRA, sequential OSRM) trade off memorization against general-capability preservation in opposite directions here: the growing-state O-LoRA loses less general capability than merged LoRA for a similar or smaller memorization gain, suggesting that the mechanism protecting old associations and the mechanism protecting general (non-task) capability are not the same lever, and optimizing one does not automatically optimize the other.

## Verification

The paper's core empirical claims rest on a full 2^4 factorial design with explicit significance testing (p<0.05, Table 1 bolds significant main and interaction effects), which is a stronger evidentiary standard than a single best-configuration comparison. Immediate acquisition ("Diag") is reported as a control to separate forgetting from failure-to-learn: if a method's low final retention instead reflected poor initial learning, that would need a different explanation, and the paper checks and rules this out by reporting near-perfect diagonal accuracy across methods. The TSH search procedure that selects hyperparameters and surviving configurations for the full-scale experiments is itself validated against the eventual 100-task outcome (Spearman correlation 0.90-0.95, Appendix D.5), rather than assumed to be reliable. General capability preservation is checked on four external benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MGSM, MMLU-Redux) rather than asserted from the memorization numbers alone. What is not reported: confidence intervals or per-seed variance for the headline retention numbers in Figures 1-3 and the main text (Appendix E.2 is cited as reporting standard deviations over seeds, but these do not appear in the main extract), and results throughout rest on three seeds per configuration.

## Field context

The paper sits in the long-standing catastrophic-forgetting literature (McCloskey and Cohen; French; Kirkpatrick et al.'s EWC) that it traces from image-classification continual learning through to sequential language-model fine-tuning and continual pretraining, and it explicitly distinguishes its setting from two adjacent lines of work: standard continual-learning benchmarks for LLMs, which mostly measure transfer or performance shift across heterogeneous downstream tasks rather than retention of specific learned associations, and sequential model-editing benchmarks, which study targeted parameter edits rather than task-wise supervised fine-tuning. Its closest cited precedent, Dark Experience Replay (Buzzega et al., 2020), already showed that combining rehearsal with distillation works well, but the paper's stated departure is to study the broader combinatorial space of anchor and allocation-rule compositions systematically rather than propose one more standalone or paired mechanism. No paper currently in this corpus works on continual learning, catastrophic forgetting, or weight-based memorization specifically, so this is a new methods-area entry point for the corpus.

## Critical discussion

The factorial design, the immediate-acquisition control, and the TSH self-validation together make this an unusually well-instrumented empirical study of a genuinely combinatorial question, and the central finding, that replay and merged LoRA interact super-additively across three datasets built from qualitatively different content (arbitrary symbols, fictional natural-language facts, real-world facts), is a specific and load-bearing result rather than a single lucky configuration.

The headline "28-fold improvement" and the strong composition results should be read against how low the achieved numbers still are in absolute terms: even the fully composed best method retains only 34.9% of 100 tasks' worth of associations on average, and the strongest per-dataset composition tops out at 54.8% (Real-QA) and as low as 23.2% (Symbol-QA); a 28-fold multiplier over a 1.2% baseline is arithmetically large but still describes a method that forgets roughly two-thirds of what it was taught, and every reported memory-decay curve (Figure 1) continues declining rather than plateauing, so the paper's own framing that composition "delays forgetting" rather than solving it is the accurate one and should anchor how the main results are read. All experiments use a single base model and scale (Qwen3-4B-Base); the paper does not report whether the composition effects, especially the specific negative SI-times-merged-LoRA interaction attributed to stale importance references, would hold at a different model scale or architecture family (`narrow-evaluation`). The TSH procedure, while self-validated, still means the full-scale 100-task results are conditioned on hyperparameters and survivors chosen using 10-, 20-, and 50-task horizon performance; the reported rank correlation is reassuring but is itself computed only over the 17 compositions that happened to be evaluated at both horizons, not over the full original 90-configuration space, so some risk of the search's early-stage bias propagating into the final comparison cannot be fully excluded from the text. The general-capability results (O-LoRA preserving more downstream benchmark accuracy than merged LoRA despite similar memorization) surface a real tension the paper reports honestly rather than downplays: the paper's own headline recommendation (all three anchors plus merged LoRA) is not the same configuration that best preserves general capability, and the paper does not resolve or further explore this trade-off beyond reporting it.

What remains well supported after these caveats: that no single continual-learning mechanism tested here retains information well at a 100-task horizon, that composing complementary anchors with merged LoRA substantially and consistently outperforms any standalone mechanism across three qualitatively different datasets, that replay and merged LoRA specifically are the two largest and most reliably interacting contributors, and that stronger memorization does not imply preserved general capability, all backed by significance-tested, multi-dataset, control-checked evidence rather than a single anecdotal comparison.

## Relevance to us

This paper is direct evidence, not for physics specifically but for the general tension between writable parametric memory and other memory mechanisms (retrieval, frozen-backbone lookup), on how lossy and effortful weight-based continual memorization currently is: even the best-engineered composition of three anchors plus a merge-based LoRA allocation rule, tuned via a validated search procedure, retains only about a third of 100 sequentially taught associations on a 4B model, and general capability degrades substantially in every configuration tested. For any design choosing between updating model weights to internalize new information (continual SFT, the mechanism this paper studies) versus a retrieval- or lookup-based alternative that leaves the backbone frozen, this paper's numbers are a useful, concrete baseline for how much engineering effort weight-based memorization currently requires to reach modest retention, and how much of that retention is still lost as the number of updates grows. It does not test whether a frozen-backbone writable-memory approach would do better under the same 100-task protocol, so it supports the tension rather than resolving it.
