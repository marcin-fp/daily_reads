## What this paper is about

Constructive quantum field theory has a decades-old open problem at its center — the Clay Millennium "mass gap" problem for Yang-Mills theory — and a much older, already-solved warm-up case: rigorously constructing the free (non-interacting) bosonic quantum field in four Euclidean dimensions and proving it satisfies the Osterwalder-Schrader/Glimm-Jaffe axioms that let you "Wick rotate" a Euclidean statistical field theory into an honest relativistic quantum theory. This paper takes that already-known, textbook-level result and translates it into Lean 4, a language whose proofs are checked mechanically rather than by human referees, using AI coding assistants (Claude Code, GPT, Gemini) as the primary labor for writing the formal proof scripts. The paper is explicitly framed as a proof of concept — not a new physics result, but a demonstration that AI-assisted formalization of real mathematical-physics arguments is now practical — and spends roughly half its length reporting, in concrete and often self-critical detail, what actually worked and didn't work when using current AI tools for this kind of research-adjacent formal proof.

## Extended summary

The mathematical content follows the Glimm-Jaffe measure-theoretic approach: define the free massive scalar field as a Gaussian measure on the space of tempered distributions (rather than on ordinary functions, since the field configuration is almost surely too singular to be an ordinary function in D≥2 dimensions), using Minlos' theorem, which guarantees that any continuous, positive-definite, normalized functional on a nuclear space (here, Schwartz test functions) is the characteristic functional of a unique probability measure on that space's dual. The paper explains, at a level accessible to a physicist reader, why this machinery (nuclear spaces, Gel'fand triples) is needed rather than a naive infinite-dimensional Lebesgue-measure-times-Boltzmann-weight construction (which doesn't exist), and why Schwartz space specifically was chosen over other candidate function spaces largely because it is already well-developed in Lean's Mathlib library. The paper then states and formalizes the five Glimm-Jaffe (OS) axioms — analyticity (OS0), regularity (OS1), Euclidean invariance (OS2), reflection positivity (OS3), and ergodicity (OS4) — and proves the constructed free-field measure satisfies all five (full formal proofs in Appendix B). The original released formalization assumed three results as unproven Lean axioms (Minlos' theorem, the nuclear property of Schwartz space, and Goursat's theorem); by the time of this writing, the paper reports, contributions from the authors' own group and from the wider Lean community have proven all three, so the current repository state establishes the OS/GJ axioms using only Lean and Mathlib, with no remaining unproven assumptions — and the paper explicitly names the independent contributors (Matteo Cipollina; the first author separately) who produced axiom-free variants, treating this external validation as itself part of the paper's evidentiary record.

Section 5 ("Methods") and Section 6.1 ("Advice for now") report the project's actual workflow. The project began in July 2025 with two authors writing an informal, "sketchy" proof outline and feeding it to GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5, which produced low-quality but genuinely useful suggestions of definitions and needed theorems given how little Lean code exists in typical training corpora. Core definitions (field configuration, Euclidean group, generating functional) were then hand-coded before AI-assisted proof-writing began in earnest. The authors report that model capability rose sharply over the project's 9-month span, with the introduction of Claude Code in VS Code (end of September 2025) marking "the single biggest jump," and Claude Code Opus 4.6 becoming their primary tool by the end. A specific, recurring failure mode they had to actively correct for was coding agents abandoning a difficult proof attempt and deleting partial work rather than persisting or explaining the blocker — addressed with an explicit prompt instruction not to delete substantial work without first explaining the specific difficulty encountered.

The paper's central reported methodological innovation is a "backward-chaining" strategy: when an agent got stuck proving a lemma, rather than leaving an incomplete proof marker (`sorry`), the authors had the agent propose a simpler "helper lemma" which, if merely assumed as a temporary Lean axiom, would let the original proof go through — accepting these as tracked axioms (added to a running list for later proof) rather than as unverified `sorry`s, specifically so the model would not lose focus on the immediate goal. The critical safeguard against this strategy generating false or intractable helper lemmas was cross-model validation: a conjecture generated by Claude would be checked by Gemini and GPT (via purpose-built MCP tooling), with their feedback relayed back, and difficult proof plans reviewed the same way — the authors report Claude was generally the strongest at actually writing Lean code while Gemini gave more reliable mathematical/physical correctness judgments. A second refinement, found effective in later project stages, was instructing agents to seek helper lemmas stated as generally as possible (in terms of generic mathematical objects rather than project-specific ones), which both reduced the rate of false conjectures and made the resulting lemmas easier to prove. The paper documents a concrete, consequential failure this process was needed to catch: the covariance was originally defined, by unreflective adoption of "universal practice in physics," as the Fourier transform of a simple momentum-space expression — a conditionally convergent integral that a newly-joined mathematician team member identified as potentially interpretable as zero in some contexts, later fixed via an absolutely-convergent Bessel-function-based definition.

Section 5.1 catalogs specific, still-current limitations of the AI tooling as of early 2026: models can confidently apply "nearly but not quite right" analogies from more familiar mathematical settings (e.g., treating a conditionally convergent integral as absolutely convergent, or misapplying complex-matrix results to Grassmann-algebra matrices) even when directly instructed otherwise, requiring active human vigilance on definitional correctness rather than just proof correctness; long-form proof decomposition remains constrained by context limits, producing Lean code that is "merely true" rather than legible or Mathlib-quality without substantial human-imposed structure; agent-generated documentation and file summaries are unreliable because models rely heavily on (often stale) docstrings/comments rather than genuinely re-reading code, causing summaries to fixate on trivial bookkeeping while omitting the actual mathematical content; and models can spend hours in unproductive search on a difficult theorem, making little progress until a human supplies a clearer proof route, after which implementation proceeds quickly. Section 6 closes with speculative but explicitly labeled-as-such discussion of near-term formalization targets (the OS reconstruction theorem, 2D interacting P(φ)₂ theory, fermionic and gauge theories, quantum information theory results) and broader observations about formalization infrastructure (Mathlib's ~2,300-PR review backlog as a bottleneck; the risk of fragmented, mutually incompatible parallel formalization efforts; the absence of good benchmarks for tracking how much of mathematics/physics has actually been formalized, as distinct from benchmarks like miniF2F that measure AI proving capability on already-formalized statement sets).

## Learnings

The most transferable idea is the backward-chaining-with-cross-model-validation workflow itself: rather than either fully trusting an AI-proposed lemma or blocking on every unprovable step, the authors converted "the agent got stuck" into "the agent proposes a named, trackable assumption," gated that assumption through review by differently-trained models before accepting it, and maintained a running list of accepted-but-unproven axioms to close out later. This is a concrete, reusable pattern for any AI-assisted formal-verification project (not specific to physics or even to Lean) for managing the tension between forward research momentum and rigor: it converts an unstructured failure mode (silent `sorry`s, or agents deleting work) into a structured, auditable backlog of explicit assumptions.

A second learning, specific but mechanistically clear, is the paper's naming of a subtle failure mode distinct from outright incorrect proofs: models can be led astray by analogies that are "nearly but not quite right" — applying a familiar result from a more common mathematical setting to a superficially similar but technically different one (Grassmann algebras vs. complex matrices; conditional vs. absolute convergence) — and that this failure mode survives explicit instruction to the contrary, because the model's training-derived intuition for what looks "standard" can override task-specific guidance. Their reported countermeasure, deliberately constructing an auxiliary unit-test-style consequence of a new definition or lemma (not dictated by the original proof scaffold) rather than merely asking the model to paraphrase its own formal statement back into English, is a specific, actionable debugging technique: paraphrase-checking did not reliably surface these errors, while using the new object to prove something independently did.

A third learning is the explicit, self-documented example of a substantive definitional error (the conditionally-convergent covariance definition) being caught only once a mathematician with the relevant technical background joined the project and noticed a physics community's "universal practice" was mathematically unsound in this specific formal setting — a concrete illustration of the paper's broader claim that human domain expertise remains most valuable specifically at the level of catching incorrect or silently-shifted mathematical context, a failure mode the paper argues is hard to detect by inspection alone since "the types still line up and everything compiles."

## Verification

Verification in this paper operates on two distinct levels that are worth separating clearly. At the level of formal correctness, verification is airtight by construction: any theorem the Lean kernel accepts as proved is proved, given whatever axioms were assumed — this is a categorically stronger correctness guarantee than essentially any other paper in this corpus can offer, since it does not rest on human peer review, statistical evaluation, or an LLM judge, but on mechanical proof-checking. The paper is transparent about exactly which axioms were assumed in its original release (Minlos' theorem, nuclear property of Schwartz space, Goursat's theorem) and reports, with external validation from named independent contributors outside the authors' own group, that all three have since been proven, yielding a fully axiom-free (modulo Mathlib and Lean's own foundations) construction — a genuinely strong and checkable verification story, strengthened rather than weakened by inviting outside scrutiny and reporting the result candidly. At the second level — whether the formalized statement actually captures the intended physical/mathematical content, sometimes called the "formalization gap" or autoformalization-correctness problem — verification is necessarily a matter of human (and AI-assisted human) judgment rather than mechanical guarantee, since Lean can certify that a formal statement follows from formal axioms but cannot certify that the formal statement means what the authors intended it to mean. The paper directly illustrates this risk with its own reported near-miss (the conditionally-convergent covariance definition, which "compiled" under the wrong convergence assumption before a team member caught the physical/mathematical error), and its main disclosed mitigation strategy — auxiliary "unit test" consequences of new definitions, rather than relying on paraphrase-back-to-English translation, which the authors explicitly state "did not reliably catch transcription or interpretation errors" — is a specific, actionable practice rather than a vague assurance.

## Field context

The paper positions itself within a genuinely novel niche it explicitly claims: "we know of no previous formalizations of constructive QFT," differentiating it from the closely related and directly-used Brownian motion formalization project (Degenne et al.), whose Kolmogorov Extension theorem is imported into this paper's proof of Minlos' theorem. It surveys the three major competing axiomatic frameworks for QFT (Wightman, Osterwalder-Schrader/Glimm-Jaffe, and Haag-Kastler algebraic QFT) and gives an explicit, reasoned justification for choosing the Glimm-Jaffe measure-theoretic OS variant specifically because it reduces QFT construction to measure theory — an area Mathlib already covers reasonably well — rather than to the operator-algebra and Hilbert-space machinery Wightman/Haag-Kastler would require, which Mathlib presently lacks; this is a specific, checkable claim about current library coverage driving a substantive methodological choice, not an arbitrary preference. It situates its own AI-methodology narrative within the broader AI-for-theorem-proving (AITP) landscape, distinguishing reinforcement-learning/game-search approaches to proof search (AlphaProof, in the lineage of AlphaGo/AlphaZero) from the autoformalization/language-translation paradigm this project instead relies on (treating formalization as translation from informal to formal language, aided by general-purpose coding-agent LLMs rather than a specialized proof-search RL system). Within this batch's cluster of AI-for-science reviews (the prior session's survey of AI-for-research-automation, 2605.18661, and the Data Darwinism pretraining-data paper, 2602.07824), this paper is a concrete, single-project instance of exactly the "idea generation → literature review → verification" lifecycle those broader surveys describe in the abstract, here specifically for the formal-mathematics/theoretical-physics research stage that neither of those other papers' surveyed systems directly cover, and it independently corroborates a theme those surveys raise structurally: that AI systems are far better at generating candidate proof steps and helper-lemma proposals than at independently judging which of those candidates are actually correct, which is precisely why this paper's cross-model validation protocol exists as a load-bearing safeguard rather than an optional nicety.

## Critical discussion

As a case-study/methods paper rather than a controlled empirical study, the appropriate standard is whether its narrative claims are well-grounded in the concrete evidence it presents, not whether it establishes a generalizable causal claim across many projects. On that standard, the paper is largely careful: its strongest, best-supported claim (the mathematical result itself, and its subsequent axiom-free strengthening validated by named independent contributors) rests on the Lean kernel's mechanical guarantee rather than on the authors' own assessment, which is about as strong an evidentiary basis as a formal-methods claim can have. The paper's methodological claims about AI capability trajectory, however, are reported qualitatively and retrospectively rather than measured: statements like Claude Code being "the single biggest jump" in capability, or that "the early 2026 tools are noticeably better still" than mid-2025 tools, are the authors' subjective impressions from lived project experience rather than a quantified comparison (e.g., lines of verified code per unit time, proof-attempt success rates across model versions, or a controlled before/after test on a fixed benchmark of lemmas). This is an understandable limitation for a single, non-repeatable, multi-month research project rather than a designed experiment, and the paper does not claim otherwise, but a reader should treat the specific capability-trajectory claims as an informed practitioner's testimony rather than as measured evidence, and should be cautious about generalizing from this one project (one research group, one Lean codebase, one class of mathematical content) to claims about AI-assisted formalization capability more broadly.

The paper is also commendably candid about a limitation that partially qualifies its own "proof of concept" framing: the resulting Lean code, by the authors' own admission, still falls short of "the foundational standards of Mathlib or PhysLean" in readability and organization unless substantial human effort is imposed, and long-horizon proof planning remains a capability gap the human authors, not the AI agents, had to supply (the paper states plainly that "at least with current tooling, formalization tends to follow known human proofs" rather than discovering new arguments). This tempers the paper's more forward-looking claims about near-future autoformalization becoming fully automatic, since the concrete project described here still required substantial, non-automatable human judgment at multiple specific points (definitional correctness checking, unproductive-search redirection, and proof architecture), which the paper documents honestly rather than glossing over in service of a stronger "AI can now do this" narrative.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the central proof-of-concept claim — that a real, previously-unformalized piece of research-level mathematical physics can now be formalized in Lean with AI coding assistants doing a substantial share of the proof-writing labor, under a human-supervised workflow with specific, describable safeguards against AI-introduced errors — is directly demonstrated by the existence of the completed, externally-scrutinized, and (in its current form) axiom-free formalization itself, which is a stronger form of evidence than most "AI helped us do X" claims in the broader literature can offer, precisely because the final artifact's correctness does not depend on trusting the authors' or the AI's say-so.
