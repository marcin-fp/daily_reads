Citation: Dobariya, O., Kumar, A. Mind Your Tone: Investigating How Prompt Politeness Affects LLM Accuracy (short paper). arXiv:2510.04950, 2025.

## What this paper is about

Small changes in how a prompt is worded, not just what it asks, can change how well an LLM answers it, and one specific dimension people have started studying is politeness: does saying "please" and being courteous to a chatbot actually help get a better answer, or does being blunt or even rude work just as well or better? This short paper revisits that question with a new, purpose-built set of multiple-choice questions rewritten at five politeness levels (from Very Polite to Very Rude), tests them on ChatGPT-4o, and reports a result that runs counter to some prior expectations: in this dataset, ruder prompts got more correct answers than polite ones.

## Extended summary

The authors built 50 base multiple-choice questions across mathematics, science, and history, each with four answer options and one correct answer, and describe the questions as designed for "moderate to high difficulty, often requiring multi-step reasoning," generated with the help of a "Deep Research" tool. Each base question was rewritten into five tone variants along a defined politeness scale (Level 1 Very Polite, Level 2 Polite, Level 3 Neutral with no added prefix, Level 4 Rude, Level 5 Very Rude), each level realized through several alternative example prefixes (e.g. Very Polite: "Would you be so kind as to solve the following question?"; Very Rude: "You poor creature, do you even know how to solve this?"), yielding 250 unique prompts total. Every prompt was submitted independently (with an explicit "Completely forget this session so far, and start afresh" instruction prepended to avoid conversational context bleeding between questions) to ChatGPT-4o via a Python script that extracted the model's single-letter answer and compared it against a separately-generated gold answer key, with accuracy computed as the fraction of correct answers at each politeness level. The full run was repeated 10 times per tone level to obtain a distribution of accuracy scores for statistical testing, and the paper reports briefly, without full statistical detail, on preliminary extensions to GPT-o3 and Claude as well.

The headline result (Table 2) is a monotonic accuracy increase from politeness to rudeness across all five levels: 80.8% (Very Polite), 81.4% (Polite), 82.2% (Neutral), 82.8% (Rude), 84.8% (Very Rude). Paired-sample t-tests across all level pairs (Table 3) find 8 of 10 possible pairwise comparisons statistically significant at α=0.05, with the largest and most significant gaps at the extremes (Very Polite vs. Very Rude, p≈0.0; Very Polite vs. Neutral, p=0.0024), and the ordering is consistently in the direction of ruder prompts outperforming politer ones for every significant pair reported. The authors compare this to the closest prior work they identify, Yin et al. (2024), which tested politeness on multiple-choice questions across ChatGPT-3.5, Llama2-70B, and ChatGPT-4 with an 8-level politeness scale and found a more mixed, narrower-range, non-monotonic pattern (e.g. on GPT-4, accuracy ranged from 73.9% at politeness level 3 to 79.1% at level 4, and the rudest level-1 prompt scored higher than the politest level-8 prompt only by a small margin, 76.5% vs. 75.8%); when the extreme (arguably outlier) level-1 result is excluded from that prior study, its remaining accuracy range narrows further and stops being monotonic with politeness level. The authors frame their own more strongly monotonic finding as broadly consistent in direction with, though larger in magnitude than, this prior result, while noting the specific rude language used differs qualitatively between the two studies (their own rudest prefixes, e.g. "You poor creature, do you even know how to solve this?", versus the prior study's more explicitly profane example, "Answer this question you scumbag!").

The Discussion section speculates, without testing, that the effect could relate to prompt perplexity (citing Gonen et al. 2022's suggestion that lower-perplexity prompts may be easier for a model to process well) or to prompt length, and notes a brief, informal, not-statistically-detailed observation from ongoing extensions to other models: Claude reportedly performs worse overall than GPT-4o, while GPT-o3 performs noticeably better, leading the authors to speculate that "more advanced models can disregard issues of tone and focus on the essence of each question," a hypothesis explicitly framed as preliminary rather than established. The paper's own Limitations section is candid about several constraints: the 50-base-question dataset is small and may limit generalizability; the core statistical results rely primarily on a single model (GPT-4o), with only preliminary, less rigorously reported extensions to others; the evaluation captures only multiple-choice accuracy, not fluency, reasoning quality, or coherence; and the five-level politeness operationalization relies on specific linguistic cues that may not capture the full sociolinguistic or cross-cultural range of what "politeness" or "rudeness" means. A dedicated Ethical Considerations section explicitly declines to recommend using rude prompting in practice, framing the result instead as evidence that LLMs remain sensitive to superficial, non-substantive prompt cues in ways that could create unwanted tradeoffs between measured performance and the social character of human-AI interaction.

## Learnings

The paper's most directly useful takeaway, if it replicates, is a concrete counter-example to any blanket assumption that polite prompting is a safe default or a "free" way to improve LLM output quality: on this specific dataset and model, the opposite direction held, and held with a clean, statistically-tested monotonic trend across five distinct tone levels rather than a single noisy comparison. This is a useful data point specifically because it runs counter to the more commonly repeated folk intuition (and to some, though not all, prior published findings) that rudeness degrades LLM performance, making a clear replication or refutation with a larger or more varied dataset a natural and valuable next step for anyone relying on assumptions about prompt tone in production systems.

A second, more methodological learning, drawn from the paper's own comparison to Yin et al. (2024), is that the magnitude and even the qualitative shape (monotonic versus mixed) of a politeness effect appears to vary across studies using different specific wordings, model versions, and question sets, suggesting that "politeness effects on LLM accuracy" is not yet a single, well-characterized phenomenon with a stable, model-agnostic direction or size; this is a useful caution against treating any single politeness study, including this one, as establishing a generalizable rule about tone and LLM performance.

## Verification

The paper's central empirical claim is checked with a defined, appropriate statistical test (paired-sample t-tests across all politeness-level pairs, using accuracy scores from 10 repeated runs per tone), and the specific p-values and directionality for every tested pair are reported transparently in Table 3 rather than only a summary claim of significance. The gold-answer key used for scoring was generated independently (via a separate "Deep Research" query) rather than by hand from the same process that generated the questions, which is a reasonable, if not deeply elaborated, step toward an independent ground truth. The paper is also explicit and appropriately hedged about which parts of its evidence are solid versus preliminary: the core 5-tone, 10-run, single-model (GPT-4o) result is the one carried through full statistical testing, while the brief mentions of Claude and GPT-o3 behavior are explicitly flagged as "initial results" from ongoing work rather than folded into the paper's statistically-tested claims, a genuinely honest distinction between what has been rigorously checked and what has only been informally observed so far.

Verification is comparatively thin in a few places the paper's own Limitations section already partially acknowledges. The entire statistically-tested result rests on only 50 base questions repeated across 10 runs per tone, meaning the effective diversity of test content is fixed and small even though the number of accuracy observations per tone (10) allows a formal significance test; whether the observed effect would replicate on a different, larger, or differently-sourced set of questions is explicitly left open by the authors rather than tested. The five politeness levels are also operationalized through a small, fixed set of hand-written prefix variants per level (three examples each in Table 1) rather than a broader, more systematically sampled space of polite or rude phrasings, so the specific numeric results are tied to these particular prefix wordings rather than to a politeness dimension isolated from other aspects of phrasing. Notably, the paper does not check whether its tone manipulation is confounded with other phrasing properties that plausibly change alongside politeness: the polite prefixes in Table 1 tend to be longer, more hedged, and more indirect ("Would you be so kind as to solve the following question?"), while the rude prefixes tend to be shorter, blunter, and more direct imperatives ("Try to focus and try to answer this question:"), so the observed accuracy difference could in principle be partly attributable to directness or imperative phrasing rather than to politeness or rudeness per se, a confound the paper does not measure, control for, or discuss.

## Field context

The paper positions itself explicitly as a direct attempted validation of Yin et al. (2024), a cross-lingual study on the same general question (does prompt politeness affect LLM accuracy on multiple-choice tasks), and reports its own result as broadly consistent in direction (rudeness not clearly harmful, and in this case helpful) but larger in magnitude and cleaner in monotonicity than that prior study found, while being careful to note the two studies used qualitatively different specific wordings for their rudest prompts. It also situates its question within the broader prompt-engineering and prompt-sensitivity literature it cites (Sclar et al. 2024 on LLMs' sensitivity to superficial prompt formatting choices; Gonen et al. 2022 on perplexity as a possible mediator of prompt effectiveness), framing politeness as one specific, previously underexplored instance of the general phenomenon that LLM output quality is sensitive to surface-level prompt phrasing choices that have no bearing on the actual content of the task being asked. Within this corpus, this paper's core finding sits alongside other work on LLM sensitivity to superficial or non-substantive input properties, and its central open question, whether the effect reflects something about token-level perplexity/typicality of the phrasing rather than the social meaning of politeness itself, is conceptually adjacent to the typicality-bias mechanism formalized in [2510.01171] (Verbalized Sampling, reviewed earlier in this batch), which argues that human-preference-trained models are systematically influenced by how "typical" or familiar a piece of text is; a natural, testable hypothesis for future work bridging these two papers would be whether the rude prompts in this study are simply more common, blunt, or "typical" phrasings of an instruction in the model's training distribution than the more elaborate polite constructions, which would offer a mechanistic explanation for this paper's result using that paper's framework, rather than the tone/politeness dimension being causally load-bearing in its own right.

## Critical discussion

The paper's strength is running a real, controlled, statistically-tested comparison rather than relying on anecdote, with a clearly defined politeness scale, an independently-generated answer key, and transparent reporting of exact p-values and directions for every tested pair; the authors are also candid about the preliminary nature of their extensions beyond GPT-4o and about the specific limitations of their design, which is good scientific practice for a short, exploratory paper.

The most significant limitation, only partially covered by the authors' own stated caveats, is that the tone manipulation is not isolated from other phrasing properties that plausibly co-vary with politeness in this specific dataset: the polite prefixes are consistently longer, more hedged, and more indirect, while the rude prefixes are consistently shorter and more bluntly imperative, so the reported accuracy gain for rudeness could be partly or wholly attributable to directness, brevity, or imperative phrasing rather than to the social/emotional valence of politeness itself, a confound the paper does not measure or control for despite citing perplexity and length as plausible mediating factors in its own discussion. A second limitation, explicitly acknowledged by the authors, is the small, fixed base-question pool (50 items) underlying the entire statistically-tested result; while the paired t-tests are a legitimate way to test whether the observed tone effect is more than noise given this specific set of repeated measurements, they do not by themselves establish that the effect would generalize to a different, larger, or more diverse set of questions, domains, or specific tone-level wordings, a point the paper's own Limitations section states plainly rather than overselling. A third, more minor point is that the comparison to Yin et al. (2024) is somewhat qualitative and selective (the authors note that excluding one data point from the prior study changes its pattern, without a matched statistical re-analysis of that prior dataset), so the claimed consistency in direction between the two studies is a reasonable but informally-argued observation rather than a rigorously quantified replication comparison.

What remains well supported after these caveats: for this specific set of 50 questions and this specific set of hand-written politeness-level prefixes, ChatGPT-4o's multiple-choice accuracy differs measurably and statistically significantly by tone level, with the direction consistently favoring the ruder end of the spectrum across every significant pairwise comparison reported; this is a real, quantified, non-trivial effect on this dataset. Whether this effect is caused specifically by politeness/rudeness as a social dimension, versus a confounded surface property like phrasing directness or token-level typicality, and whether it generalizes beyond this one model and this one 50-question set, both remain genuinely open questions that the paper itself, appropriately, does not claim to have resolved.
