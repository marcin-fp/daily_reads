Citation: Mirzadeh, I., Alizadeh, K., Shahrokhi, H., Tuzel, O., Bengio, S., and Farajtabar, M. GSM-Symbolic: Understanding the Limitations of Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), 2025.

## What this paper is about

GSM8K, a popular benchmark of grade-school math word problems, reports a single accuracy number per model on a fixed set of 1,319 questions. This paper asks whether that single number is a reliable, stable measure of a model's mathematical reasoning, or whether it hides a lot of variance that a fixed test set can't reveal. The authors build GSM-Symbolic, a version of 100 GSM8K questions turned into parameterized templates (same question structure, but names and numbers can be resampled), so that instead of one score per model they get a whole distribution of scores across many resampled variants of the same underlying questions. Using this tool across 25 open and closed models, they find real variance from question to question, show that changing only the numbers (not the names) hurts performance much more, show that adding more clauses to a question makes things worse faster than expected, and — their most striking result — show that adding a single sentence of information that sounds relevant but doesn't actually change the required calculation causes large accuracy drops across every model tested, a problem that does not go away even when the model is shown correctly-worked examples of the exact same kind of question.

## Extended summary

Starting from 100 questions sampled from the GSM8K test set, the authors hand-annotate each into a symbolic template: variables (names, numbers) with defined domains and conditions (e.g., divisibility constraints so the answer is always a whole number), verified by a multi-step quality-control pipeline — checking that none of the original question's specific values leak into the template, that the original values still satisfy all stated conditions and reproduce the original answer, manually reviewing 10 random generated samples per template, and, after running all models, manually re-reviewing any question that fewer than two models answered correctly. This yields 100 templates, each instantiated into 50 resampled variants (5,000 examples per benchmark variant), evaluated with standard 8-shot chain-of-thought prompting and greedy decoding (except OpenAI's o1 models, whose API does not expose greedy decoding) across more than 20 open models (2B-27B parameters: Gemma/Gemma2, Phi/Phi-3/Phi-3.5, Mistral/Mathstral, Llama3) plus GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o1-mini, and o1-preview — roughly 500 total evaluation runs.

The first result (Sec. 4.1) is that all 25 models show non-negligible performance variance across the 50 resampled sets built from the same 100 underlying questions (e.g., a roughly 12-point gap between best and worst set for Gemma2-9B, about 15 points for Phi-3.5-mini), even though the only thing changing between instances is names and numbers, not the required reasoning steps. The paper additionally observes that for 21 of 25 models, the model's original GSM8K accuracy on those same 100 questions falls more than one standard deviation from the center of its own GSM-Symbolic distribution, usually on the higher (easier) side, which the authors interpret as consistent with possible data contamination (the original, fixed GSM8K questions having leaked into training data), while noting the drop is negligible for a few models (Llama3-8B, GPT-4o) whose original-question performance sits closer to the center of their resampled distribution.

Section 4.2 separates the effect of changing only proper names (persons, places, foods, currencies) from changing only numerical values: across nearly all models, performance and variance are close to the original GSM8K numbers when only names change, but performance drops more and variance increases when only numbers change, and drops further still when both are changed together — described by the authors as evidence that models are more robust to superficial lexical variation than to variation in the actual quantities involved in the calculation. Section 4.3 varies question difficulty directly by adding or removing clauses (GSM-Symbolic-Minus-1 through Plus-2, illustrated with a phone-call pricing example that adds successive pricing tiers and a discount condition): across all models, average accuracy declines and variance increases monotonically as clauses are added, with the rate of accuracy decline appearing to accelerate faster than the linear growth in the number of required reasoning steps.

Section 4.4 introduces GSM-NoOp, built by adding a clause to a GSM-Symbolic template that sounds relevant to the scenario but does not affect the arithmetic needed to answer the question (e.g., "five of them were a bit smaller than average" appended to a kiwi-counting problem, which several models, including o1-mini and Llama3-8B in the paper's worked example, incorrectly subtract from the total). Every tested model shows a "catastrophic" accuracy drop on GSM-NoOp relative to GSM-Symbolic, up to 65% for the smallest model tested (Phi-3-mini) and substantial (double-digit percentage point) drops even for o1-preview, one of the strongest models tested. To test whether this is simply a few-shot prompting artifact, the authors re-run the evaluation providing 8 in-context shots that are either GSM-Symbolic variants of the exact same question (so the correct reasoning chain, including how to ignore the No-Op clause, is directly demonstrated) or GSM-NoOp variants of different questions (so the shots demonstrate the general pattern of ignoring No-Op clauses): in both cases, performance on the target GSM-NoOp question remains essentially unchanged (within the standard deviation of the original GSM-NoOp result), though a few models perform notably better under the same-question-shot condition despite performing worse on GSM8K/GSM-Symbolic overall, an observation the authors flag as interesting without fully explaining it.

The Conclusion frames all of this as evidence that current LLMs' apparent mathematical reasoning is "fragile" and "more akin to sophisticated pattern matching rather than true logical reasoning," calling for further research toward more formal, robust reasoning. The paper's data and code (templates and generated data) are released on GitHub.

## Learnings

Turning a fixed benchmark question into a resampleable template and looking at the resulting distribution, rather than trusting a single accuracy number, reveals meaningful hidden variance: several open models showed roughly 12-15 percentage-point swings in accuracy across 50 resampled variants built from the same 100 underlying question structures. A single-point accuracy score on a fixed test set is a noisier estimate of "true" performance than it appears, and generating a template family with the same structure but resampled surface values is a cheap, general-purpose way to expose that noise for any benchmark, not just this one.

Sensitivity to changing only the numbers in a problem, versus changing only the names, is a specific and easily-replicated diagnostic: across nearly all 25 models tested, accuracy and variance stayed close to baseline when only proper names changed, but degraded substantially when only numeric values changed. This asymmetry is a concrete, cheap stress test for whether a model's apparent success on a reasoning problem is tracking the actual quantitative structure of the problem or a more superficial match to a memorized or closely-seen surface form.

A single clause that sounds relevant to a problem's scenario but carries no operational weight caused large, consistent accuracy drops (up to 65%) across every model tested, including the strongest ones, and — this is the more surprising part — the drop was not repaired by showing the model several in-context examples that explicitly demonstrated the correct way to handle exactly this kind of irrelevant clause, whether from the same question or from other similar questions. "Ignore irrelevant information" did not transfer as a general in-context-learnable skill in this setup, which is a stronger and more specific claim than merely "irrelevant context can hurt performance" (already known from prior work the paper cites) — it demonstrates the failure persists even when the fix is handed to the model directly in its context.

## Verification

This paper's own verification content is mostly about the correctness of its benchmark construction, not about checking an LLM's reasoning trace: each symbolic template comes with explicit variable domains and conditions (e.g., a divisibility constraint) that guarantee, by construction, that any sampled instantiation has a well-defined, correct final answer, which is a deterministic, checkable property of the benchmark itself rather than a judgment call. On top of this deterministic construction, the authors layer a multi-step quality-control process: automated checks that the template doesn't leak the original question's specific values, that the original values satisfy the template's own conditions and reproduce the original answer, manual review of 10 random generated instances per template, and — notably — a final automated pass after evaluating all models, in which any question that fewer than two models answered correctly is manually re-reviewed for a possible annotation error. This last check is a pragmatic, if somewhat unusual, form of verification: using model consensus as a signal that a generated test item might itself be broken, rather than assuming every generated item is automatically valid. The paper does not, however, apply a comparable level of statistical rigor to its main causal claims about model behavior (see Critical discussion): the central data-contamination inference in Section 4.1 rests on a visual/distributional observation (original accuracy falling outside one standard deviation of the resampled distribution, "usually" on the favorable side) rather than a formal hypothesis test across the full set of models.

## Field context

The paper builds directly on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and explicitly positions itself against several other recent efforts to stress-test or replace it: iGSM (a synthetic pipeline capturing parameter dependencies via a hierarchical graph structure), GSM-IC (showing irrelevant context degrades performance, with a focus on prompting techniques rather than templated generation), GSM-Plus (introduces GSM8K variants but without symbolic templates or adjustable difficulty), and GSM1K (mirrors GSM8K's style to detect overfitting but is not publicly released) — the paper's stated point of differentiation is that its finding (models fail even when given multiple shots of the exact same question) is a "more fundamental issue" than prompting-technique sensitivity alone. It also situates itself within a broader literature questioning whether LLM reasoning is formal or a form of sophisticated pattern-matching (citing, among others, work on token bias, on the exponential decay of multi-token correctness probability, on transformers' theoretical limitations on non-regular tasks, and a causal-robustness study of math word problems by Stolfo et al. that the paper's own related-work section acknowledges as closely related). None of these specific precedents are yet reviewed in this corpus. Separately, and not part of the paper's own text, this work generated substantial public discussion after publication; the paper's own bibliography already includes one such critical response (an ICLR 2025 blogpost on "more rigorous evaluations of language models"), and a subsequent, more extensive external re-analysis (found via a search conducted for this review, not part of the paper itself) specifically challenges the Section 4.1 contamination inference on statistical grounds — see Critical discussion.

## Critical discussion

The GSM-NoOp result (Sec. 4.4) is this paper's strongest and least disputed finding: it is large (up to 65%), consistent across essentially every model tested including the strongest ones, and specifically shown not to be fixable by in-context demonstrations of the exact skill needed (ignoring an operationally irrelevant clause), which rules out the simplest alternative explanation (the model just needed the trick pointed out). This result stands on its own regardless of how the paper's other, more statistically contested claims hold up.

The data-contamination inference in Section 4.1 is on much weaker ground than its prominence in the paper's framing suggests. The claim rests on an informal distributional observation — the original GSM8K accuracy falls more than one standard deviation from the center of the resampled GSM-Symbolic distribution for 21 of 25 models, "usually" on the favorable side — rather than a formal significance test comparing GSM8K and GSM-Symbolic performance model by model. A dedicated external statistical re-evaluation of exactly this claim (identified via a search for this review, not part of the paper) reports that for 21 of the 25 models, the null hypothesis that GSM8K and GSM-Symbolic performance are equal cannot be rejected, and that of the 4 models where it can be rejected, 3 perform worse (not better) on GSM-Symbolic and only 1 performs better — a pattern that directly undercuts a clean contamination story, since contamination should predict systematically inflated (not mixed-direction) original-benchmark performance. This re-evaluation also raises a distinct, independently plausible alternative explanation this paper does not address: the original GSM8K question's specific numbers were chosen by a human question-writer (plausibly for aesthetic or pedagogical reasons, such as clean divisibility or round numbers), and are not necessarily a representative random draw from the same distribution the template's automated resampling uses, which alone could produce a systematic offset between original and resampled performance without any contamination at all. Given this, the paper's own framing ("hinting at potential data contamination") is more speculative than the surrounding prose's confidence suggests, and should be read as a hypothesis the paper raises rather than one it statistically establishes.

The clause-count (Sec. 4.3) and name-versus-number (Sec. 4.2) results are more straightforwardly interpretable descriptive patterns — consistent monotonic trends across nearly all models — though the paper itself flags, in a footnote, that adding or removing a clause does not correspond precisely to adding or removing exactly one reasoning step, so the "rate of accuracy drop exceeds the growth in required steps" framing should be read qualitatively rather than as a precisely quantified claim.

What remains well supported after these caveats: LLMs of all sizes and vendors tested here show real, non-trivial performance variance across resampled instances of structurally identical questions, are notably more sensitive to changes in numeric values than to changes in names, degrade as more clauses are added, and — most robustly — suffer large, in-context-demonstration-resistant accuracy drops when a plausible-but-irrelevant clause is added to an otherwise-solvable problem. The specific claim that this variance pattern demonstrates data contamination in current frontier models' training data is considerably less well established than the paper's framing suggests, and a published statistical re-analysis specifically disputes it.

## Relevance to us

The GSM-NoOp methodology — add a plausible-sounding but operationally irrelevant clause to an otherwise well-posed problem, and check whether the answer changes when it shouldn't — is a directly transferable stress test for any physics-reasoning evaluation we build, and connects concretely to our own interest (per CONTEXT.md's "physics is not math plus noise" framing) in whether a model can tell which given information actually enters a derivation versus which is context, a red herring, or an idealization detail that shouldn't change the calculation. This paper demonstrates, not merely argues, that such robustness does not reliably transfer from a few matched in-context examples, which is a concrete reason not to assume a physics-reasoning model's demonstrated performance on curated textbook problems (which rarely contain irrelevant detail) will hold up on real derivations or literature-sourced problems that do.

The "resample the template, report the distribution, not a single accuracy number" methodology is equally worth adopting directly for any physics benchmark we build or use for internal evaluation: this paper's own headline variance finding (up to 12-15 accuracy points across resampled variants of the same 100 questions) is a strong, general argument against trusting a single-point score as a stable property of a model, which is squarely in the spirit of our own preference for well-characterized coverage over a single pass/fail number.

The data-contamination critical episode is also a useful, concrete methodological lesson: this paper's own causal inference (variance pattern implies contamination) was made from an eyeballed distributional observation rather than a model-by-model significance test, and a subsequent formal re-analysis found the claim did not hold for the large majority of models tested. If we ever build an analogous argument (for example, "our physics-attuned backbone does worse on held-out variants, therefore the original benchmark leaked into training"), this paper is a specific, citable reminder to run the actual hypothesis test across the full set of comparisons before asserting a causal contamination story, rather than relying on a visually suggestive distribution plot.
