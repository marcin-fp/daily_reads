## What this paper is about

A company building AI for materials discovery argues that the real gap in AI-for-science is not answering hard scientific questions but advancing science: carrying a hypothesis through execution (simulation or physical experiment) to a checkable conclusion, then remembering what was learned for next time. They present MIRA, an AI-scientist system for chemistry, materials, and energy research, built as a coordinated "squad" of specialized agents (literature/hypothesis generation, peer review, simulation, experiment, analysis) that plans and executes multi-step research campaigns, backed by an elastic cloud-compute layer for simulations, an in-house automated wet-lab (the "AI Materials Factory") for physical experiments, and a persistent, compiled memory and wiki so completed projects, including failures, remain reusable. On two external benchmarks it reports the top score at the lowest cost among seven compared agents, and on a specialized drug-discovery benchmark it beats domain-specific tools built for that exact task, then demonstrates the full pipeline on three real research campaigns spanning reaction-network prediction, protein-ligand structure selection, and surface-catalysis DFT calculations.

## Extended summary

MIRA is organized into three layers joined by explicit interfaces: an agent squad (reasoning and task division), an execution layer (MIRA Compute for simulation, the AI Materials Factory for physical experiments), and a consolidation layer (memory and a scientific wiki). The squad follows an "agent-teams" pattern the authors distinguish from ordinary subagents specifically by a shared mailbox and task board: subagents report only to a caller and are blind to each other, while squad members can address one another directly, question each other's findings, and disagree. Roles are functional rather than topical: a supervising agent sets the run's rules; generative roles propose hypotheses from literature, internal data, and the underlying model's own knowledge; reflective roles act as peer reviewers checking correctness, novelty, and whether a proposal is even simulable; an evolutionary role recombines and extends surviving hypotheses; a similarity-checking role deduplicates near-identical proposals; and simulation/experiment roles hand surviving hypotheses to the execution layer. To counter the known failure mode where sequential search converges prematurely on the first plausible explanation, MIRA explicitly assigns members competing hypotheses with refutation of the others as part of their objective, then runs a pairwise tournament among survivors. A "scientific skills" layer sits between agents and execution: versioned, curated procedural knowledge (parameters, convergence criteria, valid-result shapes) for computational and experimental workflows, several backed by the authors' own prior published models rather than general-purpose tools — OA-ReactDiff (an equivariant diffusion model that predicts transition-state geometries directly instead of searching for saddle points from scratch), React-OT (a deterministic optimal-transport replacement for stochastic transition-state sampling), reactive machine-learned interatomic potentials trained on a large Hessian database (used to pre-screen candidates before committing to first-principles calculations), and a property-prediction foundation model trained across experimental measurements. MIRA Compute is described as an elastic, multi-tenant, cloud-native, agent-facing (not human-facing) scheduling plane, with design requirements the paper frames as specifically agent-oriented: idempotent submission, failure legible as structure rather than log prose, self-delivering results, explicit liveness signaling, and credentials kept outside the agent's reach as a defense against prompt injection. The AI Materials Factory is the physical-execution arm, an in-house automated lab whose synthesis/characterization equipment and lab-information systems are addressed by the same squad that runs calculations; the paper is explicit that closed-loop campaigns actually carrying a prediction through synthesis and characterization are not demonstrated in this report and are deferred to "a later report." The memory/wiki layer is framed as compiling rather than retrieving: raw sources (papers, patents, internal data, execution artifacts) are immutable, while a maintained wiki of interlinked pages (entities, methods, comparisons, syntheses, flagged contradictions) is built and periodically linted for gaps and superseded claims; consolidation runs offline, described by analogy to sleep-time compute, distilling completed sessions into wiki entries that later runs can recall.

On ResearchClawBench (an external, paper-grounded end-to-end research benchmark), the authors select the Chemistry, Energy, and Materials domains (4 tasks each, of the benchmark's 10 total domains) and run seven agents, including MIRA, on the same underlying model (GPT-5.4) to isolate agent-harness differences from model differences. MIRA's overall score is 17.51, ahead of ResearchClaw (15.60), EvoScientist (15.00), Codex CLI (14.57), OpenClaw (13.63), ARIS Codex (11.13), and Nanobot (10.90), and it is the only agent scoring above 16; it leads Energy (23.30) and Materials (19.79) and places second in Chemistry (9.45 vs. 9.90 for EvoScientist, in a domain where all seven agents fall within a 3.9-point band). At $0.67 per task it is also the lowest-cost agent in the comparison; the authors note no other agent achieves either a lower cost or a higher score, so MIRA dominates all six alternatives on both axes simultaneously, and that higher spend does not buy back the gap (EvoScientist costs 8.7x more per task for 2.5 fewer points). On the drug-discovery domain of SciAgentArena (comparison-agent results supplied by that benchmark's project lead), MIRA scores 81.1% overall across five task categories, ahead of Claude Code (76.4%), ToolUniverse (69.2%), Biomni (60.1%), and ChemToolAgent (56.3%), leading four of five categories and losing only data preprocessing (82.0 vs. ToolUniverse's 90.6 and Claude Code's 86.6); the paper highlights that MIRA's minimum category score (78.0) is far higher than any comparison agent's minimum (down to 44.9 for ChemToolAgent), arguing this matters because a real drug-discovery campaign requires all five stages in sequence.

Three use-case campaigns are then walked through in detail, each run end-to-end from a single natural-language prompt with no pre-specified pipeline. First, exploring the decomposition network of a γ-ketohydroperoxide (KHP) combustion intermediate: MIRA enumerated 78 then 58 candidate reactions across two network layers, dispatched parallel transition-state searches and intrinsic-reaction-coordinate (IRC) validation, and produced a 59-species, 64-reaction network (8 core reactions) whose predicted lowest-barrier pathway (cyclization at 38.47 kcal/mol, more favorable than direct fragmentation at 40.53 kcal/mol) reproduces the literature-established Korcek mechanism's cyclic-peroxide intermediate and product channels. Second, predicting and selecting among candidate ABL1-imatinib binding-mode structures: a multi-sample ESMFold2-fast run returned three candidates with closely spaced confidence scores (pLDDT 92.61-92.89, pTM 0.9603-0.9612, ipTM 0.9761-0.9766); rather than trusting the ranking alone, MIRA cross-checked protein-ligand interaction patterns (hydrogen bonds, π-stacking, hydrophobic contacts) across all three via ProteinsPlus/PoseEdit/Protoss, found all three converged on the same interaction pattern, and retained the top-ranked sample as representative, while the paper itself explicitly notes this three-way agreement "does not constitute independent cross-model or experimental validation" since all three originate from one model and one prediction run. Third, computing CO adsorption energy and differential charge density on Pt(111), a system with a known "CO/Pt(111) puzzle" where plain DFT functionals often wrongly favor hollow adsorption sites over the experimentally observed atop site: MIRA chained pymatgen (structure prep), MACE (ML-potential pre-optimization), and ABACUS (DFT relaxation and SCF) with a fixed simulation cell/grid across three consistently-computed reference systems, obtaining an adsorption energy of -1.817 eV and an atop-site optimum consistent with experiment and with dispersion-inclusive DFT literature.

## Learnings

The paper's most transferable design idea is its explicit distinction between a "squad" and a set of subagents: the difference is not the number of agents but whether they share a live task board and can address each other directly (squad) versus reporting only to a caller while blind to one another (subagents). This is a specific, checkable architectural claim, not just a naming choice, and it is offered as the mechanism that lets several full lines of inquiry stay open and get compared, rather than one agent compressing a broad question down by dropping alternatives, which is a concrete answer to "why not just widen one agent's context window."

A second reusable point is "compile rather than retrieve" for scientific memory: the paper argues that pure retrieval degrades because nothing accumulates, no cross-references, syntheses, or flagged contradictions survive past a session, and that the specific kind of knowledge a research program most needs to keep (a functional does not converge for a system, a synthesis fails above a given temperature) is exactly the kind that a plain RAG-over-papers index has nowhere to put. Building a maintained, periodically-linted wiki of interlinked pages as a separate, explicit layer above immutable raw sources is a concrete instantiation of this idea, distinguishable from typical agent "memory" implementations that just append to a growing log or vector store.

A third, domain-specific but conceptually generalizable mechanism: replacing an expensive search procedure with a trained predictor wherever the search's cost or failure mode is well characterized. Transition-state search is described as the step "most likely to fail silently" in reaction-network exploration; MIRA's response is not a better search algorithm but OA-ReactDiff and React-OT, models that predict the transition-state geometry directly, with cheaper ML potentials used to pre-screen before expensive first-principles calculations are ever invoked. The general pattern, learn a fast, direct predictor for the specific step in a pipeline that is both expensive and prone to silent failure, is worth carrying into other domains with an analogous bottleneck step.

## Verification

Verification is layered and mostly deterministic or physically grounded rather than LLM-judged. At the hypothesis stage, a dedicated reflective ("peer reviewer") agent role checks correctness, novelty, and whether a proposal is even simulable before it proceeds, and a tournament mechanism pits competing hypotheses against each other with refutation as an explicit objective, intended to counter premature convergence on the first plausible explanation. At the computational-execution stage, verification is largely deterministic and physics-native: intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) validation confirms that a predicted transition state actually connects the intended reactant and product, and first-principles DFT calculations serve as the higher-fidelity adjudicator over cheaper machine-learned potentials used for pre-screening. The protein-structure case study demonstrates a self-aware, appropriately limited use of agreement-as-evidence: MIRA checked whether three independently sampled structures from one model run converged on the same protein-ligand interaction pattern before trusting a confidence-score ranking whose top candidates were closely spaced, but the paper itself explicitly flags that this same-model, same-run agreement is not independent cross-model or experimental validation, a caveat that is unusually honest for a vendor technical report and correctly scopes what was actually shown. The paper frames physical experimentation via the AI Materials Factory as the ultimate verification step for the whole system, but no closed-loop synthesis-and-characterization campaign is actually reported here; that is explicitly deferred to a future report, so the wet-lab side of MIRA's verification claim is currently a design commitment rather than a demonstrated result.

## Field context

MIRA sits in a fast-growing, largely 2025-2026 line of "AI scientist" and agentic-research-system papers that the authors themselves cite directly and position against (Google's Co-Scientist, Anthropic's Claude Science, Kosmos, an autonomous-goal-evolving-agents system, and domain tools like ToolUniverse, Biomni, and ChemToolAgent used as comparison baselines). Its stated point of distinction from that literature is scope and physical grounding: the authors argue prior AI-scientist progress has clustered in life sciences and literature-grounded analysis, while chemistry and materials, where a hypothesis must ultimately confront a physical molecule or device, have drawn less attention, and MIRA is built specifically for that end-to-end loop including a wet-lab execution arm. Within this corpus, MIRA's closest relative is [25mcpsim], which also coordinates several specialized LLM agents through shared persistent memory to turn an ambiguous natural-language physics request into working, self-corrected solver code across twelve simulation tasks; both papers rely on deterministic, executable checks (25mcpsim's solver code execution and error correction, MIRA's IRC validation and DFT adjudication) rather than an LLM judge, but MIRA's scope is broader (hypothesis generation and physical experimentation, not only simulation setup) while 25mcpsim's evaluation is narrower but arguably more systematic (twelve fixed tasks with explicit ambiguity levels, versus MIRA's mix of two external benchmarks and three illustrative case studies). MIRA's squad-versus-subagent architectural framing is also a useful concrete design point to set against [26masat]'s finding that the value of multi-agent coordination over a single strong agent is conditional and predictable mainly from single-agent task difficulty: MIRA's own justification for a squad (parallel exploration of several full hypotheses at once, rather than one agent compressing and dropping alternatives) is a specific, testable mechanism-level claim for when the coordination overhead should be worth paying, of exactly the kind that 26masat's fitted model is trying to predict from cheaper signals rather than assume by default.

## Critical discussion

The headline benchmark claim, that MIRA achieves the top score at the lowest cost among seven agents on ResearchClawBench, is well specified (same underlying model across all seven agents to isolate harness differences, dominance on both axes simultaneously against every alternative) and is a genuinely strong, unambiguous result as reported. Two aspects narrow how far it should be read. First, the domain selection is the authors' own: MIRA is evaluated on 3 of ResearchClawBench's 10 total domains, chosen because they suit "physical science," which is a defensible scoping decision for a physical-science-focused system but means the reported win cannot be read as evidence of a general research-agent advantage across the benchmark's full domain range, only within the domains selected. Second, no repeated-run variance or confidence interval is reported for any ResearchClawBench score; single-run point estimates separated by margins as small as roughly 1 to 2 points (e.g., MIRA's 1.91-point lead over the second-place agent, smaller than the 3.9-point spread of all seven agents within the Chemistry domain alone) are treated as decisive without any indication of run-to-run noise, so the precise ranking among the top few agents should be read with some caution even though MIRA's overall dominance (best score, lowest cost, against all six alternatives) is a more robust pattern than any single point estimate. The SciAgentArena comparison is on firmer independence footing since the comparison-agent numbers were supplied by that benchmark's own project lead rather than run in-house, though MIRA's own scores on the same benchmark are still self-reported by the authors.

The three use-case campaigns are demonstrations, not controlled experiments, and the paper is appropriately restrained about what they show: the KHP network result is checked against an independently published mechanism (the Korcek pathway) rather than only against itself, which is the strongest form of external validation offered anywhere in the paper; the CO/Pt(111) result is checked against known experimental site preference and prior dispersion-inclusive DFT literature, another genuine external anchor; but the ABL1-imatinib case's three-candidate agreement, as the paper itself states plainly, is same-model, same-run agreement and not independent validation, a limitation the authors name rather than paper over, which is a mark of good-faith reporting even though it means that specific case study demonstrates internal-consistency checking rather than accuracy against a ground truth (despite an experimental ABL1-imatinib structure existing and being cited, the paper does not report a direct numerical comparison against it). As a company technical report advancing a commercial platform, with no closed-loop physical-experimentation result yet demonstrated (explicitly deferred to a later report) despite the wet-lab arm being a named third of the architecture's three-layer pitch, the paper is best read as a credible, unusually self-aware system report and a strong simulation-side result, rather than as evidence yet for the full simulation-to-bench discovery loop the title and framing promise.

What remains well supported: the agent-squad architecture (shared task board and mailbox, functional role decomposition, tournament-based hypothesis competition) is a specific, well-motivated design distinct from simpler subagent patterns, and is validated at least by convergent design choices in the cited Co-Scientist and other systems; the ResearchClawBench and SciAgentArena results, within their stated scope (selected domains, GPT-5.4 backbone), show a real and fairly wide performance-and-cost advantage over the compared agents; and the two case studies with independent external anchors (KHP mechanism, CO/Pt(111) site preference) are credible demonstrations of the computational pipeline working correctly on non-trivial chemistry and materials-science problems.
