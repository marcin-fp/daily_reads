Citation: Huang, J., Zhang, Y., Zhang, Q., Lin, H., Xu, H., Zhang, L. Domino: Decoupling Causal Modeling from Autoregressive Drafting in Speculative Decoding. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29707v1

## What this paper is about

Speculative decoding speeds up LLM text generation by having a cheap "draft" model guess several upcoming tokens at once, which the real model then checks in a single parallel pass. The catch is a trade-off: drafters that generate tokens one at a time (autoregressively) can use each earlier guess to inform the next, producing better guesses, but this sequential process is itself slow; drafters that guess an entire block of tokens in one parallel step are fast, but each position is guessed independently of the others, so the guesses are worse. This paper introduces Domino, which tries to get both properties at once: it keeps the fast block-parallel guessing step, then adds a small, cheap correction step afterward that lets later positions in the block take earlier guessed tokens into account, recovering much of the quality benefit of sequential drafting without its speed cost.

## Extended summary

Domino has two parts. A parallel draft backbone (instantiated as the DFlash architecture) takes the verified prefix's context features and a masked draft block, and in one non-autoregressive forward pass produces preliminary "base" logits for the whole block via the target model's frozen LM head. On top of these, the Domino head adds prefix-dependent correction: a lightweight GRU causal encoder sequentially summarizes previously drafted tokens into a compact causal state, and a low-rank correction head (rank 256) combines this causal state with the base hidden representation to produce a residual logit correction, which is added to the base logits before sampling. Because the correction operates in logit space on a low-rank bottleneck rather than re-running a full LM-head projection, it avoids reintroducing the expensive per-step vocabulary projection that makes autoregressive drafting slow.

Two training choices address specific failure modes the authors identify empirically. First, the causal encoder is trained with teacher forcing (ground-truth token embeddings) rather than the training-time-testing (self-generated prefix) approach used by EAGLE-3, because the correction only matters for positions where all preceding draft tokens were actually accepted, so training on noisy self-generated prefixes creates an off-distribution mapping; an ablation shows teacher forcing improves average acceptance length from 3.80 to 3.96 versus TTT. Second, because teacher forcing gives the correction branch access to clean ground-truth context, directly optimizing only the final corrected logits lets the correction branch "shortcut" the parallel backbone, causing the backbone's own loss to collapse (shown in a training-curve figure); a base-anchored curriculum that linearly anneals the loss weight from the base logits to the final logits over training prevents this collapse and further improves acceptance length to 4.19.

On Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B under the Transformers backend across eight math/code/chat benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH-500, AIME25, HumanEval, MBPP, LiveCodeBench, MT-Bench, Alpaca), Domino improves average end-to-end speedup over DFlash from 4.70x to 5.47x (Qwen3-4B) and 4.66x to 5.49x (Qwen3-8B) under greedy decoding, with similar relative gains under sampling (T=1); it also outperforms EAGLE-3 (autoregressive drafting) and DART (a diffusion-inspired parallel drafter) throughout, and adds only 56M parameters (+5.3% over DFlash) and 2.8% additional draft-then-verify latency for a 16.6% acceptance-length improvement. Under SGLang serving across concurrency levels 2-32, Domino sustains the highest throughput among tested methods on both model sizes, though its relative speedup over the no-speculation baseline shrinks substantially as concurrency rises (e.g., Qwen3-8B GSM8K drops from 5.1x at concurrency 2 to 2.1x at concurrency 32), consistent with speculative decoding's verification cost competing with other requests for GPU compute under heavy load — a pattern shared by every speculative method tested, not unique to Domino. A same-training-data ablation (all methods retrained on ShareGPT, Qwen3-8B only) isolates architectural contribution from data differences and confirms Domino still wins, notably revealing that EAGLE-3's throughput advantage over no speculation collapses below 1x (0.97x on GSM8K) at concurrency 32, meaning sequential autoregressive drafting becomes net-harmful under high load in this setting, while Domino (2.61x at concurrency 4, 1.24x at concurrency 32) and other parallel methods degrade more gracefully. A component ablation isolates the Domino head's contribution specifically: disabling it (leaving only the parallel backbone) drops average acceptance length from 4.19 to 3.49 and speedup from 3.31x to 2.84x. Authors' stated limitations: the work targets inference acceleration only, not training/fine-tuning cost; the implementation is mainly adapted to SGLang, with compatibility with other serving frameworks not systematically evaluated; and practical speedup is expected to vary across hardware platforms with different memory bandwidth, compute capability, and kernel efficiency. No funding is disclosed.

## Learnings

The core transferable idea is architectural: rather than choosing between "draft sequentially with real causal dependency" and "draft in parallel with no causal dependency," add a cheap, logit-space residual correction pass after parallel drafting that reintroduces just enough sequential information (via a lightweight recurrent state, not a full autoregressive re-run of the drafter) to recover most of the quality gap. This "decouple the dependency-modeling problem from the execution-cost problem" framing is a pattern that likely generalizes beyond speculative decoding to any setting where a fast parallel/independent approximation is available and a full sequential model is prohibitively expensive but not needed at full fidelity everywhere.

The teacher-forcing-plus-curriculum training recipe is a specific, well-motivated solution to a shortcut-learning failure mode (the correction branch learning to ignore the base backbone because teacher-forced ground truth makes the correction task too easy) that is likely to recur in any two-stage architecture where a cheap correction module receives privileged, cleaner inputs than the base module it is meant to complement — the general lesson (anneal supervision from the "harder, more honest" base task toward the "easier, corrected" final task rather than optimizing the final task directly from the start) is a reusable recipe for preventing exactly this kind of shortcut collapse.

## Verification

Verification here means the standard machine-learning-systems practice of ablation isolation, and the paper does this reasonably carefully: it separates the contribution of training data from architecture (Table 3, all methods retrained on identical ShareGPT data), separates the contribution of the training strategy from the architecture (Figure 4, TTT vs. teacher forcing vs. teacher forcing plus curriculum, holding the model fixed), and separates the Domino head's contribution from the parallel backbone alone (Table 4/6, same trained model with the correction branch enabled or disabled). This layered-ablation approach is a genuine strength: each claimed source of improvement (architecture, training strategy, the correction module specifically) has a corresponding controlled comparison rather than resting on the aggregate headline number alone. What is not verified anywhere in the paper is any measure of run-to-run variance: every reported speedup, acceptance length, and throughput number appears to come from a single run per configuration, including under T=1 sampling decoding where stochastic variation across seeds would be expected and is not reported (no standard deviation, confidence interval, or repeated-seed comparison anywhere in the main results, ablations, or appendix).

## Field context

Domino sits in an active and rapidly iterating "parallel drafting for speculative decoding" line the paper reviews thoroughly: Medusa and Hydra (parallel or sequentially-dependent decoding heads), FR-Spec and SpecVocab (reducing full-vocabulary projection cost while keeping autoregressive execution), and most directly PARD, DART, and DFlash (fully parallel block drafters), with DFlash serving as Domino's own backbone architecture and primary point of comparison throughout. It is directly comparable, within this reviewed corpus, to [reviews/26dspark.md](26dspark.md) (DSpark, from DeepSeek-AI): both papers independently diagnose the same core problem — a fast block-parallel drafter's quality degrades because each position is guessed independently of the others — and both respond by reintroducing a limited amount of genuine token-to-token dependency into an otherwise-parallel drafter rather than reverting to full autoregressive drafting. DSpark additionally addresses production serving-load scheduling (deciding how much of a draft block is worth verifying given current server load), a dimension Domino's evaluation touches only indirectly through its concurrency-sweep throughput results, without any adaptive scheduling mechanism of its own. Together the two papers suggest that "add a lightweight sequential-dependency patch to a parallel drafter" is converging as the dominant design pattern in this sub-area as of this batch's dump date, arrived at independently by an academic group (SJTU/HUST/Fudan/UESTC/Huawei) and an industry lab (DeepSeek-AI) with production deployment experience.

## Critical discussion

The paper's ablation structure is its strongest asset: by retraining every baseline on identical data (Table 3) it directly rules out the most common confound in speculative-decoding comparisons (different draft-training corpora producing different acceptance lengths for reasons unrelated to architecture), and its component-by-component ablations (training strategy, then the correction head itself) give a specific, falsifiable account of where the reported gains come from rather than an opaque end-to-end number.

Two gaps limit how far the headline numbers should be read as complete. First, FR-Spec — one of the four baselines the paper explicitly names as a comparison target in Section 5.1 ("we compare Domino with... FR-Spec") — appears only in the smaller same-data ablation (Table 3, three benchmarks, Qwen3-8B only) and not in the main cross-benchmark, cross-model results (Table 1/2) that produce the paper's flagship "5.47x/5.49x average speedup" figures; since FR-Spec targets an orthogonal source of overhead (vocabulary-projection cost) that could in principle compound with Domino's causal-correction gains, its absence from the main comparison leaves open how Domino would fare against every baseline the paper claims to benchmark against, in the setting used to generate the headline claim. Second, no run-to-run variance is reported anywhere, which matters most for the T=1 sampling-decoding results and for the high-concurrency throughput sweep, both of which are inherently noisier measurements than greedy single-request latency; the reported per-benchmark speedup differences between methods are often modest (e.g., Domino 5.33x vs. DFlash's bolded 5.48x on LiveCodeBench in Table 1, one of only two cells where a baseline outperforms Domino), and without variance estimates it is not possible to judge whether such close results are stable or within noise. A genuinely interesting result that the paper's own narrative underemphasizes is buried in the same-data ablation: EAGLE-3's throughput advantage over no speculative decoding at all collapses to below parity (0.97x) at concurrency 32 on GSM8K, meaning fully sequential autoregressive drafting can become actively counterproductive under realistic high-load serving conditions — a finding at least as important as Domino's own comparative numbers for anyone deciding whether to deploy autoregressive drafting in production, but it receives only a single sentence in the ablation discussion rather than being foregrounded as a headline finding in its own right. What remains solidly supported: under the specific models (Qwen3-4B/8B), backbone (DFlash-derived), and benchmarks tested, adding a lightweight, logit-space causal-correction module to a parallel drafter recovers a substantial fraction of the acceptance-length gap to autoregressive drafting while retaining most of parallel drafting's speed advantage, and this specific mechanism (not merely more or better training data) is directly responsible for the improvement, as demonstrated by the component ablation.
