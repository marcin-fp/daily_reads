The Red Queen Gödel Machine: Co-Evolving Agents and Their Evaluators. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26294v2

## What this paper is about

Self-improving agent systems (like the Darwin and Huxley Gödel Machines) search over a growing archive of self-modified agent variants, keeping the ones that score better on some fixed benchmark or verifier. This paper points out that treating the evaluation criterion as permanently fixed breaks down exactly where self-improvement would matter most: tasks like scientific paper writing or proof writing that have no direct benchmark at all, or where a static judge saturates or gets gamed as agents improve. The authors introduce the Red Queen Gödel Machine (RQGM), which lets the evaluator itself be a learned, evolving agent that co-evolves alongside the task agents it scores, while a specific bookkeeping mechanism ("controlled utility evolution") keeps the underlying self-improvement search mathematically well-behaved even as the objective changes over time.

## Extended summary

The technical core builds on tree-search self-improvement (following the Huxley Gödel Machine): an archive of agent nodes grows via alternating "expand" (edit a node into a new child) and "evaluate" (score a node on a task) steps, nodes are selected by Thompson sampling over clade-pooled success rates ("clade metaproductivity"), and the final agent is chosen by an epsilon-best-belief score, a conservative lower bound on true performance derived from the Beta posterior over observed successes and failures. RQGM's four specific modifications are: (i) each archive node is a shared, editable multi-agent workspace rather than a single agent, holding several "roles" (task agents and evaluators); (ii) evaluators are themselves learned agentic processes, replaceable during search; (iii) the utility function driving search may change at designated "evolutionary epoch" boundaries; and (iv) evaluators may be swapped out at those boundaries. The key theoretical device, "controlled utility evolution," keeps one evaluator frozen within an epoch (so the epoch is a fixed-criterion search problem to which prior Gödel Machine convergence guarantees apply directly) while allowing a challenger evaluator to replace the incumbent at an epoch boundary only if it scores higher (by the same epsilon-best-belief criterion) against a fixed, evaluator-independent "ground-truth anchor" dataset. When a replacement happens, "selective erasure" discards only the utility records that depended on the displaced evaluator (not the whole archive's history), and an exponentially-spaced checkpoint schedule keeps the resulting re-evaluation cost linear rather than quadratic in the search budget (Props. 3, 4, 6 in the appendix). Training/validation/test data isolation is maintained throughout: node selection during search uses only a validation split, and final numbers are reported on a held-out test split.

The empirical study spans three domains, each pairing a generator role with a learned or fixed evaluator role and a ground-truth anchor: verifiable coding (Polyglot tests, with a co-evolved code-review evaluator anchored to the CRAVE accepted/rejected pull-request dataset), scientific paper writing (paired with paper review, anchored to APReS accept/reject decisions), and Olympiad proof writing (paired with proof grading, anchored to IMO-GradingBench human grades). The primary baseline, HGM-H, is the Huxley Gödel Machine search algorithm applied to HyperAgents-style multi-role agents, using a frozen external evaluator; a further comparison uses previously published HyperAgents (DGM-H) patches and, for proofs, a hand-engineered non-learning pipeline (IMO25) that won an actual IMO gold medal. All main-text experiments use GPT-5.5 (low) as the underlying model for every role.

On Polyglot coding, adding a cheap, co-evolved agent-as-judge code reviewer alongside test execution lets RQGM exceed HGM-H's held-out pass rate (71.7% vs. 69.9%) using 1.35-1.72x fewer tokens; inspecting which code the meta-agent edits shows 90% of accepted patches touch shared infrastructure used by both the coder and reviewer roles, which the authors offer as the mechanism behind the efficiency gain (one edit improving two roles' utility at once) rather than co-evolution splitting search effort across competing objectives. On paper writing, evaluated against a fixed four-reviewer panel (including the RQGM's own adversarially-corrected reviewer, HGM-H's reviewer, DGM-H's reviewer, and the Sakana AI Scientist's prompted reviewer), the RQGM writer reaches a 1.78x higher mean panel acceptance rate than HGM-H at matched search cost, and 1.86x for the best-found specialist (Table 1); this gain is explicitly attributed to a co-evolved reviewer that has been additionally regularized (see below) to resist reward hacking. On proof writing, gains are more limited and appear only at longer search horizons: at matched compute the RQGM generalist ties HGM-H exactly on every reported metric (Table 2: 3.73 mean score, 51.7% Pass@6, 45.0% Pass@7 for both), and only the RQGM specialist pulls ahead (4.33 mean score, 61.7% Pass@6), still falling short of the hand-engineered IMO25 baseline's Pass@7 (48.3% vs. 55.0%) even though it exceeds IMO25's mean score and Pass@6.

Two further analyses probe the mechanism. First (RQ3), tracking Spearman correlation between pre- and post-replacement archive rankings shows each evaluator replacement produces a substantial and permanent re-ranking (correlation settles well below 1 and does not recover), while a no-erasure control (which keeps stale utility records after a replacement) stays pinned near the old ranking (rho >= 0.90), demonstrating that selective erasure specifically, not just a stronger evaluator being introduced, is what lets the new criterion actually reshape the search; a finer-grained archive visualization (Fig. 4) shows the single best-scoring lineage typically survives each replacement intact even as the surrounding population is substantially re-ranked, consistent with a curriculum-like effect rather than a full restart. Second (RQ4), the two learned evaluators behave very differently with respect to a known LLM-judge failure mode, self-preference bias (the tendency of an LLM judge to favor AI-generated text): the proof grader, which is conditioned on a reference solution, needs no special correction and simply improves through ordinary co-evolution, reaching IMO-GradingBench accuracy above both HGM-H's grader and static baselines at 3x lower search cost for the specialist configuration; the paper reviewer, which has no reference solution to check against, is measured to accept AI-generated papers at 1.42-1.91x the rate of human-written ones under the HGM-H baseline reviewer. RQGM corrects this specifically by exploiting its epoch-boundary mechanism: after a replacement, the AI-generated papers the displaced reviewer had accepted are collected into an adversarial pool, and the next epoch's reviewer search is additionally rewarded for rejecting them while still tracking APReS ground truth. The resulting adversarial reviewer accepts AI-generated and human papers at similar rates while retaining 80% raw ground-truth accuracy, at the cost of lower raw APReS accuracy than the lenient HGM-H reviewer; the authors argue this calibrated, harder-to-hack signal is precisely what drives the writer's improvement in Table 1, since an accurate-but-exploitable reviewer would otherwise let the co-evolving writer reward-hack it. A final cost ablation (Sec. 5.5) finds evaluation, not the meta-agent's code-editing calls, dominates total search cost (expansion is roughly 20% of the total), and that routing search-time task-agent calls through a cheaper model (Nemotron 3 Ultra) instead of GPT-5.5 cuts price-equivalent search-token cost by roughly 13x on the paper-writing domain while approaching the GPT-5.5-only run's final accuracy.

The authors are explicit that this is "a preliminary empirical investigation" from "short search horizons we intend to extend," that SWE-bench was excluded from this version due to runtime constraints, and that the framework's theoretical guarantees are epoch-local (covering improvement only within an epoch, not an end-to-end guarantee across the whole non-stationary search), with the overall quality of any learned evaluator ultimately bounded by the quality of its fixed ground-truth anchor.

## Learnings

The specific mechanism of "controlled utility evolution" (freezing the evaluation criterion within fixed epochs, allowing evaluator replacement only at epoch boundaries under a ground-truth-anchored comparison, and using "selective erasure" to discard only the utility records tied to a displaced evaluator rather than the whole archive's history) is a concrete, transferable pattern for anyone building a self-improving or continually-retrained system where the evaluation signal itself needs to change over time: it offers a specific way to get some of the benefits of a moving target (adapting to weaknesses, correcting judge bias) without fully sacrificing the convergence properties that make archive-based self-improvement search well-behaved in the first place.

The empirically demonstrated technique for correcting an LLM judge's self-preference bias, collecting the specific artifacts a displaced, lenient evaluator had accepted into an explicit adversarial pool and then rewarding the next evaluator generation for rejecting exactly those artifacts (while still tracking ground truth), is a specific, reusable debiasing recipe distinct from generic prompt-engineering fixes for judge bias: it turns a documented failure of the previous evaluator generation directly into training signal for the next one, and the reported result, retaining 80% ground-truth accuracy while equalizing AI-vs-human acceptance rates, is a specific, checkable target for anyone else building an LLM-judge-based evaluator that must resist this bias.

The finding that 90% of accepted code edits in the Polyglot experiment touched infrastructure shared between the coder and the reviewer role is a useful, generalizable observation for anyone designing multi-role or multi-objective agent search: when multiple roles share an editable codebase, co-optimizing several objectives at once may not trade off against each other as much as intuition suggests, because a single well-targeted infrastructure improvement can raise multiple roles' utility simultaneously rather than the roles competing for a fixed amount of useful search effort.

## Verification

The paper backs its central mechanism with formal guarantees (Propositions 3-6, deferred to an appendix) rather than treating "controlled utility evolution" as a purely empirical heuristic: epoch-local validity (each epoch reduces to a fixed-criterion search problem to which prior Gödel Machine convergence results apply directly), anchor-guided improvement (an evaluator is only promoted when it exceeds the incumbent's epsilon-best-belief score on a fixed, evaluator-independent ground-truth anchor), and amortized transition cost (the bookkeeping cost of erasure and re-evaluation grows linearly, not quadratically, in the total search budget under an exponential checkpoint schedule). The main empirical claims are also checked with more than a single headline number: the RQ3 re-ranking analysis includes a no-erasure control condition specifically designed to isolate whether selective erasure, rather than merely a stronger evaluator appearing, is responsible for the observed re-ranking, and Section 5.5's cost ablation is a genuine mechanistic decomposition of where search cost actually goes (evaluation vs. expansion) rather than an unexamined aggregate number. Reported accuracy and acceptance-rate figures throughout carry 95% Jeffreys intervals or standard errors, rather than being presented as bare point estimates.

The main verification gap is that, by the paper's own description, this is a preliminary study run over "short search horizons," and the text does not describe running each experimental arm's full evolutionary search process multiple times with different random seeds; the reported uncertainty intervals (Beta/Jeffreys, s.e.m.) capture noise in the held-out evaluation of a given discovered agent, not variance across independently repeated end-to-end search runs, which matters because LLM-driven tree search with stochastic expansion and evaluation steps could plausibly produce meaningfully different archives and winners across separate runs with the same configuration. The proof-writing domain's own reported numbers illustrate why this matters: the RQGM generalist ties HGM-H exactly across three metrics at matched compute (Table 2), a result close enough to the baseline that a reader cannot tell, from the evidence presented, whether a repeated run under the same configuration would reproduce this tie, a clear RQGM win, or a small RQGM loss. Separately, because every role (task agents, evaluators, and the meta-agent that edits them) is instantiated from the same base model family (GPT-5.5) in the main experiments, the self-preference-bias correction the paper applies to the paper-reviewing domain specifically is not applied to, or checked for, the agent-as-judge code-review signal used in the Polyglot domain, so it remains untested within this paper whether that signal carries a similar, uncorrected bias.

## Field context

The paper builds directly and explicitly on the Darwin Gödel Machine and Huxley Gödel Machine (which established archive-based tree search with clade-pooled utility as a tractable approximation of the theoretical Gödel Machine) and HyperAgents (which extended this search beyond coding to arbitrary task agents via a meta-agent/task-agent split), positioning its own contribution as removing the shared assumption of all three, that the evaluation criterion stays fixed outside the improvement loop, and connecting this specifically to the classical evolutionary-biology idea of co-evolving competitors (the Red Queen hypothesis) as well as to older machine-learning traditions of co-evolution against a moving target (fitness sharing, self-play). Within this corpus, two connections are worth drawing without forcing them. [reviews/2606.10917.md](2606.10917.md) (Role-Agent) pursues a closely related idea via a different mechanism: rather than a separate, formally-versioned learned evaluator co-evolving with a task agent inside an archive search, Role-Agent has a single model play both the agent and the environment/curriculum-generator role simultaneously within ordinary RL training, using the environment role to diagnose the agent's specific failure patterns and pull up more training tasks like them; both papers converge on the same underlying premise, that treating the training signal (whether a reward, a curriculum, or an evaluator) as fixed and external limits self-improvement, while differing sharply in mechanism (formally-guaranteed archive search with epoch boundaries versus joint RL training of dual roles in one model). [reviews/2606.04075.md](2606.04075.md) (SocioHack) is relevant to the paper's own stated motivation for co-evolving evaluators rather than trusting a static one: SocioHack finds that ordinary RL optimization pressure, with no adversarial instruction, reliably rediscovers real regulatory loopholes in imperfectly-specified rule systems, an independent demonstration of exactly the general phenomenon (a fixed, imperfectly-specified objective becomes exploitable once an optimizer searches against it long enough) that motivates this paper's adversarial correction of its own paper-reviewing evaluator's self-preference bias.

## Critical discussion

The paper's theoretical contribution is a genuine strength: rather than only proposing "let the evaluator also evolve" as a heuristic, it identifies the specific condition (epoch-local stationarity plus selective erasure of dependent records at transitions) under which prior self-improvement guarantees can be shown to still apply, and backs this with propositions rather than intuition alone. The RQ3 re-ranking analysis with its no-erasure control is a well-designed piece of evidence that isolates the specific causal claim (erasure, not just evaluator quality, drives re-ranking) rather than conflating the two.

The empirical evidence for the paper's broader claims is real but uneven across the three tested domains, and the paper is reasonably candid about where it is weakest. The proof-writing domain shows the RQGM generalist matching, not beating, the baseline at matched compute, with an advantage emerging only for a separately-selected specialist configuration, and even that specialist concedes ground to a hand-engineered, non-learning baseline (IMO25) on the strictest scoring metric (Pass@7); a reader should weigh the abstract's framing ("RQGM improves performance over prior self-improving agents") against this more mixed, metric-and-selection-dependent picture in the proof domain specifically. The absence of any reported multi-seed replication of the full evolutionary search process, as distinct from statistical uncertainty on a single discovered agent's held-out evaluation, means the paper's comparative claims (RQGM beats HGM-H by a specific margin) cannot currently be distinguished from run-to-run search variance using the evidence presented, which matters more here than in a typical single-model-comparison paper because the search process itself is stochastic and path-dependent by construction (which lineage survives a given evaluator replacement, as shown in Fig. 4, is itself a specific realized outcome of one search run). Finally, since every role across all three domains is built on the same base model family in the main experiments, the specific self-preference-bias correction demonstrated for paper reviewing is a proof of concept for one instance of a broader class of judge biases the framework could in principle address, not evidence that the framework's other, uncorrected evaluator roles (the Polyglot code reviewer, in particular) are free of similar undetected biases.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the coding and paper-writing results, which show consistent, sizeable gains (lower token cost at matched or better held-out performance for coding; markedly higher panel-judged acceptance for paper writing) under a broader and more heterogeneous evaluation panel than the search's own reviewer, are not solely an artifact of the search grading itself against its own evolving criterion; the mechanistic explanation for the paper-writing gain, an adversarially-regularized reviewer that specifically resists the self-preference exploit a plain co-evolved reviewer would otherwise be vulnerable to, is directly measured (acceptance-rate parity between AI and human papers) rather than merely asserted; and the theoretical framework's core claim, that epoch-local convergence guarantees can be preserved even as the evaluation objective itself changes over time, is supported by an explicit, checkable formal argument rather than by empirical results alone.
