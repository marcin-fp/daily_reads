## What this paper is about

Every existing LLM self-evolution benchmark hands the agent an already-operationalized task: a named dataset, a fixed metric, a specified success criterion — the agent only has to figure out how to improve on that fixed target. Real deployment usually starts one level higher: someone says "get better at research" or "improve mathematical reasoning," and a human (or, aspirationally, the agent itself) has to first decide what concretely to measure and train on before any optimization can even begin. Aspire tests this missing layer directly: it gives an agent only a natural-language capability goal, keeps the actual evaluation items completely hidden (the agent gets at most a bounded aggregate score, never the items, answers, or per-item feedback), and asks whether the agent can turn that vague direction into a real, retained capability gain — either by updating its own model weights or by rewriting the agent harness around a fixed model.

## Extended summary

**Setup.** A "vague goal" is a natural-language capability direction with no attached task format, dataset, difficulty anchor, or metric. The agent must diagnose its own capability gaps, decide what data and update method to use, build its own training and validation signals, and decide when (or whether) to evaluate — while a hidden, expert-authored, 520-item evaluation set (six goals: scientific/academic reasoning 75 items, humanities/social science 110, health/medicine 100, mathematical reasoning 126, logic/reliability/instruction-following 89, academic/scientific writing 20) is retained on the controller side and used only to score outcomes, never disclosed as items, reference answers, or rubrics. Every registered training dataset is checked for overlap against the hidden set before admission. A single agent-facing tool exposes composable actions (search/import/synthesize data; launch SFT or GRPO+LoRA; check status; run self-designed validation; branch or stop) without exposing shell access or infrastructure mechanics, and a safety rule retains a trained candidate only when its (score-gated) evaluated change is positive, otherwise rolling back to the incoming state.

**RQ1: what changes when an explicit task becomes a vague goal (using PostTrainBench's own sealed evaluators)?** Under vague-goal prompting, Claude Opus 4.8 scores 27.07 versus its official PostTrainBench-reported 32.90, and GPT-5.6 scores 29.58 versus its official 36.23 — though the task-level pattern is not uniform (vague-goal Claude Opus 4.8 actually does better on HumanEval and BFCL). In matched Claude Opus 4.8 trajectory pairs, vague-goal runs show more decision-model thinking time, more GPU-idle time, less active training/evaluation time, and roughly 2.4-3x denser access to task materials and proxy evaluation scripts — consistent with effort shifting toward figuring out what to optimize rather than executing the optimization itself, though the authors are explicit that these are descriptive, not isolated causal, comparisons since some of the reference numbers come from PostTrainBench's own separately-run official scores rather than a fully matched re-run.

**RQ2: can a model turn a vague goal into a retained weight-level gain (starting from an instruction-tuned checkpoint)?** In the final-only protocol (24 runs across Qwen3.5-4B/9B x six goals, two runs per model-goal pair, no intermediate feedback before the single terminal submission), only 1 of 12 model-goal pair means beats the base score (Qwen3.5-9B on scientific reasoning, 45.33→48.00), and only 3 of 24 individual final checkpoints exceed their base score at all. In the adaptive-feedback protocol (30 configuration-goal cells, sparse aggregate scores available to guide search), 28 of 30 cells produce an evaluated checkpoint and 21 pass eligibility, but only 2 cells' best checkpoint beats base score, and only 1 (GPT-5.6 Terra directing Qwen3.5-4B on mathematics, 17.86→20.10) survives as a genuinely retained improvement after rollback. Two documented trajectory patterns explain much of the gap: apparent local progress can be recovery from the agent's own earlier training-induced regression rather than genuine above-base improvement (one mathematics lineage rises 0.79→1.75→2.38 while its base score is 17.86 — a rising slope that never gets close to where it started); and more search does not mean better search (the most exploratory configuration, GPT-5.6 Sol, produces 33 evaluated checkpoints across 76.56 GPU-hours yet beats its base score in only 1 of 6 goals). A concrete, specific failure mode — training on numeric-label data (GSM8K/MMLU-style single-digit answers) even when the target goal is unrelated (writing, logic) — is directly linked to "answer-format collapse," where the resulting model emits single-digit answers regardless of the actual task, scoring at or near zero; this pattern recurs across five separately submitted checkpoints with the same signature.

**RQ3: can the agent harness itself evolve (fixed Qwen3.5-4B weights, one goal: academic/scientific writing)?** The small model itself (Qwen3.5-4B as its own harness creator) fails to produce a valid successor harness at all. Three larger models (GPT-5.6 Luna/Terra/Sol) each produce a working, behaviorally distinct successor harness, but all three score numerically below the original hand-engineered Qwen-Agent reference (28.64 task-macro / 27.65 example-micro); the best successor (Sol's, 27.22/25.97) comes closest but still falls short. Traced failure modes are specific and mechanistic: one successor's self-designed 8-item validation checklist gets solved perfectly by adding a rigid five-part answer template, which then recasts unrelated hidden-set questions (mechanisms, formulas, engineering details) as study-design problems — a case of overfitting to a narrow, self-authored proxy; another successor's fix for a hallucinating two-pass reviewer introduces a new bug (an empty final response after a tool call causes a partial draft to be submitted), which alone accounts for roughly 70% of that execution's score decline in one inspected run; a third successor's guard against this exact failure mode (checking the final response is non-empty) never actually fires on any of the 20 inspected evaluation items, so it demonstrably "improves failure handling" without a shown task-accuracy benefit.

**Self-stated limitations (Conclusion).** Results are bounded by the six evaluated goals and the coverage/quality of their expert-authored items; by using only one canonical adaptive-feedback run per configuration-goal cell (no repeated-run variance estimate); by content-level trace evidence that is available only under controlled access (not independently verifiable by an outside reader from the paper alone); and by testing only non-recursive weight/harness search (the decision model is held fixed within each reported run rather than being replaced by its own trained descendant across rounds), so the paper explicitly does not claim to have tested recursive self-improvement.

## Learnings

The central methodological device — retaining a real, expert-authored, hidden evaluator that the agent can query only for a bounded aggregate score, never for items or per-item feedback, with every agent-registered training dataset checked for overlap before admission — is a clean, reusable pattern for any study that wants to distinguish "the agent improved on its own constructed proxy" from "the agent's capability actually improved," which is exactly the gap the paper is built to expose.

The "apparent progress can be recovery from training-induced regression" pattern (a locally rising score trajectory that never returns to the pre-training baseline) is a specific, well-illustrated instance of a broader measurement trap worth generalizing: without a fixed, always-visible baseline anchor, a monotonically improving intermediate metric can look like genuine progress while the system as a whole remains net-negative relative to where it started — a reason to always report trajectories against the original baseline, not only against the first observed checkpoint in a search.

The numeric-label-SFT/answer-format-collapse failure mode is a concrete, mechanistically traced example of a more general risk in autonomous data selection: an agent importing a convenient, easily available public dataset (GSM8K, MMLU) without checking that its answer format matches the target goal's expected output form can silently overwrite the model's general-purpose response behavior, producing a specific, diagnosable degradation rather than generic "capability loss."

## Verification

Central to the paper's design: the hidden evaluation set goes through independent expert review, blind multi-model difficulty calibration, exact/semantic deduplication, overlap auditing against reference benchmarks (GPQA, MMLU-Pro, MedQA, used only as format/coverage references, with no items copied), scorer binding, and immutable versioning, and every registered training dataset is checked for overlap with the hidden set before it can be used. The safety-rollback rule (retain a trained candidate only if its score-gated change is positive, else roll back) is applied consistently and its effect on reported outcomes is explicitly separated from raw candidate scores. The failure-mode case studies (numeric-label collapse, the harness validation-overfitting and empty-response bugs) are each traced to specific, inspectable trajectory evidence rather than inferred from aggregate scores alone. What is not independently verified: the RQ1 comparison against "official" PostTrainBench reference scores for Claude Opus 4.8 Max and GPT-5.6 draws on externally reported numbers from a separate benchmark run under conditions the authors themselves note may differ in unaccounted ways ("other run metadata can also differ, so the results below describe the observed difference rather than isolate a prompt-only causal effect"); and the adaptive-feedback protocol's headline "retained improvement" (GPT-5.6 Terra on mathematics) is explicitly flagged by the authors as "evidence of feedback-guided selection... rather than an independently replicated capability gain," since it was chosen using repeated feedback on the same fixed evaluation slice with no separate confirmation slice.

## Field context

Aspire is explicitly framed as isolating a layer that a cluster of concurrent 2026 self-evolution and automated-post-training benchmarks (PostTrainBench, LaMDAgent, RSIBench-Data, AI4AI-Bench, PAST-Bench, MLE-bench, MLAgentBench, RE-Bench, AIDE, DS-Agent, ADAS, Darwin Gödel Machine, the Red Queen Gödel Machine) generally presuppose already solved: a fixed, agent-visible task, score, or evaluator. It also distinguishes itself from self-training/self-evolution algorithms (STaR, ReST-EM, Self-Rewarding LM, SPIN, R-Zero, Absolute Zero Reasoner, SEAL), which the paper treats as complementary — Aspire introduces no new training algorithm and instead asks whether an agent can construct an effective learning process (using whatever algorithm) around a goal that has not yet been operationalized. Within this batch, Aspire is a direct companion piece to S3Gym ([2608.31100.md](2608.31100.md)), sharing several authors, the same project page, and cross-referencing paragraphs in each paper's own Background section (S3Gym: "Aspire isolates how broad deployment needs are translated into concrete learning objectives"; this paper cites S3Gym as studying "whether interaction experience can be judged and reused under executable verification"). The two together form a coordinated pair examining different layers of the same forward-deployed-engineer-inspired framing: S3Gym asks whether an agent can reliably self-judge and convert judged experience into improvement given a fixed task, while Aspire asks whether an agent can even determine a sensible task and evaluation signal in the first place. Notably, RQ2's numeric-label-SFT answer-format-collapse finding and S3Gym's RQ1 finding (parameter training on self-judged experience causing severe, persistent negative transfer on Plants-vs-Zombies) are two independent, mechanistically distinct instances of the same broader pattern: naive training on agent-selected experience without a strong compatibility check can silently degrade previously working behavior.

## Critical discussion

The paper's core empirical claim — that closing the training or harness-editing loop is not the same as closing the capability loop — is well supported by concrete, traceable evidence at every level: aggregate scores (1/12 final-only pairs above base; 1/30 adaptive-feedback cells retained), trajectory-level patterns (regression-then-recovery, more-search-not-better-search), and mechanistically diagnosed individual failure cases (numeric-label collapse; the harness validation-overfitting and empty-response bugs) that go well beyond asserting a negative result and instead show specifically how and why it happens.

Three things temper how the results should be read. First, the RQ1 headline comparison (vague-goal scores well below "official" PostTrainBench references) partly rests on externally reported numbers from a different set of runs rather than a fully matched re-execution of both conditions, a limitation the authors state directly rather than obscure, but it means the exact magnitude of the vague-goal penalty in RQ1 is more an observed association than an isolated causal estimate (`missing-control`). Second, the benchmark covers only six goals, one of which (academic/scientific writing) has just 20 hidden items and is also the only goal tested in the entire RQ3 harness-evolution study, so the harness-evolution conclusion ("all three successors remain below the engineered reference") describes one goal under one small item count rather than a result shown to generalize across the other five goals (`narrow-evaluation`). Third, the adaptive-feedback protocol uses a single run per configuration-goal cell with no repeated-seed variance reported, a limitation the authors state explicitly in their Conclusion, and the one surviving "retained improvement" in that protocol is itself explicitly caveated by the authors as reflecting feedback-guided selection on a single fixed evaluation slice rather than an independently confirmed gain (`missing-uncertainty`).

What remains well supported after these caveats: the overwhelming pattern across both RQ2 protocols and independent of any single external reference comparison is that current agents routinely execute the mechanics of self-directed training (selecting data, launching jobs, producing evaluable checkpoints) far more often than they produce a checkpoint that is actually better than where they started, and the specific, inspectable failure mechanisms the paper documents (format mismatch, proxy overfitting, an unexercised safety check) give concrete, actionable diagnosis rather than a bare negative headline number.
