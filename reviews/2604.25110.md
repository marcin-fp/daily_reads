Citation: Wang, W. Knowledge Distillation Must Account for What It Loses. arXiv preprint arXiv:2604.25110, 2026.

## What this paper is about

Knowledge distillation compresses a large "teacher" model into a smaller "student," and success is almost always reported the same way: the student kept the teacher's score on the main benchmark. This position paper argues that a preserved headline score is not evidence that the student preserved the underlying capabilities that made the teacher trustworthy in the first place. A student can match the teacher's accuracy while quietly losing calibration, robustness under distribution shift, refusal behavior, privacy protection, or the diversity of its outputs — none of which the primary metric was ever measuring. The author names this the "retention assumption" implicit in current evaluation practice, shows it is not logically warranted, reframes distillation as a "lossy projection" of teacher behavior onto whatever narrow set of observables the distillation process actually optimizes and measures, and synthesizes prior findings scattered across many separate, specialized literatures into one organizing taxonomy of off-metric losses. To make the argument actionable rather than only diagnostic, the paper proposes scenario-specific preservation targets (different deployment contexts care about different off-metric capabilities) and a short, standardized "Distillation Loss Statement" that names what was preserved, what was lost, and why the remaining losses are acceptable for the intended use.

## Extended summary

Section 2 formalizes the argument. Let m(·) be the primary metric a distillation paper reports (accuracy, pass rate, win rate); a typical success claim is that the student matches the teacher under m. This is a claim about one observable, not about a broader teacher-student equivalence. Let C = {c₁, ..., cₖ} be a set of scenario-critical capabilities not directly measured by m (calibration, refusal boundary, retrieval behavior, self-verification, long-tail coverage). The "retention assumption" — that matching under m implies matching under every cᵢ ∈ C — is stated explicitly and shown not to be logically warranted: a scalar benchmark can show two models reach similar outcomes while leaving open whether they assign similar probabilities, fail on similar inputs, abstain under similar uncertainty, or preserve similar tail behavior. The paper extends this even to richer training objectives: if a distillation objective matches some observed signal φ_A over a subset A of possible observables, that only constrains what is observed and evaluated; if a relevant off-metric capability is not represented in φ_A, or not tested on the distribution where it actually matters, then retention of the primary metric cannot establish retention of that capability. This is formalized as distillation being a "lossy projection" of a fuller teacher-relevant observable vector Φ(M, x): distillation makes the projected dimensions P_A(Φ(S,x)) ≈ P_A(Φ(T,x)), but the full vectors Φ(S,x) and Φ(T,x) need not match. For a capability c with stress distribution D_c (the inputs where c actually matters), observable/proxy φ_c, and discrepancy measure d_c, an "off-metric distillation loss" is defined, and the paper's stated central problem is that a student can satisfy ordinary score-retention while still incurring a large off-metric loss on some c ∈ C.

Section 3 turns this from a logical point into an empirical synthesis. It first distinguishes "retention evidence" (does the student remain competitive on the main task) from "loss evidence" (does the student still match the teacher on capabilities the main task doesn't measure), observing that the field's default reporting practice documents the former far more than the latter — supported by a self-labeled, explicitly non-systematic 50-paper "reporting-pattern checklist" (Appendix A) that the paper states plainly is not a meta-analysis and does not validate or reject any cited paper's findings, only illustrates the asymmetry. The paper then organizes prior empirical findings into nine categories of off-metric loss (Table 1), each backed by 2-5 representative citations: predictive-distribution loss (student and teacher can diverge in probability distributions even under matched accuracy); representation/relation loss (intermediate representations, attention relations, and invariances can be inherited unevenly, motivating hint- and relation-based distillation methods like FitNets and MiniLM in the first place); capability-boundary loss (adversarial robustness, out-of-distribution robustness, and subgroup/fairness behavior can fail to transfer even when average-case accuracy holds); privacy/memorization loss (distillation can still permit membership inference and can change a student's memorization profile depending on the distillation objective used); calibration/abstention loss (confidence reliability and knowing when not to answer are not automatically inherited); process/on-policy-stability loss (compressed reasoning traces need not be faithful, and training only on static teacher-generated data can leave a student unable to recover from its own errors under its own rollouts); grounding/provenance loss (answer quality can diverge from evidence-selection and citation quality in retrieval-augmented settings); safety-boundary loss (response-based distillation for multilingual jailbreak prevention has been shown, in one cited study, to unwittingly compromise the resulting student's safety); and long-tail/diversity loss (recursive synthetic-data pipelines can lose distributional tails even while average downstream performance looks acceptable). One "single-scenario loss accounting" instance is highlighted as an existence proof the pattern is not hypothetical: a metamorphic-testing study of distilled code models (MetaCompress) finding students can preserve conventional accuracy while showing up to 285% greater performance drop under adversarial attacks and up to 62% behavioral discrepancies invisible to accuracy-based evaluation.

Section 4 turns the taxonomy into two practical proposals. Table 2 gives scenario-specific preservation-target guidance across eight common distillation deployment contexts (reasoning-model students, code/tool-using agents, RAG and domain QA systems, high-risk domain assistants, privacy-sensitive students, safety/refusal students, synthetic-data pipelines, and on-device/local students), pairing each with the off-metric capabilities most likely to matter and the stress tests that would reveal loss in that specific capability. The paper stresses this is guidance, not a benchmark specification, and that no single scenario needs to preserve every capability in the taxonomy. Building on this, the paper proposes a "Distillation Loss Statement" (Table 3): a short, eight-item reporting template (deployment context, primary metric, critical off-metric capabilities, preservation targets, stress distributions, observed or unmeasured losses, accepted losses or intended divergences, and deployment implications) explicitly modeled on documentation practices like model cards and datasheets for datasets, deliberately not prescribing universal numeric thresholds since what counts as acceptable loss is argued to depend on deployment context (a calibration gap tolerable for a toy classifier may not be for a medical assistant).

Section 5 addresses five explicit objections the author anticipates: that distillation is inherently lossy by design (accepted, but argued to narrow rather than weaken the claim: expected losses should still be named and justified); that primary metrics may suffice in low-risk deployments (accepted, consistent with the proposal's scenario-specific framing); that loss accounting may be costly or inconsistent (addressed by standardizing the reporting question rather than a numeric answer); that black-box teachers make perfect accounting impossible (addressed by pointing to behavioral proxies still available under black-box access: multi-sample behavior, self-consistency, refusal boundaries, tool traces, retrieval sets, privacy probes); and that not all divergence is loss, since a student can correct undesirable teacher behavior such as over-refusal or overconfidence (addressed by arguing divergence should be classified — harmful loss, acceptable narrowing, unmeasured divergence, or intended correction — rather than automatically treated as either good or bad). The paper situates itself against four adjacent literatures: teacher-student fidelity studies (which show divergence exists but do not provide a general, intended-use-linked reporting norm); capability-specific preservation or diagnostic methods (which reduce or diagnose particular losses but do not say which losses matter in which deployment scenario); documentation-norm literature such as model cards and datasheets (which the paper's own template is explicitly modeled on, but which do not target the teacher-to-student transformation specifically); and existing single-domain loss-accounting instances such as the cited metamorphic-testing and membership-inference studies (which show the approach is feasible but remain local to one domain). The paper's own stated Limitation is that many off-metric capabilities are hard to define and measure, especially for black-box teachers where only imperfect behavioral proxies are available, and that some teacher behaviors (bias, overconfidence, unsafe refusals) should not be preserved at all — clarifying, in the author's framing, the proposal's scope rather than removing its motivation.

## Learnings

The "retention assumption" formalized here — that matching a reference system on one tracked metric does not imply matching it on other, unmeasured properties — is a clean, general-purpose caution that extends well beyond knowledge distillation specifically to any situation where we compress, fine-tune, or otherwise transform a model and declare success based on one retained benchmark number; the paper's specific contribution is giving this familiar caution a precise notation (Equations 1-8) and a concrete domain (teacher-to-student capability transfer) rather than inventing the underlying logical point.

The paper's proposed recipe for instantiating an "off-metric loss" check — for a specific capability, choose an observable or proxy, a stress distribution where that capability actually matters (not the distribution the primary metric is evaluated on), and a discrepancy measure — is a concrete, reusable template rather than an abstract complaint that "something might be lost," and Table 2 works through this recipe for eight distinct real deployment scenarios, giving each a specific, scenario-appropriate answer rather than a single one-size-fits-all metric.

A single, well-chosen existence proof can do a lot of argumentative work: the cited MetaCompress study (metamorphic testing of distilled code models finding up to 285% greater adversarial performance drop and up to 62% behavioral discrepancies invisible to standard accuracy evaluation) is a concrete, already-measured demonstration that "the student kept the accuracy number but lost something else" is not a hypothetical worry in at least one domain, useful to cite whenever arguing that post-distillation evaluation needs to look beyond the headline score.

## Verification

This is a position paper proposing a reporting and verification norm rather than reporting a system's own verification results, so what matters here is what the paper says about how loss-accounting claims should be checked, and how its own supporting evidence was assembled. The proposed Distillation Loss Statement is itself a verification-oriented practice: for each deployment scenario, name a capability, choose an observable or proxy for it, pick a stress distribution on which that capability actually matters (explicitly distinct from the distribution the primary metric is evaluated on), and report the resulting discrepancy, with unmeasured losses disclosed as unmeasured rather than implicitly treated as zero — a "who checks the checker, and on what inputs" mindset applied specifically to comparing a teacher and a student. The paper is explicit that it standardizes the reporting question, not the numeric answer, precisely because acceptable loss is argued to be deployment-context-dependent rather than a fixed property of a model. On the paper's own methodological rigor: the central taxonomy (Table 1) and the scenario-preservation table (Table 2) are original synthesis contributions built from curated citations rather than a stated systematic search or inclusion protocol, and the paper is transparent about this limit in Appendix A, stating directly that its 50-paper reporting-pattern checklist is "not a systematic meta-analysis" and "does not validate or reject the empirical claims of the cited papers," offered only as illustrative support for the reporting-pattern-asymmetry claim in Section 3.1. This is an honestly and clearly flagged scope limitation rather than a hidden one, but it does mean neither the taxonomy's completeness nor the checklist's representativeness of the broader field has been independently established.

## Field context

The paper positions itself against four adjacent literatures rather than as an incremental refinement of any single one. Teacher-student fidelity studies (Stanton et al.'s "Does Knowledge Distillation Really Work?"; Ojha et al.'s study of what knowledge gets distilled; Mohanty et al.'s "What Is Lost in Knowledge Distillation?") are credited with showing teacher-student divergence exists, but not with providing a general, intended-use-linked reporting norm. Capability-specific preservation or diagnostic methods spanning many separate specialized literatures (representation- and relation-based distillation such as FitNets and MiniLM; adversarial-robustness-transfer studies; calibration-transfer studies; membership-inference and memorization studies for LLM distillation; on-policy-distillation surveys; chain-of-thought-faithfulness studies; retrieval-augmented-generation and citation-fidelity studies; a study showing response-based distillation for multilingual jailbreak prevention can compromise safety; model-collapse and tail-diversity studies) are credited with reducing or diagnosing particular losses, but argued not to tell authors, in general, which losses matter for which deployment scenario. Documentation-norm literature (Model Cards, Datasheets for Datasets, a position paper on measuring rather than merely claiming dataset diversity, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework) is the explicit template the paper's own Distillation Loss Statement is modeled on, while argued not to specifically target the teacher-to-student transformation. A small number of existing single-domain loss-accounting instances (the cited metamorphic-testing study of distilled code models; a membership-and-memorization-in-LLM-distillation study) are treated as feasibility proofs that remain local to one domain rather than general solutions. The paper frames its own contribution as connecting these four threads into a single general claim — that distillation should be treated as a lossy transformation whose consequential losses must be accounted for — rather than displacing any of them, a synthesis and reframing contribution rather than a new empirical finding, which the paper states about itself directly. None of these specific precedents (Stanton et al., MetaCompress, the LLM membership-inference-on-distillation study, Model Cards, Datasheets for Datasets) are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first paper in the corpus with knowledge distillation as its central subject, and the first pure single-author position/synthesis paper on model-compression evaluation practice specifically.

## Critical discussion

Since this is a position paper reporting no original experiments, the relevant scrutiny is whether the argument holds together logically, whether the supporting evidence is fairly characterized, and whether the proposed remedy is actionable as claimed, rather than whether an experimental comparison was fair. The central formal argument (Section 2) is a valid, cleanly stated point, but it is essentially a domain-specific restatement of a general and already familiar methodological caution — that matching on one observable does not imply matching on unobserved ones — rather than a new theoretical result; the paper does not claim otherwise (it explicitly frames its contribution as connecting existing empirical threads into a general reporting norm, not as a new impossibility theorem), but the notation in Equations 1-8 should be read as a precise restatement of the argument rather than a novel technical contribution distinct from the synthesis and template that follow it.

The paper's central empirical support for "off-metric losses are concrete, recurring, and measurable" is a curated set of 2-5 citations per taxonomy row plus a self-described non-systematic 50-paper checklist that the paper itself explicitly disclaims as neither a meta-analysis nor a validation of the cited findings. This is honestly and clearly flagged, but it does mean the taxonomy's completeness and the checklist's representativeness of the field have not been established by any stated search or inclusion protocol; a reader cannot tell from the paper how the roughly 50 cited works were selected, how many studies showing successful, well-validated capability preservation under distillation might exist and were not included, or whether the nine-category taxonomy exhausts the space of consequential off-metric losses or reflects the categories that happened to already have a convenient citation available.

The proposed Distillation Loss Statement is deliberately designed to avoid prescribing numeric thresholds or a required measurement protocol, a reasonable choice for a first proposal and one the paper defends explicitly by appeal to model-card and datasheet precedent, but this also means the template's actual effect depends entirely on voluntary author compliance and on reviewers choosing to hold submissions to it; the paper proposes no enforcement mechanism, incentive structure, or venue-level requirement, unlike a benchmark or automated check that could be run independently of author cooperation, leaving the gap between "this would be a good norm" and "authors and venues actually adopt it" entirely unaddressed.

The paper's own Limitations section candidly acknowledges that many off-metric capabilities are hard to define and measure for black-box teachers, and that some teacher behaviors should be corrected rather than preserved — an appropriate, self-aware qualification. It also means the proposal's most operationally difficult case is exactly the one that dominates current practical LLM distillation (a closed, frontier, black-box teacher), where the paper's own recommended remedy — behavioral proxies such as multi-sample behavior, self-consistency, and refusal boundaries — is weakest and most indirect, since none of these proxies can substitute for direct access to the internal representations, logits, or training data that the paper's own taxonomy treats as informative signals when they happen to be available.

What remains supported after these caveats: the logical point that primary-metric retention does not imply broader capability retention is sound and is echoed by the cited literature's own individual findings (predictive-distribution divergence, uneven subgroup effects, calibration failure, membership-inference risk, and the rest), even though the specific citation set assembled to support each taxonomy category was not gathered through a systematic review; the taxonomy and scenario-preservation tables are a genuinely useful organizing device for a reader deciding what to check after distilling a model for a specific deployment context, independent of whether the underlying literature synthesis is exhaustive; and the paper is transparent about exactly which parts of its own contribution remain asserted rather than independently validated — the taxonomy's completeness, the checklist's representativeness, and the proposal's real-world adoption prospects — rather than presenting a fully closed case.

## Relevance to us

This paper's core argument generalizes cleanly beyond LLM distillation to any of our own work that compresses, distills, fine-tunes, or otherwise transforms a model and then claims success based on a single retained benchmark score. The "retention assumption" formalized here (matching on a tracked metric does not imply matching on capabilities nobody checked) is exactly the caution our own verification-first stance already tries to embody, and this paper gives it a clean, citable name and a concrete reporting template we could adapt directly. Table 2's scenario-specific preservation-target guidance is directly reusable as a checklist if we ever distill a physics-reasoning model into a smaller, deployable version: its "reasoning-model students" row explicitly names capability boundary, self-verification, process reliability, and knowing when to spend more inference compute as the off-metric capabilities most likely to be silently lost, which maps closely onto our own concern that verification-aware and self-checking behavior should survive model compression, not just headline benchmark accuracy. The Distillation Loss Statement template itself (deployment context, primary metric, critical off-metric capabilities, preservation targets, stress distributions, observed or unmeasured losses, accepted losses, deployment implications) is a concrete, low-cost accountability practice we could apply to any of our own model-compression or fine-tuning work, independent of whether the broader field ever adopts it as a formal norm.
