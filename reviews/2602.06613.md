Citation: Wróbel, A., Gairola, S., Tabor, J., Schiele, B., Zieliński, B., and Rymarczyk, D. DAVE: Distribution-aware Attribution via ViT Gradient Decomposition. arXiv preprint arXiv:2602.06613, 2026.

## What this paper is about

When trying to visualize which parts of an image a Vision Transformer (ViT) is actually "looking at" to make a prediction, existing pixel-level attribution methods (which color each pixel by how much it influenced the output) tend to produce noisy, unstable maps full of grid-like artifacts that come from the model's own patch-based architecture rather than from anything meaningful about the image. This paper introduces DAVE, an attribution method built by mathematically decomposing a ViT's input gradient into two pieces: a stable "effective transformation" that captures how the network actually maps this specific input to its output, and a separate "operator variation" term that captures how sensitive that mapping is to tiny input perturbations and is mostly responsible for the noisy artifacts. DAVE keeps only the stable piece, further cleans it by averaging over small rotations/shifts of the input and adding a touch of Gaussian smoothing, and the result is reported to produce sharper, more consistent, better-localized attribution maps than several established alternatives, without needing to fall back to coarse, patch-resolution explanations.

## Extended summary

The method starts from a unifying mathematical description of a ViT layer as an input-dependent linear operator followed by a bias, which the paper shows covers every standard ViT component (fully-connected layers, single- and multi-head self-attention, layer normalization, GELU/Swish nonlinearities, and residual connections; the derivations for each are worked out explicitly in the appendix). Under this description, the derivative of a layer with respect to its input splits cleanly into two terms: the "effective transformation" (the linear operator itself, evaluated at the current input) and an "operator variation" term (how that operator's parameters change as the input changes infinitesimally). The paper argues, and illustrates with a toy example, that the operator-variation term is what amplifies high-frequency, input-sensitive noise in ordinary gradient-based attributions, so DAVE simply discards it and keeps only the effective transformation, composed layer-by-layer into a single input-dependent weight matrix for the whole network.

Keeping only the effective transformation is not sufficient on its own, because it still contains architecture-induced patterns (grid-like artifacts from patch embedding and attention routing) that are stable across many inputs but not informative about any specific prediction. To suppress these, DAVE applies a "Reynolds-inspired" averaging operator (borrowing a classical idea from representation theory for extracting invariant/equivariant components) over a small group of spatial transformations (rotations within ±20°, small translations, horizontal flips) applied to the input, keeping only the part of the effective transformation that varies consistently — i.e., equivariantly — under these small transformations. A final low-pass filtering step adds Gaussian-perturbation averaging (mathematically shown to correspond to convolution with a smoothing kernel) on top of the equivariant transformation, further suppressing high-frequency instability, in a manner the paper likens to SmoothGrad but applied to the already-cleaned effective transformation rather than to the raw gradient. The full pipeline — average the effective transformation over sampled spatial transformations and Gaussian noise, then multiply element-wise with the input — is approximated in practice via Monte Carlo sampling (50 samples per attribution, chosen after an explicit convergence check) and computed with a single modified forward-and-backward pass per sample.

Experiments evaluate DAVE against six established attribution methods (Input×Gradient, Integrated Gradients, SmoothGrad, LeGrad, AttnLRP, and Chefer-LRP) on four pretrained ImageNet-1k classifiers — ViT-B/16, DeiT-B/16, DeiT-III-B/16, and the self-supervised DINO ViT-B/16 — plus two inherently-interpretable B-cos ViT variants (compared additionally against the models' own built-in B-cos explanations). Two evaluation axes are used: localization, via the Grid Pointing Game (does the single most-attributed pixel fall inside the ground-truth object box?) and the Energy Pointing Game (what fraction of total attribution mass falls inside the box?), both on ImageNet-1k validation images with bounding-box annotations; and faithfulness, via pixel-deletion curves that progressively remove the least-important pixels first and track how the target-class probability degrades, with a flatter curve indicating attributions more consistent with what the model actually relies on. On conventional ViTs, DAVE achieves the best Grid Pointing Game score on every tested backbone, with the largest margins on DeiT-B/16 (+8.05 percentage points over the next-best method) and DeiT-III-B/16 (+11.92 points); on the Energy Pointing Game it is best on three of four backbones but is outperformed by Chefer-LRP specifically on ViT-B/16 (78.60% vs. 80.82%, a 2.22-point gap). On the two B-cos models, DAVE improves over the models' own built-in explanations on both metrics, with especially large Energy Pointing Game gains (+9.14 and +4.02 points). Pixel-deletion curves (shown as plots, not reported as a single numeric summary statistic in the main text) are described as consistently flattest for DAVE across the tested backbones, indicating the most stable attributions under progressive pixel removal.

A set of ablations supports several of the method's design choices: a convergence analysis shows the Monte Carlo averaging estimate stabilizes (measured by the L1 distance between successive cumulative averages) to a small residual after roughly 100 samples, with no further metric improvement observed beyond 50 samples, which is the number used throughout the main experiments; a rotation-sensitivity analysis measures how much a model's output probability actually changes under small input rotations and uses this to justify the chosen ±20° equivariance neighborhood (rotations within this range change model output little, so treating attributions that vary consistently within it as "equivariant" is a reasonable proxy for stability); a similar noise-sensitivity analysis (varying additive Gaussian noise standard deviation) is used to justify the amount of low-pass smoothing applied, with the paper noting model-dependent sensitivity (ViT-B/16 is more noise-sensitive than DeiT-III-B/16). A computational-complexity comparison places DAVE's cost as linear in the number of Monte Carlo samples, comparable in asymptotic class to several existing multi-step baselines (Integrated Gradients, SmoothGrad, LeGrad, AttnLRP are all also reported as scaling with a "Steps" parameter), though no concrete step/sample counts for the baselines are given alongside DAVE's stated 50, so a reader cannot directly compare absolute computational cost between DAVE and these specific competing methods from the numbers reported.

## Learnings

Decomposing a network layer's input-gradient into a "direct effective transformation" component and a separate "sensitivity of that transformation to the input" component is a clean, mathematically explicit way to isolate what specifically drives noisy or unstable attribution maps in a class of models (here, Vision Transformers), rather than treating instability as an unavoidable side effect of gradient-based explanation methods in general; the toy example in the paper (Figure 4, showing operator variation numerically dominating a stable underlying transformation) makes this concrete rather than purely conceptual.

When a decomposition or filtering step in a method depends on a hyperparameter that trades off two competing goals (here, the size of the spatial-transformation neighborhood used for equivariance averaging, and the amount of Gaussian smoothing applied), grounding that hyperparameter in a direct, measured property of the actual models being studied — here, how much the model's own output changes under small rotations or noise — is a more defensible calibration procedure than picking a default value, and the paper's explicit sensitivity ablations (Appendix D.1) are a reusable template for this kind of design-choice justification whenever a new perturbation-based or averaging-based method introduces a "how much perturbation is small" parameter.

A post-hoc attribution method can, in this paper's own results, outperform even a model's built-in, architecturally-enforced explanation mechanism (the B-cos networks' own alignment-based explanations) on the same localization metrics used to justify that architecture's design in the first place — a useful data point that architectural interpretability commitments (like B-cos's weight-input alignment) do not automatically guarantee the best available explanation for a given model, and that post-hoc methods remain worth comparing against purpose-built interpretable architectures rather than assuming the latter are a ceiling.

## Verification

The paper's most substantive verification work is methodological rather than about checking a model's predictions: it derives, in the appendix, that its layer-level decomposition (effective transformation plus operator variation) actually covers every standard ViT component (fully-connected layers, single- and multi-head attention, layer normalization, GELU/Swish, and residual connections), which functions as a completeness check on the method's applicability to the architecture class it targets, rather than an empirical claim. On the hyperparameter side, the paper explicitly verifies rather than assumes two of its own key design choices before using them in the main experiments: a quantitative convergence check (median and interquartile range of the L1 distance between successive cumulative Monte Carlo averages) confirms that 50 samples is sufficient before that number is adopted as the default, and rotation/noise sensitivity analyses (measured as the actual change in a model's own output probabilities under small perturbations) are used to set the equivariance neighborhood and smoothing scale, rather than picking these values a priori. These are commendable "check the tool before trusting it" practices. What is not reported anywhere in the main results is any variance, confidence interval, or seed-level information for the headline localization numbers in Tables 1 and 2 — these are large-sample evaluations over the full ImageNet-1k validation set with bounding-box annotations, which somewhat mitigates concern about small-sample noise, but some of the reported gains are quite small in absolute terms (for example, +1.35 points on DINO-B/16's Grid Pointing Game, or +0.49 points on DeiT-III-B/16's Energy Pointing Game), and no statistical test or uncertainty estimate is offered to indicate how confidently these specific margins should be read.

## Field context

The paper positions DAVE against three distinct lines of prior work it lays out explicitly. Architecture-agnostic gradient- and perturbation-based methods (Saliency Maps, SmoothGrad, Deconvolution, Guided Backpropagation, Integrated Gradients, GradCAM, FullGrad, and the authors' own prior work on "dynamic linear" decomposition, OMENN) are treated as generally applicable but prone to ViT-specific artifacts because they are not designed around the architecture's patch-token and attention structure; DAVE explicitly extends the "dynamic linear" framing (an input-dependent linear operator standing in for a nonlinear layer) that appears in both OMENN and the B-cos line of work, specializing and generalizing it into the effective-transformation/operator-variation split used here. ViT-specific attribution methods (raw attention visualization, Rollout, Attention Flow, Chefer-CAM/Chefer-LRP, Iterated Integrated Attributions, AttnLRP, and LeGrad) are the paper's direct empirical competitors, benchmarked head-to-head in the main results. Inherently interpretable architectures (B-cos networks and the B-cos Vision Transformer, plus B-cosification, a method for converting pretrained models into B-cos form cheaply) are treated as a different design philosophy — modifying the architecture itself to be interpretable by construction rather than explaining a fixed model post hoc — against which DAVE is shown to be competitive or superior as an add-on explanation method, applied without any architectural modification. None of these specific precedents (OMENN, B-cos, LeGrad, AttnLRP, Chefer-LRP) are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first Explainable-AI/attribution-method paper reviewed here, a distinct genre from the LLM-reasoning, multi-agent, and evaluation-methodology papers that make up most of the corpus so far.

## Critical discussion

The paper's own results table contains one clear exception to its blanket "DAVE consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods" framing in the Conclusion: on ViT-B/16's Energy Pointing Game, Chefer-LRP scores 80.82% versus DAVE's 78.60%, a 2.22-point loss that the paper's own Δ row honestly reports (as a negative number) but which the surrounding prose in the Results section and the Conclusion's general "consistently outperforms" language does not foreground. This is a minor overclaim in framing rather than in the underlying numbers, since the actual table is transparent about the exception, but a reader relying on the Abstract or Conclusion alone would not learn that the method has at least one clear loss among its eight reported localization comparisons on conventional ViTs.

The faithfulness evaluation (pixel deletion) is reported only as a qualitative visual comparison of curves ("DAVE remains the most stable... exhibiting the flattest curves"), without a corresponding single-number summary statistic (such as area under the deletion curve) presented alongside the quantitative localization tables. Given that the paper does report precise percentage-point figures everywhere else, the absence of an equally precise, tabulated faithfulness metric is a gap in an otherwise quantitatively rigorous paper, and makes the faithfulness claim harder to independently verify or compare against future work than the localization claims.

The computational-complexity comparison (Table 3) reports only asymptotic complexity classes (O(1), O(Steps), O(Samples)) without concrete step or sample counts for the baseline methods, while stating DAVE's own default of 50 samples explicitly. Because several baselines (Integrated Gradients, SmoothGrad, LeGrad, AttnLRP) are also multi-step methods whose typical step counts are not stated here, it is not possible from this paper alone to tell whether DAVE is actually more, less, or comparably expensive than these specific competitors in absolute wall-clock or FLOP terms; the paper's own Conclusion acknowledges "higher computational overhead compared to raw gradient methods" as a limitation, which is honest, but a concrete side-by-side cost comparison would substantiate this more precisely than the qualitative admission and the asymptotic-only table.

The equivariance-neighborhood and noise-smoothing hyperparameters are calibrated using sensitivity measurements taken on the same models (ViT-B/16, DeiT-B/16, DeiT-III-B/16) that are subsequently evaluated on the localization and faithfulness benchmarks, using images from the same ImageNet-1k validation pool. This is not label leakage or metric-gaming in any strong sense — the calibration measures a general property of model behavior (sensitivity to small transformations) rather than fitting to the localization or faithfulness metrics directly — but it is a mild form of in-sample design-choice tuning worth naming plainly, since a stricter protocol would calibrate these hyperparameters on a held-out set of models or images disjoint from the ones used for the final reported numbers.

Finally, the paper's own motivating framing (in both the Introduction and the Impact Statement) repeatedly invokes high-stakes domains — medical diagnostics, autonomous driving — as the reason stable, trustworthy attribution matters, but all reported experiments are on ImageNet-1k object classification; no medical-imaging, autonomous-driving, or other high-stakes-domain dataset is tested, so the paper's motivating claim about safety-critical applicability remains an unevaluated extrapolation from its actual experimental scope, which is a common but real gap between framing and evidence in this kind of methods paper.

## Relevance to us

This paper is well outside our core physics-reasoning and language-model-evaluation focus — it is specifically about pixel-level visual attribution for image classifiers, a different modality and a different notion of "explanation" than the reasoning-trace or claim-level verification we usually care about, and none of the mathematical machinery here (spatial-transformation equivariance averaging, Gaussian gradient smoothing) has an obvious direct analogue for text or reasoning-token attribution. The one genuinely transferable methodological habit worth naming is the paper's discipline of verifying a design choice with a targeted, quantitative sensitivity check before adopting it as a default (here, measuring how much a model's own output actually changes under a candidate perturbation range before treating that range as "small enough" to define equivariance) — a generally good practice for any of our own work that introduces a perturbation-based or sampling-based hyperparameter, independent of the vision-specific details of this particular method. Beyond that specific habit, the paper does not bear on any FirstPrinciples bet or tension. None otherwise.
