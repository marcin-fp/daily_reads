Citation: Pareek, S., Govers, J., Kollerup, N. K., Wong, E., Velloso, E., and Goncalves, J. Sensemaking in Multi-Agent LLM Interfaces: How Users Interpret Transparency and Trustworthiness Cues. Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26).

## What this paper is about

Multi-agent LLM systems, where several AI agents vote, critique each other, or debate before producing a final answer, generate a lot of internal process, disagreement, diverse rationales, critique, consensus, that could in principle be shown to end users as a signal of how trustworthy the system's answer is. But nobody had systematically studied how ordinary people actually interpret that signal, what they want to see, or whether showing more of it makes people trust the system more appropriately or just adds mental effort. This paper runs a qualitative, design-led lab study in which 12 participants interact with five mock multi-agent chat interfaces, ranging from showing only a final answer to showing a full multi-turn agent debate, across two contrasting task types, and finds that people do not want maximum transparency. They want a "Goldilocks," just-enough amount that shifts with the task, their own confidence, and their general disposition toward trusting AI, and several of the intuitive signals people actually rely on, such as "more agents means more trustworthy" or "the agents mostly agree, so it must be right," are exactly the kind of heuristic that can mislead when the underlying reasoning quality does not actually match the surface impression.

## Extended summary

The authors first derive a design space for multi-agent transparency through a structured scoping review: an initial ACM Digital Library search (691 hits) is narrowed via inclusion criteria (multiple LLM agents, described agent behaviors, and reported or illustrated user-facing or system-level design details rather than only back-end architecture or benchmark results) to 33 full-text papers, which are open-coded and clustered into seven interface-level design dimensions (D1 Agent Composition/Number of Agents, D2 Role Specialisation, D3 Response Format/Reasoning Visibility, D4 Disagreement and Critique, D5 Interaction Topology, D6 Information Flow, D7 Output Aggregation). Five interface variants (V1 through V5) are then constructed, each instantiating a distinct, theoretically motivated combination of these dimensions rather than an exhaustive sweep: V1 (Final Answer) shows only the system's aggregated decision with no agent-level visibility; V2 (Agents Answer) shows each of four agents' individual answers plus a majority vote, without rationales; V3 (Agents Explain) adds a brief rationale per agent; V4 (Agents Critique) adds a dedicated fifth "critic" agent that evaluates each of the other four agents' responses before a summarized final answer; V5 (Agents Debate) shows a multi-turn inter-agent discussion in which initially disagreeing agents converge on a consensus. Ensemble size (four agents) is deliberately held constant across V1 through V5 to keep each variant a focused probe, with a separate side-by-side four-versus-eight-agent comparison run as its own activity to isolate that dimension specifically.

Participants completed two contrasting tasks, a binary yes/no general-knowledge information-seeking task and a harder LSAT-style multiple-choice logical-reasoning task, both selected to be answerable without specialized domain knowledge but genuinely challenging (participants' own pre-task confidence averaged around 40 out of 100 on both). For each task, participants answered independently first, then interacted with all five interface variants in randomized order while thinking aloud and being interviewed, followed by three digital card-sorting activities (Comparative Structured Observation, following Mackay and McGrenere) in which they arranged the five interfaces along spectrums of perceived transparency, task-context helpfulness, and reliability, using the sort order and any cross-scale tensions as structured prompts for follow-up questions. All final answers shown across every interface variant were correct; for the reasoning task, agent rationales and critiques were drafted by prompting GPT-4o from official LSAT explanations and then manually reviewed and edited by three authors for plausibility, logical (mis)alignment, and stylistic consistency, with the multi-turn V5 debate sequences manually authored from the vetted V3/V4 material. Twelve participants (six men, six women) with varied AI familiarity and dispositional trust were recruited via university notice boards and snowball sampling; sessions lasted about 65 minutes and were analyzed via Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis.

On mental models, participants spontaneously described the multi-agent system using team, panel, or committee metaphors, frequently anthropomorphizing agents as having distinct specialties (one participant imagined "one of them could be good at biology, one at logic, one at comprehension"). Several participants explicitly wanted to know the underlying model or vendor identity of individual agents as a reliability heuristic, with one stating plainly that if the agents were all a stronger, newer model they "would not even need an explanation," while an older or weaker model would require more visible reasoning to trust, an explicit "capability substitutes for transparency" finding.

On which affordances function as trust cues: ensemble size (D1) was widely read as a reliability signal ("lots of little people working towards one goal... seems more powerful and credible"), but also as a cognitive-load cost, and participants wanted to know that many agents existed without necessarily seeing all of their outputs, with several explicitly wanting the desired ensemble size to scale with task difficulty. Response format (D3) showed a clear gradient: V1 (bare final answer) was widely seen as too opaque to trust or use for sensemaking, though a minority explicitly preferred it for simple tasks, with one participant stating they felt more confident specifically because they could not see the system's imperfections, an "ignorance is confidence" pattern; V2 (agent answers without rationale) was seen as barely better than V1 for building trust or supporting reasoning; V3 (brief per-agent rationales) was widely described as the sweet spot, letting participants weigh competing reasoning paths themselves and explicitly preserving their sense of decision agency ("I'm not handing [it] the agency and autonomy 100%"). On disagreement and critique (D4/D2): unexplained disagreement in V2 reduced trust and induced doubt, with several participants wishing they had not seen it at all, but the same disagreement in V3, accompanied by a rationale from each side, was reframed as a useful signal that let participants interrogate and judge its relevance; a dedicated critic agent (V4) was broadly seen as boosting trust by demonstrating the system "checking itself" and provided scaffolding material for participants' own reasoning, but for a minority the same critique backfired, explicitly reducing confidence in the system's underlying capacity by making its fallibility newly salient, and adding perceived cognitive burden. Interaction topology and information flow (D5/D6), properties multi-agent-systems researchers often treat as core architectural choices, did not register as salient cues at all; only one participant raised the underlying coordination structure, and only when prompted by a visible symptom (disagreement) rather than by the architecture itself. On aggregation (D7), visible majority agreement strongly and durably boosted perceived reliability even with zero rationale present, at times leading participants to simply dismiss a dissenting agent ("it doesn't have enough friends"), with one participant explicitly catching themselves in this bandwagon-style reasoning in real time ("the majority could kind of fool me"); the multi-turn debate-to-consensus format (V5) was seen by many as the most naturalistic and trust-boosting presentation, mirroring human deliberation, but was also seen by some as excessive for simple tasks.

On task-dependence, the "Goldilocks" transparency level shifted systematically with task difficulty: simple fact-lookup tasks favored brief formats (V1/V2), complex reasoning tasks favored deeper ones (V4/V5), and, importantly, giving more transparency than a task seemed to warrant did not merely go unused, it actively reduced trust, with one participant describing an overly elaborate debate on a simple question as the system "trying too hard to convince me." Perceived stakes could override task complexity (a trivia question framed as consequential, such as a game-show scenario, justified more scrutiny than the same question framed casually). Individual task-domain expertise, task confidence, and general dispositional trust in AI further and independently modulated how much process visibility participants wanted. Eight of the twelve participants explicitly wanted progressive, collapsible disclosure, a simple default view (such as the consensus answer) with the ability to expand into individual agent rationales, critiques, or the full debate on demand, rather than a single fixed transparency level built into the interface.

The paper's discussion reframes multi-agent transparency as a dynamic sufficiency judgment rather than a "more is better" dial, and argues that no single epistemic cue (ensemble size, rationales, disagreement, critique, consensus) functions as a universally positive trust signal; each carries a design trade-off whose effect depends on how, when, and in what context it is surfaced. The authors note this raises a specific concern for trust calibration: "just right" transparency may boost subjective trust because it feels contextually aligned, independent of whether that trust is actually warranted by the system's real accuracy, a concern the authors flag as untested in their own study since all shown answers were correct.

The paper's stated limitations include: tasks were predefined and had objectively verifiable answers rather than being self-directed, which supported comparability but may not capture behavior in open-ended use; specific operationalizations were fixed (for example, a 3:1 majority ratio, and only four versus eight agents rather than a finer sweep of ensemble sizes or disagreement proportions) rather than exhaustively varied, a deliberate trade-off to keep each interface variant a focused, interpretable probe; as an interpretivist, design-led Comparative Structured Observation study, the goal was to understand user behavior and build theory rather than to quantify causal effects or estimate population-level parameters; the sample (N = 12) is consistent with thematic-saturation guidance and comparable prior CSO studies but may still limit the breadth of perspectives captured; and, most consequentially for the study's own central topic, all final AI outputs shown across every interface were accurate, which isolated interface effects cleanly but left open how users interpret and calibrate trust when agent errors are present or unevenly distributed within an ensemble.

## Learnings

The specific cues users actually rely on to judge a multi-agent system's trustworthiness, ensemble size, presence of rationales, visible disagreement, visible consensus or majority vote, and presence of a dedicated critic, are largely surface-level and behavioral, not architectural. Interaction topology (sequential versus parallel processing) and information flow (pipeline versus hierarchical message routing), properties that matter a great deal to multi-agent-systems researchers, were found to be essentially invisible to end users and did not shape trust judgments at all in this study unless a downstream symptom of them happened to surface. Any transparency design for a multi-agent system should prioritize the visible, behavioral signals users actually attend to rather than assuming that exposing the underlying coordination architecture itself will register as meaningful transparency.

Several trust cues in this study were genuinely double-edged, with the same underlying signal increasing or decreasing trust depending on how it was framed, not merely how much of it was shown. Unexplained agent disagreement damaged trust and several participants explicitly wished it had been hidden, but the identical disagreement accompanied by a stated rationale for each side was reframed as a useful feature that let users weigh competing views themselves; a dedicated critic agent boosted most participants' trust by demonstrating the system "checking itself," but for a minority the same critique backfired by making the system's fallibility newly salient. Neither signal is unconditionally good or bad; its effect depends on whether it comes packaged with enough context for a user to read it as a deliberate design feature rather than a symptom of failure.

Increasing transparency beyond what a task seems to warrant does not merely have diminishing returns, it can actively reduce trust: participants who found a multi-turn agent debate reassuringly thorough on a hard reasoning task described the identical format as excessive and trust-eroding when applied to a simple factual question, reading it as the system "trying too hard to convince me." The common design assumption that users can simply ignore transparency detail they do not need does not hold here; excess process visibility carried a real perceived cost, not merely an ignorable one, when applied to a task that did not need it.

## Verification

This paper does not perform technical or scientific verification in the sense of checking AI-output correctness against ground truth; every final answer shown across every interface variant was deliberately correct, an explicit design choice the paper's own limitations section names directly. Instead, verification appears here as the object of study rather than the paper's own method: the paper investigates how a specific verification-adjacent design feature, a dedicated "critic" agent whose entire function is to check other agents' reasoning before a final answer is produced, is perceived by end users, finding that its mere visible presence functions as a strong, though not universally positive, trust signal, independent of whether the critique actually caught anything of substance. This is a genuinely useful, if indirect, contribution to thinking about verification interface design: it shows that making a verification step visible changes how much a person trusts an output, which is a distinct question from whether that verification step is actually reliable. Because all agent outputs in the study were scripted to be correct, the paper explicitly could not test whether users' trust in a visible critic agent tracked the critic's actual reliability, or whether visible critique produced better-calibrated trust once genuine AI errors were present, both of which are left as open questions for future work.

## Field context

This paper connects a multi-agent-LLM-performance literature (multi-agent debate, self-consistency voting, critic and summarizer role specialization, developed primarily to raise task accuracy) with the classical trust-calibration and transparency literature in human-AI interaction (built on Lee and See's definition of trust and Lee and Moray's process/performance/purpose framework, and on a body of work documenting how LLM fluency undermines transparency techniques originally developed for classifier-style AI). It builds a systematic design space via a structured scoping review specifically because prior HCI work in this niche, a "devil's advocate" agent reducing overreliance in group decision support, role-specialized "critic" and "summariser" agents helping users recognize diverse opinions, had been scattered and exploratory, each examining an isolated configuration rather than a principled space of design choices. It also directly extends a growing challenge in the explainable-AI literature to the "more transparency is always better" assumption into the specifically higher-stakes context of multi-agent systems, where the volume of process material a system could show a user is far larger than for a single model. Within this corpus, this paper's central "Goldilocks" finding, and its theme that epistemic cues are double-edged rather than universally positive signals, connects to a recurring pattern across this session's reviews (24selfcor, 25mcrit, 26madrag) that a persuasive-looking multi-agent process, debate, critique, consensus, does not by itself guarantee sound underlying reasoning; this paper adds the complementary, user-facing half of that same concern, showing end users can be exactly as susceptible to being reassured by the surface appearance of deliberation as the automated judges examined in those other papers are susceptible to being misled by it.

## Critical discussion

The design-space derivation is methodologically disciplined for this kind of work: a structured, criteria-based scoping review (691 initial hits narrowed to 33 fully coded papers via explicit inclusion criteria) grounds the seven design dimensions in actual patterns from the literature rather than an ad hoc researcher-generated taxonomy, and the five interface variants are explicitly justified as a deliberately curated, theoretically motivated subset with a clear, stated rationale for holding ensemble size constant across the main variants and testing it separately.

A significant, self-disclosed limitation is directly relevant to the study's own central topic: every final answer shown across all five interfaces was correct. A study about how transparency signals shape trust could not, by this design, observe what happens to those trust judgments when the underlying multi-agent reasoning is actually wrong, which is precisely the situation transparency and trust-calibration mechanisms are meant to help with. The paper is explicit about this gap and frames it correctly as future work rather than glossing over it, but it means the reported findings describe how people build trust in a hypothetical, always-correct system, not whether the specific cues identified (majority agreement, ensemble size, visible critique) actually help people detect or avoid errors when the system fails (narrow-evaluation). The sample is small (N = 12) and drawn largely from one university community via convenience and snowball sampling; the paper defends this with appropriate citations to thematic-saturation guidance and comparable prior Comparative Structured Observation studies, a reasonable justification for this qualitative methodology, but specific proportions reported (such as "eight of twelve participants") should be read as illustrative of a pattern rather than as population-level estimates, consistent with the paper's own explicitly interpretivist, non-hypothesis-testing framing. The reasoning-task stimuli's rationales and critiques were generated by prompting GPT-4o and then manually reviewed and edited by the author team for plausibility and stylistic consistency, rather than sourced from genuine independent multi-agent LLM runs; this is a reasonable and disclosed way to produce controlled, comparable stimuli across five otherwise identical interfaces, removing confounds from actual model variance, but it means the specific rationale content participants reacted to was curated by the research team for plausibility rather than being an authentic sample of a real multi-agent system's actual failure modes, error styles, or disagreement patterns.

What remains well supported: the central, repeatedly triangulated finding that users evaluate multi-agent transparency as a contextual sufficiency judgment rather than a "more is always better" dial, with both excess and deficient transparency actively reducing trust depending on task demands, is supported convergently across the think-aloud protocol, the card-sorting activity, and follow-up interviews across two contrastingly different task types, a reasonably strong triangulated evidentiary base for a qualitative study of this design. The specific catalog of double-edged epistemic cues (ensemble size, disagreement, critique, consensus) and the finding that architectural properties (interaction topology, information flow) are perceptually invisible to end users are both grounded in specific, quoted participant reasoning rather than only aggregate impressions, and the paper's limitations section accurately scopes what the study can and cannot claim.

## Relevance to us

This paper connects directly to our interest in how physicist-collaborators will actually experience a multi-agent or multi-check reasoning system, and to the standing observation that scientists want a sparring partner where decision-making stays with the person, not an obedient intern that either withholds everything or dumps everything. Several findings are worth carrying forward directly. The "Goldilocks" sufficiency framing, and specifically the finding that giving a user more transparency or verification detail than the task's difficulty or their own stated confidence warrants actively erodes trust rather than merely being ignorable, argues against a default design instinct to always show the full derivation, verification trace, and assumption ledger for every physics query regardless of context; a progressive-disclosure pattern, defaulting to a concise, confident answer with an explicit, easy expansion into the full derivation, assumption ledger, or disagreement trace, is exactly what a majority of this study's participants asked for, and maps naturally onto our own collaborator framing. The finding that a visible verification or critique step boosts trust largely through its mere presence, independent of whether it actually caught anything, is an important caution for us specifically: if we build a visible verification layer into a physics-reasoning tool, such as a graded checker or an assumption auditor, we should be wary of the same effect this paper documents, where the appearance of self-checking can create trust that outruns the check's actual reliability, especially since this study could not test what happens to that trust once the underlying system is actually wrong; this argues for deliberately validating that our own visible verification surfaces are calibrated to their real reliability, not merely reassuring-looking, echoing this session's reviews of 26madrag and 25mcrit on the same underlying risk from the generation side. The finding that architectural properties (interaction topology, information flow) are essentially invisible and irrelevant to end-user trust, while surface behavioral cues (agreement, disagreement, critique content) are what actually register, suggests that if we ever expose multi-agent or multi-verifier structure to a physics collaborator's end user, the surfaced cues should be designed deliberately around behavior and content, whether checks agree, what a dissenting check actually flagged, rather than assuming that exposing the pipeline's architecture itself will function as meaningful transparency. The minority "ignorance is confidence" finding, where not seeing a system's visible imperfections made some participants more rather than less confident, is a useful caution against assuming every scientist-user will always want maximal visibility into uncertainty and disagreement; some may specifically not want it for simple, low-stakes queries, reinforcing the case for user-controlled, task-sensitive disclosure rather than one fixed default level of shown reasoning.
