Citation: Hu, Y., Zhou, Z., Huang, K., Huang, X., Wang, Q. Can MLLMs Absorb Math Reasoning Abilities from LLMs as Free Lunch? NeurIPS 2025. arXiv:2510.14387.

## What this paper is about

Text-only LLMs have gotten quite good at math reasoning through expensive, carefully curated training, while multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), which need to reason about a mix of images and text, lag noticeably behind on math problems that involve diagrams, charts, or geometric figures. Since an MLLM is typically built around a text-only LLM plus a vision component, this paper asks whether an MLLM could simply "absorb" an existing, separately-trained math LLM's reasoning skill directly at the parameter level, without any additional training, data, or increase in model size. The naive way to try this (a standard model-merging trick of just adding the two models' learned parameter differences together) turns out not to work, and the paper diagnoses why and proposes a more careful merging procedure that does.

## Extended summary

The paper starts from "task arithmetic," a well-established model-merging technique: define a model's task vector as the difference between its fine-tuned and pre-trained parameters, and combine two specialized models by simply adding their task vectors onto a shared base. Applying this directly to merge a math-specialized LLM's task vector into an MLLM's parameters does not improve, and can actively harm, the MLLM's math performance. The authors trace this failure to a structural mismatch: they measure the "trace value" (a proxy for how much a parameter changed during fine-tuning, used in prior work to gauge learning difficulty) across 30 MLP layers in two math LLMs and one MLLM, finding a large, systematic gap between how math LLMs and MLLMs have shifted from their shared pretrained starting point, since the two model types were fine-tuned on very different objectives (multimodal alignment versus text-only mathematical reasoning).

Two more precise empirical diagnoses follow. First, using singular value decomposition (SVD) on each layer's task vector, the authors measure "subspace similarity" between the math LLM and the MLLM via the cosine angle between their corresponding singular vectors (ordered by singular value rank), finding that the top singular direction (associated with the largest singular value) shows dramatically higher similarity than lower-ranked directions, and that only a subset of layers/parameters show high similarity at all; selectively merging only these high-similarity layers, rather than all layers uniformly, produces a real accuracy gain (11.3% to 13.4% on MathVerse for a base LLaVA model, versus a drop to 9.8% from naive full-layer task arithmetic), directly validating that these specific parameters are the ones actually carrying transferable math-reasoning signal. Second, the authors show, via a short linear-algebra argument based on matrix norm inequalities (Proposition 1), that if a math LLM's task vector has a much larger maximum singular value (magnitude of parameter change) than the corresponding MLLM parameters, naive addition will let the math LLM's larger-magnitude update numerically "overshadow" the MLLM's own learned alignment between vision and text, and they confirm empirically that math LLMs do tend to have substantially larger top singular values than MLLMs in corresponding layers.

Building on both diagnoses, the paper proposes IP-Merging (Identify then Project), a three-step, training-free procedure: (1) Parameter identification, selecting only the layers/parameters whose subspace similarity (per the SVD-based corresponding-angle measure) exceeds a threshold, restricting merging to attention and MLP layers shared between the two models while leaving the visual encoder and vision-language projection layers untouched; (2) rescaling, computing a per-layer rescaling factor from the ratio of nuclear norms between the MLLM's and math LLM's parameter spaces to normalize the magnitude mismatch identified above; and (3) projection, weighting the math LLM's selected subspace basis vectors by their similarity-based importance and projecting the rescaled math-LLM parameters into the MLLM's own subspace before finally adding them in, intended to keep the transferred parameters close to what the MLLM already "expects" in that part of its representation space. The whole procedure requires no additional training data or gradient updates, only one-time SVD computations and linear algebra on existing model weights.

Evaluated across three base MLLM/math-LLM pairs (LLaVA-1.5-7B with Tora-Code-7B, Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct with Qwen-2-Math-7B, and InternVL3-8B-Instruct with DeepSeek-R1-distilled-Qwen-7B) on four math-reasoning benchmarks (MathVista, MathVerse, DynaMath, MathVision) and three general-capability benchmarks (MMMU, TextVQA, MMBench), IP-Merging consistently outperforms three existing merging baselines (Task Arithmetic, Ties Merging, EMR Merging) and, in most but not all cases, meaningfully outperforms the unmerged base model itself. The largest absolute gains occur for the weaker base models: LLaVA-1.5-7B improves from 11.3% to 15.3% overall on MathVerse (+4.0) and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct improves from 24.8% to 28.5% (+3.7), while the strongest tested base model, InternVL3-8B-Instruct (built on the already math-capable Qwen2.5 backbone), improves only marginally on MathVerse (38.5% to 39.0%, +0.5) and shows a small regression on one MathVista subtask (VQA, 53.1% to 52.5%). By contrast, the three baseline merging methods frequently perform substantially worse than simply using the unmerged base model at all (e.g., Task Arithmetic drops Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct's MMMU score from 50.7% to 32.6% and its TextVQA score from 83.8% to 37.9%), indicating that naive or partially-corrected merging is often actively destructive to a strong base model's existing capabilities, not merely ineffective at adding new ones. IP-Merging is the only tested method that consistently avoids this destructive pattern, maintaining general-benchmark scores close to or matching the unmerged base model across all three base-model families (Table 3), which the authors attribute directly to its selective, similarity-gated parameter identification step leaving parameters important to non-math capabilities untouched. A notable, self-reported exception is LLaVA-1.5-7B's Geometry Problem Solving (GPS) subtask, where IP-Merging does not improve over the base model (21.6% vs. 22.1%) and is clearly outperformed by Ties Merging (40.4%), which the authors attribute to Ties Merging's sign-consistency-based parameter selection happening to better preserve some shared geometric-reasoning representation in this specific case, an outcome not fully explained by the paper's own similarity-based framework. Additional experiments show the method scales to larger (13B-parameter) model pairs, composes when merging in multiple math LLMs simultaneously (progressively improving MathVista accuracy), transfers more effectively from "System-1" math LLMs trained with a chain-of-thought-plus-code, self-correcting recipe (Tora-Code) than from simpler chain-of-thought-only recipes (WizardMath, MetaMath), and also benefits already fine-tuned math-specialized MLLMs (TableLLaVA, G-LLaVA, VL-Rethinker) when a math LLM is merged in on top of their existing fine-tuning. An ablation confirms all three components (selection, rescaling, projection) contribute additively, with the full combination outperforming any subset, and a qualitative layer-analysis shows the automatically-selected parameters concentrate in middle-to-late MLP layers, consistent with prior findings that reasoning-related knowledge in LLMs is predominantly encoded in deeper MLP blocks rather than early attention layers.

## Learnings

The paper's central, broadly transferable insight is a specific, mechanistic diagnosis of why naive parameter-space model merging fails across a modality boundary: it is not simply that "the two models are different," but two distinct, separately measurable and separately correctable problems, a mismatch in which specific subspace directions actually carry the transferable capability (addressed by selecting only high-subspace-similarity parameters) and a mismatch in the sheer magnitude of parameter change between the two models (addressed by rescaling before merging). Decomposing "models don't merge well" into these two separately diagnosable and separately fixable failure modes is a reusable analytical move for anyone facing a similar cross-domain or cross-modality merging problem, not specific to math or to MLLMs.

A second, more specific and immediately practical learning is the finding that merging benefits vary systematically with how much reasoning headroom the target model already has: gains are large for base models with comparatively weak native math ability and shrink to near-zero for a base model whose foundation LLM (Qwen2.5) is already comparatively strong at math, a concrete, quantified instance of a diminishing-returns pattern worth expecting whenever this kind of capability-transfer technique is applied to progressively stronger starting models, rather than assuming the technique's benefit is a fixed additive quantity independent of the base model's starting capability.

A third, narrower but concrete learning from the additional experiments is that not all "math LLMs" transfer equally well as merge sources: models trained with a richer reasoning recipe combining chain-of-thought, code execution, and a self-correction/reflection mechanism (Tora-Code) transferred more effective math-reasoning parameters into the MLLM than models trained with chain-of-thought supervision alone (WizardMath, MetaMath), suggesting that the internal representations built by a more sophisticated training recipe may be more cleanly separable or more subspace-aligned with an MLLM's existing math-relevant parameters, a specific, testable hypothesis for future work on which kinds of specialist models make the best merge donors.

## Verification

The paper grounds each step of its diagnosis in a specific, empirically-checked measurement rather than asserting a mechanism a priori: the claim that math LLMs and MLLMs occupy different regions of parameter space is checked via a concrete trace-value comparison across 30 layers before any method is proposed; the claim that only some parameters carry transferable math-reasoning signal is checked via the SVD-based subspace-similarity measure and directly validated by a controlled experiment (Table 1) showing that merging only the identified high-similarity layers, versus all layers, produces a real accuracy gain rather than a degradation; and the claim that magnitude mismatch causes one model's update to overshadow the other's is supported by both a short formal argument (Proposition 1, a standard norm-inequality bound) and a direct empirical measurement of the relevant singular-value distributions (Figure 4) showing math LLMs do have systematically larger top singular values than the corresponding MLLM layers. The main results are checked against three genuine, actively-used prior merging methods (Task Arithmetic, Ties Merging, EMR Merging) rather than only a "do nothing" baseline, with grid-searched hyperparameters for each baseline reported explicitly, reducing the risk that the comparison methods are weakened by poor hyperparameter choices. The paper also separately verifies a claim that could otherwise be assumed rather than checked, that the method does not damage the MLLM's non-math capabilities, via three dedicated general-purpose benchmarks (MMMU, TextVQA, MMBench) rather than reporting only math-task numbers, and an ablation study isolates the individual contribution of each of the three method components (selection, rescaling, projection) rather than presenting only the fully-combined result.

Verification is comparatively thinner in a few places. The GPS geometry-subtask exception for LLaVA (where IP-Merging underperforms both the base model and Ties Merging) is reported candidly but is explained only informally, via a plausible but untested post hoc hypothesis (that Ties Merging's sign-consistency criterion happens to preserve a different, geometry-relevant shared representation), rather than being investigated with the same subspace-similarity tooling the paper otherwise relies on, leaving open whether the paper's own similarity-based selection criterion is systematically less well-suited to certain reasoning subtypes like multi-step geometric proof. The theoretical justification for why magnitude mismatch causes performance degradation, not just numerical overshadowing of the vector sum, rests on a norm-based bound (Proposition 1) that establishes a mathematical fact about vector addition but does not itself establish a causal link between "the merged vector's norm is dominated by the larger vector" and "downstream task accuracy degrades," a connection the paper treats as intuitive rather than separately proven, though the empirical results are consistent with it. Finally, the paper's own stated Limitation, that the method requires the MLLM and math LLM to share both parameter count and the same underlying foundation LLM architecture, is disclosed plainly but not empirically probed at its boundary (e.g., no experiment shows what happens, or by how much performance degrades, when architectures are close but not identical), so the practical scope of applicability beyond the specific same-architecture pairs tested remains untested rather than merely asserted to be limited.

## Field context

The paper positions itself within the established model-merging literature, explicitly building on and comparing against task arithmetic (Ilharco et al. 2023) and more recent conflict-resolving merging methods (Ties Merging, EMR Merging), and explicitly distinguishes its contribution from prior model-merging work by noting that essentially all of that prior work merges models operating within a single modality, whereas this paper is specifically concerned with merging across a modality boundary (a text-only math LLM into a vision-and-text MLLM), which the paper argues introduces the parameter-space alignment problem that motivates its entire method. It also draws on and extends a specific empirical pattern from separate prior work on where reasoning ability is stored in transformer parameters (deeper MLP layers rather than early attention layers), using this as corroborating context for its own finding that automatically-selected high-similarity parameters concentrate in exactly those deeper MLP layers. Within this corpus, this paper's core idea, that useful, specialized capability from one already-trained model can be transferred into another without any additional training or generated data, by directly and carefully manipulating existing model weights, sits alongside two other "combine multiple existing models more cleverly" techniques reviewed earlier in this same batch: [2509.14786] (Pre-training under infinite compute), which combines multiple independently-trained models via ensembling and then distillation, and [2510.00931] (FusioN), which combines multiple models' output samples via LLM-based synthesis rather than selection; this paper's parameter-space merging approach is a third, mechanistically distinct point in the same broader design space of "getting more capability out of models you already have without full joint retraining," operating at the weight level rather than the output-sample level, and a natural comparison for future synthesis is how the specific benefits and failure modes of parameter-merging (large gains for weak base models, near-zero gains for already-strong ones, an architecture-compatibility constraint) compare to the sampling-level techniques' own reported strengths and limits.

## Critical discussion

The paper's diagnostic methodology is a genuine strength: rather than proposing a fix and only showing it works, it separately measures and separately validates each of the two specific problems it claims cause naive merging to fail (subspace misalignment and magnitude mismatch), with a targeted controlled experiment for each diagnosis before the full method is even introduced, which gives the resulting IP-Merging method's design real empirical grounding rather than being an arbitrary combination of plausible-sounding tricks. The comparison against genuinely competitive, well-tuned prior merging baselines, together with the explicit general-capability preservation check, addresses two of the most obvious ways such a result could otherwise be overstated (a weak baseline, or an undisclosed cost to other model capabilities).

The clearest limitation is that the paper's headline framing, that the method "enhance[s] the math reasoning ability of MLLMs directly from Math LLMs without compromising their other capabilities," understates how unevenly this benefit is realized across the three base models actually tested: the gain is substantial for the two weaker-math base models (LLaVA-1.5-7B, Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct) but shrinks to a marginal +0.5 percentage points overall, with an actual small regression on one subtask, for the strongest base model tested (InternVL3-8B-Instruct, built on an already math-capable Qwen2.5 backbone). This is not a fatal flaw, since a diminishing-returns pattern for a capability-transfer method as the target model's own competence rises is intuitively reasonable and the paper does report the InternVL3 numbers transparently rather than omitting them, but it does mean the paper's abstract-level framing of consistent, general "free lunch" gains somewhat overstates what is actually a base-model-dependent effect that becomes quite small for the strongest model in the paper's own experiments, and the paper does not explore or predict where this diminishing-returns curve would flatten out entirely for an even stronger base model. A second, self-disclosed but not further probed limitation is the requirement that the MLLM and math LLM share both parameter count and foundation LLM architecture, a real practical constraint given how varied current MLLM and specialist-LLM ecosystems are (different base models, different sizes), and one the paper's own experiments do not test the boundary of. A third, minor point is the unexplained GPS-subtask exception for LLaVA, where the paper's own selection criterion underperforms a competing baseline method on a specific, reasoning-heavy subtask without a mechanistic account beyond an informal, untested hypothesis, leaving open whether the similarity-based selection criterion has a systematic blind spot for certain reasoning styles (e.g., extended geometric proof chains) that the paper does not otherwise investigate.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the two-part diagnosis of why naive cross-modality model merging degrades performance (subspace misalignment and magnitude mismatch) is demonstrated with targeted, controlled evidence rather than only argued informally; IP-Merging reliably outperforms three genuine, competitively-tuned prior merging methods across four math-reasoning benchmarks and three model families without the severe, sometimes catastrophic degradation in general capabilities those baseline methods exhibit; and the specific, quantified pattern that a weaker base model gains substantially more from this kind of parameter transfer than an already-strong one is a real, useful, transparently-reported finding about the technique's actual scope, even though it sits somewhat in tension with the paper's more sweepingly-framed headline claim.
