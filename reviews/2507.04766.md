Citation: Zhang, Y., Ma, Y., Gu, Y., Yang, Z., Zhuang, Y., Wang, F., Huang, Z., Wang, Y., Huang, C., Song, B., Lin, C., Zhao, J. ABench-Physics: Benchmarking Physical Reasoning in LLMs via High-Difficulty and Dynamic Physics Problems. arXiv:2507.04766, 2025.

## What this paper is about

Large language models score increasingly well on math and coding benchmarks, but the paper argues physics has been comparatively under-tested, and that most existing physics benchmarks are too easy (high-school or intro-university level), use multiple-choice formats that sidestep the need to actually produce a numerical solution, or are static in a way that lets a model score well simply by having memorized similar problems during pretraining. The paper introduces ABench-Physics, a 500-problem numerical-answer benchmark of graduate- and Olympiad-level physics, split into a fixed high-difficulty set and a second set built with an automatic variation engine that perturbs the numeric constants inside each problem, specifically to test whether a model's correct answer on one version of a problem generalizes to differently-parameterized versions of the identical underlying physical model.

## Extended summary

The benchmark has two parts. Phy A (400 problems) is a static set of high-difficulty, graduate- or Olympiad-level problems spanning classical mechanics, electromagnetism, optics, quantum physics, electrodynamics, semiconductor and fluid mechanics, atomic physics, and other modern topics; problems were selected via "experimental screening that identified cases where models tend to produce long responses with low accuracy," i.e. explicitly chosen because contemporary LLMs already struggle with them. Phy B (100 problems) is built from problems with clearer, more well-defined computational steps, each paired with a variation engine that perturbs the numeric constants embedded in the ground-truth LaTeX solution to generate multiple variants sharing the identical underlying physical model but different numbers; the paper notes this engine is "still ongoing" development and the current preprint release includes three variants per Phy B question, with a fuller version planned for future updates.

Construction proceeded from real textbook and examination sources, digitized via OCR, manually extracted, automatically quality-checked, and then manually annotated for structural and semantic accuracy. DeepSeek-R1 was used to rewrite problem scenarios (introducing controlled contextual variation), with the rewrites manually reviewed for correctness; every question and its solution was independently verified by one to two human annotators who solved it step by step, and each question was separately paraphrased by an independent reviewer specifically to reduce overlap with public pretraining corpora, with all symbolic derivations and numeric endpoints independently produced and cross-checked. Evaluation requires a numerical answer within a strict 1% relative-error tolerance, with unit and significant-figure requirements specified per question and a lightweight LaTeX-parsing function to normalize model outputs before comparison; on Phy B, a model receives credit for a problem only if it correctly answers every one of that problem's numeric variants, a stricter all-or-nothing criterion than per-variant accuracy.

Across 15 evaluated frontier and near-frontier models (Gemini 2.5 Pro, o3, o1, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1-0528, DeepSeek-V3, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and its Thinking variant, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen-Max, QwQ-32B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B), the best-performing model (Gemini 2.5 Pro with reasoning) solves only 43.0% of Phy A, with most other models clustering well below that, and the weakest tested model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B) solving only 2.3%. On Phy B, models generally score much higher on a single (static, un-perturbed) pass, 49-79% across models, than under the strict all-variants-correct dynamic criterion, 22-55%, an average absolute drop of 22.5 percentage points (worst case 28 points, for o3), which the paper interprets as evidence that a meaningful share of a model's apparent competence on a given problem reflects fitting to that specific instance's numbers rather than a robust grasp of the underlying physical relationship, since the semantic and structural content of the problem is unchanged across variants. A secondary pattern reported is that models trained with reinforcement learning (o3, o1, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) tend to show smaller relative accuracy drops from static to dynamic Phy B evaluation than supervised-fine-tuned counterparts, in some cases overtaking a model that had ranked higher on Phy A, which the authors interpret as RL training producing more robust, less pattern-matching-dependent generalization to numeric variation than SFT alone.

## Learnings

The paper's central, reusable methodological point is a specific, concrete demonstration that a large gap can exist between a model's accuracy on a single instance of a problem and its accuracy on requiring correctness across several numerically-perturbed variants of the identical underlying model, quantified here as a roughly 22.5-percentage-point average drop; this is a directly transferable diagnostic technique for probing whether apparent reasoning competence in any quantitative domain (not just physics) reflects genuine understanding of the underlying relationship or curve-fitting to the specific numbers seen.

A second, more tentative learning is the paper's observation that RL-post-trained models show smaller relative degradation than SFT-only models when facing this kind of numeric perturbation, a specific, testable hypothesis (that RL training produces representations less tied to memorized numeric instances) worth treating as a lead for further investigation rather than an established mechanism, since the paper offers this as an interpretation of a correlational ranking-reversal pattern across a modest number of models rather than a controlled ablation isolating RL as the causal factor.

## Verification

The paper takes real, concrete steps to guard against two specific validity threats: answer-format ambiguity and pretraining contamination. Format ambiguity is addressed by requiring a single numerical answer with specified units and significant figures, checked against ground truth with an explicit 1% relative-error tolerance via a dedicated LaTeX-parsing evaluation function, rather than relying on free-form answer matching or an LLM judge to interpret correctness. Contamination risk is addressed with multiple concrete steps rather than an assumption: every problem and solution was independently verified step-by-step by one to two human annotators, an independent reviewer paraphrased each question specifically to reduce textual overlap with public pretraining corpora, and Phy B's variation engine changes the actual numeric content of each problem so a memorized answer to the original problem cannot directly transfer to its variants. No LLM-judge is used anywhere in the evaluation pipeline (the LaTeX-based extraction function performs a fixed, deterministic numerical comparison), which avoids a class of judge-reliability concerns relevant to open-ended benchmarks reviewed elsewhere in this corpus. What is not verified or discussed is whether the DeepSeek-R1 rewriting step, used to introduce contextual variation into problem scenarios, could itself introduce subtle errors or shifts in problem difficulty that the subsequent manual review might not fully catch, since no inter-annotator agreement statistic or explicit error-rate check on the rewritten/paraphrased problems is reported.

## Field context

The paper positions itself within a fast-growing wave of physics-specific LLM reasoning benchmarks it cites directly: MMLU-Physics (multiple-choice, lower difficulty), UGPhysics (undergraduate-level, symbolic and numeric answer formats), the PHYSICS dataset (large-scale, train/test split for fine-tuning), and PhysBench (vision-language physical-world understanding), distinguishing ABench-Physics by combining graduate/Olympiad-level difficulty, a strict numerical-only answer format, and, most centrally, a dynamic variation mechanism that none of the cited static benchmarks include. It frames this dynamic-evaluation design as a direct response to a broader, separately-cited literature on data contamination and memorization in LLM benchmarks (Deng et al. 2024, Xie et al. 2025, Kiyomaru et al. 2024), applying the general "test robustness to distributional shift, not just point accuracy" principle specifically to physics numerical reasoning. Within this corpus, the paper's central diagnostic, comparing accuracy on a fixed problem instance against accuracy on systematically perturbed variants of the identical underlying structure to isolate genuine reasoning from memorized pattern-matching, is directly comparable in spirit to [25tpbench] (Theoretical Physics Benchmark, cited by this paper as prior work and previously reviewed in this corpus) and to other reviewed benchmarks built around dynamic or contamination-resistant evaluation; a natural point of comparison for a future synthesis is whether the magnitude of the static-to-dynamic accuracy drop reported here (22.5 points average) is consistent with contamination-robustness findings reported in those other benchmarks, or whether ABench-Physics's specific numeric-perturbation approach reveals a larger or smaller gap than benchmarks using other robustness-testing strategies.

## Critical discussion

The paper's dynamic-evaluation design is a genuine methodological strength directly relevant to the contamination concern it is built to address: because Phy B's variants share identical problem structure but different numbers, a large static-to-dynamic accuracy drop is difficult to explain by anything other than the model's accuracy depending partly on the specific numeric instance rather than a general grasp of the underlying relationship, and the paper reports this drop with a clear, reproducible, judge-free numerical evaluation criterion.

A more significant limitation concerns how Phy A's difficulty was established: problems were explicitly selected via screening for cases where current models "tend to produce long responses with low accuracy," meaning the headline finding that "even the most advanced models... perform poorly" on Phy A is, in part, a near-tautological consequence of the selection procedure itself, since problems were chosen specifically because models already failed them during construction. This does not make the underlying observation uninteresting (a benchmark specifically curated to surface hard cases is a legitimate diagnostic tool), but it does mean the paper's framing, that the low accuracy numbers demonstrate a broad, general "substantial gap... between general language proficiency and rigorous quantitative reasoning," somewhat overstates what a selection-filtered difficulty benchmark can establish about typical or representative physics-reasoning competence, as opposed to competence specifically on the subset of problems today's models already struggle with; a benchmark constructed this way will, by design, always show a large gap regardless of how much genuine physics ability current models actually have on the broader problem distribution. A second, more clearly self-disclosed limitation is Phy B's current scale and maturity: the automatic variation engine is explicitly described as "still ongoing" and the released version includes only three variants per question, a comparatively thin basis (three perturbations, all-or-nothing scoring) for the strong claim that a model's accuracy "reveals" its reliance on memorization versus genuine modeling, since three variants provide limited statistical power to distinguish true robustness from chance failure on a difficult perturbation, though the direction and rough magnitude of the reported drop is unlikely to be pure noise given how consistently it appears across all 15 tested models. The RL-outperforms-SFT finding is also reported from a correlational ranking-reversal pattern across a modest and non-controlled set of models (different labs, different scales, different training recipes beyond just RL-vs-SFT), so it should be read as a suggestive association rather than a controlled demonstration that RL training specifically, as opposed to other differences between these particular models, causes the observed robustness gain.

What remains well supported: current frontier LLMs, across a wide range of providers and architectures, show a real, consistent, and substantial drop in accuracy when required to correctly solve several numerically-perturbed variants of the same physics problem rather than just the single originally-posed instance, and this specific, judge-free, reproducibly-measured pattern is difficult to attribute to evaluation artifacts given the deterministic scoring protocol. The claim that today's best LLMs struggle with graduate/Olympiad-level physics specifically on the curated hard subset in Phy A is also well supported by the reported numbers, though should not be read as a claim about physics-reasoning competence on an unfiltered or representative problem distribution.
