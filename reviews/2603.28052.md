## What this paper is about

The code wrapped around an LLM — what gets stored in memory, what gets retrieved, how the prompt is assembled, when to ask again — can swing performance on the same benchmark by as much as 6×, yet this "harness" is almost always designed by hand. Existing automated methods for improving text/code artifacts (OPRO, TextGrad, AlphaEvolve, GEPA, and similar "text optimizers") exist, but the authors argue they're built for a different, easier regime: they compress feedback into scalar scores or short summaries because that's what fits when optimizing a single prompt or a stateless function. A harness is a stateful, long-horizon program where a single design decision can affect behavior many steps later, so compressed feedback throws away exactly the information needed to trace a failure back to its cause. Meta-Harness's answer is to stop compressing: give a coding-agent "proposer" full filesystem access to every prior harness's source code, scores, and complete execution traces, and let it decide what to read.

## Extended summary

Meta-Harness runs a simple outer loop (Algorithm 1): maintain a population of evaluated harnesses and a filesystem recording each one's full source code, scores, and execution traces (prompts, tool calls, model outputs, state updates); at each iteration, a coding-agent proposer (Claude Code with Opus-4.6 in all experiments) queries this filesystem via ordinary tools like grep and cat — reading a median of 82 files per iteration, roughly split between prior source code (41%) and execution traces (40%) — then proposes new harness candidates, which are evaluated and logged back into the filesystem for the next iteration to inspect. There is no parent-selection rule, no fixed mutation operator, and no hand-designed search structure beyond a minimal "skill" describing the directory layout and constraints; the paper frames this minimalism as deliberate, since it lets search quality improve automatically as underlying coding-agent capability improves rather than being bottlenecked by hand-designed heuristics. A single evaluation can generate up to 10,000,000 tokens of diagnostic information — roughly three orders of magnitude beyond the largest per-step feedback budgets used in the compared prior text-optimization methods (Table 1 puts OPRO/TextGrad/AlphaEvolve/GEPA/Feedback-Descent/TTT-Discover all in the 2K-26K-token range per iteration).

Three evaluation domains are studied. (1) Online text classification (LawBench, Symptom2Disease, USPTO-50k), where an LLM (GPT-OSS-120B) sees labeled examples one at a time and is evaluated on a held-out test set. Under a compute-matched comparison (same proposer configuration, same evaluation budget) against Best-of-N, OpenEvolve, and TTT-Discover, Meta-Harness matches the best prior method's final search-set accuracy using 10× fewer evaluations and ultimately exceeds it by more than 10 points (Figure 1/4, Table 4). Against hand-designed context-management baselines (ACE, MCE) and few-shot prompting, the selected Meta-Harness harness reaches 48.6% test accuracy versus ACE's 40.9% and MCE's 40.0%, while using 11.4K context tokens versus ACE's 50.8K and MCE's 28.5K (Table 2) — a genuine accuracy-and-efficiency win rather than a pure accuracy-for-cost trade. A controlled ablation isolates which part of the interface matters: giving the proposer only scores, or scores plus LLM-generated summaries (but no raw execution traces), caps median accuracy at 34.6-34.9%, while the full interface with raw traces reaches 50.0% median (Table 3) — the paper's strongest single piece of evidence that raw traces, not just "more search," are doing the work, since summarization actively discards the signal rather than merely compressing it losslessly. An out-of-distribution test on nine entirely unseen classification datasets (SciCite, FiNER-139, Amazon Reviews, Financial PhraseBank, GoEmotions, Banking77, AG News, SciTail, TweetEval-Hate) shows the selected harness generalizing, with the best average accuracy (73.1%, vs. ACE's 70.2%) and best performance on 6/9 individual datasets.

(2) Retrieval-augmented olympiad math reasoning, a domain the paper notes retrieval has historically helped less in than fact-grounded settings. A 40-iteration search over a 250-problem search set (using GPT-OSS-20B, with a ≥500,000-problem retrieval corpus explicitly decontaminated against evaluation sets via exact prefix matching plus fuzzy Jaccard similarity, with manual inspection of top BM25 retrievals for held-out examples) produces a single selected harness — a four-route lexical router (combinatorics/geometry/number-theory/default) using domain-specific BM25 reranking and deduplication strategies per route — evaluated on 200 unseen IMO-level problems across the search model plus four held-out models never used during search (GPT-5.4-nano/mini, Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite, Gemini-3-Flash). The discovered harness improves over no-retrieval by 4.7 points on average across all five models and edges out plain BM25 retrieval by 1.3 points, while avoiding the regressions dense retrieval and random few-shot prompting show on some models (Table 6).

(3) Agentic coding on TerminalBench-2 (89 long-horizon, autonomous-execution tasks), initialized from two strong open baselines (Terminus 2, Terminus-KIRA) and evaluated on the same 89-task benchmark used for search — explicitly justified as a "discovery problem" given the benchmark's cost and the fact that competing published systems already report benchmark-specific iteration, with overfitting checked via manual inspection and regex audits for task-specific string leakage rather than a held-out split. On Claude Opus 4.6, the discovered harness reaches 76.4% pass rate, beating hand-engineered Terminus-KIRA (74.7%) and ranking #2 on the public leaderboard behind ForgeCode (81.8%), a result the authors state they could not reproduce from ForgeCode's public code alone. On the weaker Claude Haiku 4.5, the discovered harness reaches 37.6%, the best score among all reported Haiku 4.5 agents (next-best: Goose at 35.5%). A detailed qualitative trace analysis (Appendix A.2) walks through the actual search log: the first six iterations all regress because structural bugfixes get bundled with prompt-template rewrites; by iteration 3 the proposer explicitly identifies the prompt rewrites (not the bugfixes) as the shared cause of the regressions, tests an isolated version to confirm the diagnosis, and by iteration 7 pivots to a purely additive fix (injecting an environment snapshot before the agent's first turn) that becomes the best candidate — presented as direct evidence the proposer performs causal reasoning over its own failure history rather than undirected mutation.

## Learnings

The most transferable idea is the paper's core architectural bet, made explicit in its comparison table (Table 1): existing text/code optimizers scale by aggressively compressing feedback (a scalar score, a short reflective summary, a fixed-size window of recent attempts), and this compression is a pragmatic engineering choice for their target regime, not evidence that the discarded information is unimportant. Meta-Harness tests this directly with a controlled ablation (Table 3) rather than merely asserting it: giving the proposer LLM-generated summaries of execution traces performs no better (in fact slightly worse on best-found accuracy) than giving it scores alone, while giving it the raw traces themselves produces a large jump. This is a specific, falsifiable finding — not just "more context helps" but "summarized context does not substitute for raw context in this setting" — that generalizes as a design principle beyond harness optimization specifically: for diagnosing why a long-horizon, stateful system failed, a compressed summary can discard precisely the causal information a diagnostic process needs, even when the summary is generated by a capable model.

A second learning is the specific proposer design choice of exposing history through a filesystem navigable with ordinary developer tools (grep, cat) rather than through a fixed prompt template or a hand-designed retrieval mechanism over past attempts. This converts "how much history can the optimizer see" from a fixed context-budget constraint into an agent capability question (can the coding agent decide what's relevant and go find it), which is a substantively different scaling axis than increasing a fixed context window, and ties the method's future improvement directly to general-purpose coding-agent capability rather than to hand-designed retrieval-over-history heuristics that would need separate engineering effort to improve.

A third learning, illustrated concretely by the TerminalBench-2 trace analysis rather than asserted abstractly, is the value of a search process that produces an auditable causal narrative rather than only a final artifact: the proposer's own logged reasoning ("Root cause of regressions: Prompt template changes... The structural bugfixes were confounded with harmful prompt changes") is a specific instance of using accumulated experience to isolate confounded interventions — a general debugging pattern (bundle two independent changes, observe a joint failure, then deliberately re-test them in isolation to attribute causal responsibility) that is good experimental practice independent of whether an AI or a human is doing it, and the paper's contribution is showing a coding agent can be prompted to actually execute this pattern reliably when given the raw evidence to reason over.

## Verification

The paper takes verification seriously across all three domains in ways that are individually checkable. For text classification, the compute-matched comparison protocol (same proposer configuration, same evaluation budget across all compared search methods, test sets held out until final evaluation, candidate selection based solely on search-set performance) is a genuine controlled comparison rather than a comparison across incommensurable setups, and the interface ablation (Table 3) isolates the specific causal claim (raw traces, not merely more search budget or more information in any form, drive the improvement) with a proper controlled condition rather than relying on an uncontrolled before/after comparison. For math retrieval, the decontamination procedure is unusually thorough and explicitly described: exact prefix matching plus fuzzy Jaccard similarity (threshold 0.8) against both evaluation benchmarks and the search set, filtering of OpenMathReasoning entries whose source matches any evaluation benchmark family before deduplication, and a stated manual inspection of top BM25 retrievals for held-out examples — a level of contamination-risk mitigation this corpus has seen inconsistently applied elsewhere. For TerminalBench-2, the paper is transparent about the one place its verification is weakest: search and final evaluation share the same 89-task benchmark with no held-out split, a limitation the authors justify (cost, benchmark scarcity, and the fact that competing systems already engage in benchmark-specific iteration) rather than hide, and partially mitigate via manual inspection and regex-based audits for literal task-specific string leakage into the evolved harness — though this specific check can only catch overt leakage, not subtler forms of overfitting to this particular task distribution's idiosyncrasies. The paper is also honest about a result it could not independently verify: it reports being unable to reproduce ForgeCode's higher-scoring (81.8%) TerminalBench-2 result from ForgeCode's own published code, explicitly flagging that ForgeCode's leaderboard score may depend on unpublished components rather than quietly omitting this caveat.

## Field context

The paper positions itself with unusual precision against the specific text-optimization methods it improves on, using a comparison table (Table 1) that quantifies each prior method's per-iteration context budget in megatokens rather than describing the contrast only qualitatively — OPRO and TextGrad condition on a single prior candidate or the last textual feedback; AlphaEvolve maintains a program database with scalar scores; GEPA and Feedback Descent use LLM-generated reflective summaries; TTT-Discover retains a windowed history of solution fragments — and situates Meta-Harness as considering "orders-of-magnitude more context per artifact evaluation" than any of these. It explicitly separates itself from two adjacent lines of work on the executable-code-search side: evolutionary program search over fixed scaffolds or designated functions (AlphaEvolve/OpenEvolve, FunSearch-style methods, meta-agent-programmed agent design via ADAS) and memory-design search for continual-learning agents (memory that persists across a task stream), noting that Meta-Harness instead searches over domain-specific harnesses that reset between tasks, with a deliberately minimal outer loop (no fixed scaffold, no persistent archive-selection rule) rather than a structured evolutionary algorithm. Within this batch, the paper's central empirical finding — that giving a search/optimization process access to full raw diagnostic history outperforms giving it compressed summaries, even LLM-generated ones — is a direct, controlled demonstration of a pattern this batch has repeatedly gestured at more loosely: 2603.18009's UCPOF similarly gates an expensive fallback (retrieval) on a cheap internal signal, but Meta-Harness's ablation goes further by directly testing and rejecting summarization as a substitute for raw signal, which is a more rigorous instantiation of "don't discard diagnostic information prematurely" than most comparable claims in this batch provide evidence for.

## Critical discussion

The paper's empirical protocol is unusually careful for a method paper claiming broad applicability, but two domain-specific scope limitations are worth naming clearly rather than absorbing into the paper's generally confident framing. First, every headline result depends on a single, very capable proposer model (Claude Code with Opus-4.6), and the Discussion section states this directly: "our experiments demonstrate that harness search can work with one particularly strong coding-agent proposer... a broader study of how the effect varies across proposer agents remains for future work." This is an honest disclosure, but it means the central claim — that unrestricted filesystem access to raw history beats compressed feedback — has been demonstrated only at the high end of current coding-agent capability; it remains untested whether a weaker or less capable proposer model would extract the same benefit from full traces (which require substantial autonomous judgment about what to read and how to interpret it) versus a more structured, compressed interface that does some of that filtering work for a weaker model. Second, the TerminalBench-2 evaluation has no train/test split at all — search and final reported performance share the same 89 tasks — which the paper justifies on practical grounds but which means the 76.4%/37.6% pass rates should be read as "the harness discovered for and evaluated on this specific, fixed, known task set" rather than as evidence of generalization to genuinely unseen long-horizon coding tasks in the way the math and classification OOD experiments more properly demonstrate; the manual/regex leakage audits are a reasonable but partial safeguard, since they can catch literal string hard-coding but not more diffuse forms of fitting to this particular 89-task distribution's structural regularities.

A more general, cross-cutting point: none of the paper's result tables (Tables 2, 4, 5, 6, 7) report variance, confidence intervals, or repeated-run statistics, and several of the closer comparisons (e.g., the 76.4% vs. 74.7% Opus-4.6 TerminalBench-2 margin, corresponding to roughly one to two tasks out of 89; several sub-1-point deltas in the OOD classification table) sit within a range where a single evaluation run's noise could plausibly account for part of the reported gap. The math-reasoning results are somewhat more robust to this concern since they are averaged over three samples per problem across 200 problems and show a consistent positive direction across all five held-out models rather than resting on one favorable comparison, but the paper does not report per-model statistical significance for these gains either.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the paper's most tightly-controlled and mechanistically specific claim — that raw execution traces, not merely a larger information budget in any form, are the active ingredient enabling effective harness search — is demonstrated through a genuine ablation with a real negative control (the summary condition performs no better than scores-only, ruling out "any additional information helps" as the explanation) rather than an uncontrolled comparison, and this is the paper's strongest and most generalizable individual result. The cross-domain breadth of the demonstration (three genuinely different task types: online classification, retrieval-augmented reasoning, and long-horizon agentic coding) with domain-appropriate held-out generalization checks in two of the three domains (unseen datasets for classification; unseen models for math) provides real evidence the underlying mechanism is not narrowly specific to one task type, even accounting for the TerminalBench-2 scope limitation and the single-proposer-model caveat the authors themselves flag as open questions for future work.
