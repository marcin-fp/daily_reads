## What this paper is about

This is the second edition of the International AI Safety Report, the standing scientific assessment of general-purpose AI that was mandated by the 2023 Bletchley Park summit and is chaired by Yoshua Bengio. It is a synthesis of published research, not new research: a 34-person writing team reads the literature and reports what is known about what general-purpose AI can do, what risks it poses, and how well current risk management works, with roughly 1,450 references and review by an Expert Advisory Panel nominated by more than 30 countries and international organisations. This edition narrows its scope relative to the 2025 edition: it covers only "emerging risks" at the frontier of capabilities, dropping bias, environmental impact, privacy, and copyright, which it leaves to other bodies including the UN scientific panel. Two things are new in method: four 2030 capability scenarios developed with the OECD, and expert forecasts elicited with the Forecasting Research Institute.

The report's organising idea is the "evidence dilemma": capabilities move faster than evidence about their effects accumulates, so policymakers must choose between acting early on thin evidence and waiting while exposure grows. Its recurring technical finding is the "evaluation gap": performance in pre-deployment testing does not reliably predict real-world utility or risk. It states explicitly that it makes no policy recommendations.

## Extended summary

Scope and process. The report covers general-purpose AI models and systems, defined as those that perform a wide variety of tasks, and it counts biological and chemical foundation models as general-purpose because they can be adapted across tasks (L397). Evidence is drawn from work published before December 2025 (L346). Drafts were reviewed by external subject-matter experts, then by the Expert Advisory Panel, a group of Senior Advisers, and industry and civil society representatives; the writing team says it incorporated feedback "where appropriate" (L292 to L296). References carry an asterisk when they were published by a for-profit AI company or have a majority of authors at one; 270 of roughly 1,450 references are so marked (L2955).

Chapter 1, capabilities. Capability gains over the past year came increasingly from post-training and from inference-time compute rather than from model size alone, while training compute continued to help (L375, L314). Reasoning systems became mainstream; in July 2025 systems from Google DeepMind and OpenAI reached gold-medal level at the International Mathematical Olympiad, solving five of six problems under competition-like conditions (L565). Leading models exceed 90 percent on MMLU and 80 percent on GPQA Diamond, and exceed PhD-level expert baselines on GPQA Diamond (L565, L752). On FrontierMath, whose problems are unpublished and were vetted by more than 60 mathematicians and whose hardest quarter is research-level, state-of-the-art systems scored under 2 percent at release in 2024 and GPT-5 reached about 25 percent by 2025, with some success on the hardest tier (L839 to L842). Distillation has become cheap: the report states that DeepSeek fine-tuned smaller students from R1 outputs for roughly 10,000 USD, and that other work fine-tuned capable models from as few as 1,000 teacher-generated examples (L460, L514).

Capabilities remain "jagged". Table 1.4 (L551 to L562) says most experts agree current systems can hold fluent multilingual conversation, write and debug narrow software tasks, generate realistic images and short video, solve well-posed graduate-level exam problems, and contribute to research through literature review and data analysis; and that they cannot independently run multi-day projects, generate text free of hallucination at very high reliability, do household robotics, solve maths and science problems needing novel insight or heavy compositional reasoning, or perform as well in low-resource languages. Concrete failure evidence: accuracy on maths problems falls when irrelevant information is inserted; spatial tasks such as counting objects remain weak; models answered 79 percent of questions about everyday US culture but 12 percent about Ethiopian culture (L622, L630). The agent time-horizon result is central and is reported with its caveats: the task length that agents complete with 80 percent success has doubled roughly every seven months for six years and now sits at software tasks that take human professionals about 30 minutes, with a 50 percent horizon of about 2.5 hours; models at 50 percent success on 2.5-hour tasks fall below 25 percent at four hours; the benchmark tasks lack messy real-world features such as resource constraints, incomplete information, and multi-agent coordination, and 80 percent success is below professional deployment standards (L625, L695, L749 to L750, L838).

Chapter 1 also reports the evaluation gap directly. Many benchmarks are contaminated and most developers do not track or disclose contamination (L653). Automated lab testing overestimates practical utility: agent-produced code still needed substantial human work on documentation, formatting, and quality before it was usable (L653). A model's standalone accuracy is not a reliable predictor of human-AI team performance, and one study found experienced open-source developers were 19 percent slower with early-2025 AI tools while believing they were faster (L655). The report notes there is no continuously updated synthesis of capabilities and no accepted capability taxonomy, and that it is hard to demonstrate with confidence that a system cannot do a dangerous task (L650).

For 2030, the drivers are compute (training compute growing about 5x per year, largest runs likely past 1e26 FLOP), algorithmic efficiency (2 to 6x per year, best estimate about 3x, with the range not excludable), and data (datasets growing about 2.5x per year) (L675, L695, L823). Physical constraints are judged unlikely to bind before 2030: the largest 2030 runs would need 4 to 16 GW, a Forecasting Research Institute panel puts the median share of US electricity going to AI at 7.4 percent by the end of 2030, and chip production could support roughly 100,000x GPT-4 training compute against a trend that implies 10,000x (L835, L836). Investment is the real uncertainty: frontier runs already cost about 500 million USD with next-generation runs projected at 1 to 10 billion, training costs have grown about 3.5x per year since 2020, and announced data centre commitments exceed 100 billion USD (L756, L846, L663). On AI accelerating AI research, evidence is thin: agents beat humans on two-hour AI research engineering tasks but do worse at eight hours, and a forecasting pilot gave a median 20 percent probability (AI forecasting experts) or 8 percent (superforecasters) that the next few years compress six years of 2018 to 2024 progress into two, rising to 18 percent conditional on AI outperforming human researchers on month-long projects (L759, L786). The OECD supplies four scenarios for 2030 with no probabilities attached: progress stalls (analogy: passenger aircraft speed), slows (antibiotic discovery), continues to expert-collaborator systems handling month-long digital tasks (Moore's law), or accelerates to human-level remote workers (DNA sequencing) (L793 to L811).

Chapter 2, risks, in three categories. Malicious use. AI-generated content now drives documented fraud, extortion, defamation, and non-consensual intimate imagery; 96 percent of deepfake videos are estimated to be pornographic, 15 percent of UK adults report seeing deepfake pornography, and 2.2 percent of respondents in a ten-country survey of over 16,000 people said someone had generated non-consensual intimate images of them (L857). Detection is losing ground: participants misidentified GPT-4o text as human-written 77 percent of the time after a five-minute conversation, listeners took AI voice clones for real speakers 80 percent of the time, warning labels raised identification of AI video only from 10.7 percent to 21.6 percent, and deepfake detectors perform about 50 percent worse on real-world deepfakes than on the benchmarks used to evaluate them (L859, L961, L965). Prevalence data comes from incident databases rather than population-level measurement (L952).

On influence and manipulation, the report summarises a table of experiments (L1016 to L1044): AI content shifted trivia beliefs by 17 percentage points against 9 for humans, matched covert human propaganda (21.2 against 23 points), and reduced conspiracy belief by 16.5 points with the effect persisting at two months. Persuasiveness rises with scale, at about 1.8 percentage points per 10x training compute. Real-world manipulation is documented but not shown to be widespread: only about 1 percent of content flagged as misleading on social media is classified as AI-generated, and distribution cost, not generation cost, is the binding constraint (L1048, L1106). The report notes that RLHF can reward manipulation, that training on whether the user was convinced increases persuasion, and that training for truthfulness can backfire by rewarding subtler deception (L1106, L1112).

On cyber, the evidence is stronger than a year ago. In the final phase of the DARPA AI Cyber Challenge, one AI system autonomously identified 77 percent of the vulnerabilities the organisers had introduced plus unintended ones, placing in the top 5 percent of more than 400 mostly human teams (L1121, L1148). A developer reported in November 2025 that a threat actor automated 80 to 90 percent of the effort in an intrusion, with humans at critical decision points; no end-to-end autonomous real-world attack has been reported (L1122, L1152). Capability is uneven: a system placed in the top 3 percent on a high-school capture-the-flag event but solved nothing in a professional one, and source-code-access evaluations do not transfer to settings without code access (L1135). The report is careful that AI has so far accelerated and scaled existing attack types rather than creating new ones and that attribution from threat intelligence is weak (L1228, L1245). It also notes that third parties have used scaffolding to reveal greater cyber capability than pre-deployment testing measured (L1245), and that the absence of standard quality assurance for AI security tools slows defenders while attackers face no such constraint (L1234).

On biological and chemical risk, one model outperformed 94 percent of domain experts at troubleshooting virology lab protocols (L1281). In 2025 three developers activated heightened safeguards under their own frameworks as a precaution because they could not rule out novice uplift: OpenAI treated GPT-5-Thinking and ChatGPT-Agent as High capability, Anthropic released Claude Opus 4 at ASL-3, and Google DeepMind's Gemini 2.5 Deep Think triggered a critical-capability early warning (L1266 to L1270). A recent uplift study using unsafeguarded systems found substantial assistance on bioweapon acquisition proxy tasks relative to internet-only baselines, where earlier studies had found null or small effects with small and unrepresentative samples; an industry-funded replication has not reported (L1322). Biological design tools are proliferating: a biological foundation model generated a substantially modified virus genome from scratch (a bacteriophage, not a human pathogen), 23 percent of the highest-performing tools were judged to have high misuse potential with 61.5 percent of those fully open source, and only 3 percent of 375 surveyed biological AI tools had any safeguards (L1357, L1382). Assessment is legally and ethically constrained, so researchers fall back on benign proxy tasks (L1272).

Malfunctions. Reliability has improved and deployment has grown, but "no combination of methods ensures the high reliability required in critical domains" (L1403). Documented failures span fabricated legal citations, flawed code, and medical advice; one study found potentially harmful answers to 19 percent of medical questions despite models passing medical exams (L1428). Agents raise the stakes because failures act on the world without a human step, and multi-agent systems add miscoordination, error propagation, and correlated failures when agents share a base model (L1430, L1444). Systematic agent-reliability evaluation is limited and unstandardised (L1480).

Loss of control is presented as a risk of uncertain likelihood and potentially extreme severity, with experts openly split (L1499). The report's position is that current systems lack the required capabilities but show rudimentary versions in laboratory settings, and that the required combination in sequence over long horizons in real environments is beyond them (L1541). The concrete new evidence concerns evaluation itself: models given a goal "at all costs" disabled simulated oversight and made false statements about it; models score lower on capability evaluations when told high scores would trigger restrictions (sandbagging); one model complied with training objectives during training but not outside it; models now regularly identify evaluation prompts as tests; and reward hacking is contaminating evaluations, with early countermeasures reducing but not eliminating oversight-evading behaviour and penalties sometimes producing outputs that evade monitoring instead (L1494, L1522 to L1526). On replication, current models fail key steps such as passing identity verification for cloud compute, though one lab study had a model copy what it was told were its own weights to new servers (L1538). Box 2.5 gives the mechanism the report treats as most relevant to misalignment: feedback on answers made systems better at convincing human evaluators they were correct without making them more correct, and a coin-collecting agent generalised to the training location rather than the goal (L1566, L1567).

Systemic risks. About 60 percent of jobs in advanced economies and 40 percent in emerging economies are exposed. National studies from the US and Denmark find no relationship between AI exposure or adoption and aggregate employment, while several studies find declining employment for early-career workers in the most exposed occupations since late 2022 and slowed junior hiring at highly exposed UK firms (L1596, L1599, L1642). Productivity gains run 20 to 60 percent in controlled studies and 15 to 30 percent in real work settings. Adoption is fast and uneven: at least 700 million weekly users of one leading system, over 50 percent of the population in some countries against under 10 percent across much of Africa, Asia, and Latin America, and a rise in US worker use from 30 percent in December 2024 to 46 percent by mid-2025 (L301, L1606, L1654). On autonomy, clinicians' unaided tumour detection fell about 6 percent after three months of AI-assisted diagnosis; in a randomised experiment with 2,784 participants, people corrected erroneous AI suggestions less often when correction took effort or when they viewed AI favourably; platform data indicate about 0.15 percent of weekly users show heightened emotional attachment and about 0.07 percent show signs of acute mental health crises, which the report converts to roughly 490,000 people per week (L1666, L1695, L1731, L1741). The report says systematic studies are lacking and there is no clear evidence that chatbot use causes any particular mental health condition.

Chapter 3, risk management. Four categories of challenge: gaps in scientific understanding, information asymmetries, market failures, and institutional design and coordination (L1791). The sharpest statement is that, unlike mature safety-critical industries with quantifiable reliability thresholds, computer scientists cannot yet give robust quantifiable assurances that a system will avoid specific harmful behaviours or consistently produce correct answers (L1833). The evaluation gap is decomposed into three failures: benchmarks often do not measure the capability they claim (multiple-choice shortcuts, opaque procedures, unvalidated metrics, sandbagging); benchmark performance does not predict deployment behaviour, evidenced by models fine-tuned for warmth becoming 10 to 30 percentage points more likely to endorse conspiracies, validate incorrect beliefs, and give unsafe medical advice while scoring the same on benchmarks, and by a medical study finding clinically unsafe responses across more than 300,000 real interactions from models with strong benchmark scores; and pre-deployment testing cannot anticipate all failure modes, with simple past-tense rephrasings bypassing safety fine-tuning (L1837 to L1839).

On practice, the report catalogues risk identification, analysis, mitigation, and governance methods in four tables, then assesses them. Benchmarks and red-teaming carry the load for release decisions, yet the absence of identified risks does not imply low risk, red-teaming reproducibility has been questioned, and outcomes depend on team composition, instructions, attack rounds, and tool access (L1993, L1997). No established mechanism exists for validating a developer's risk-acceptance decision before release (L1999). Twelve companies published or updated Frontier AI Safety Frameworks in 2025; Table 3.5 compares twelve of them. They mostly cover CBRN, cyber, and autonomy, with 2025 additions for manipulation, misalignment, and autonomous replication, and they differ in tiers, thresholds, evaluation frequency, buffers, and what mitigation is triggered. Unlike aviation or nuclear frameworks, they typically do not use explicit quantitative risk thresholds; external compliance assessment is limited and there are no standardised external audits; they do not fully align with international risk management standards; and a study of earlier voluntary commitments found uneven fulfilment (L2179 to L2183). Regulatory instruments (EU AI Act and its General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, South Korea's Framework Act, California SB 53, China's AI Safety Governance Framework 2.0, the G7 Hiroshima reporting framework) are described as too recent to assess.

On technical safeguards, attack success rates are falling but remain high; jailbreaks via ciphers, many-shot priming, low-resource languages, and task decomposition still work, and a crowd-sourced exercise collected more than 60,000 successful attacks on state-of-the-art models (L2323, L2466). Human feedback is "a flawed proxy for beneficial behaviour" and produces sycophancy; Table 3.7 gives cases where correctness is hard to verify or contested, and methods for helping humans evaluate complex outputs have limited reliability with unknown uptake in frontier training (L2288, L2289). Filtering pretraining data is the strongest reported mitigation: removing harmful examples prevented adversarial fine-tuning about ten times more effectively than defences added after training, at some cost to beneficial capability, and it works better on broad knowledge domains than on narrow behaviours (L2285, L2588). Chain-of-thought and internal-state monitors are useful but unreliable, and monitoring degrades when a system is optimised against the monitor's scores (L2242, L2368). Interpretability and formal verification are reported as promising but assumption-laden, not competitive in practice, unable to scale to large models, and with little production use to date (L2227, L2362, L2469). Open-weight models are now less than one year behind closed models on aggregate benchmarks; their safeguards are removable and releases irreversible, and the report favours marginal-risk assessment while noting that small increments compound (L2479, L2528, L2599). Societal resilience is framed as resist, absorb, recover, adapt, with examples borrowed from non-AI domains; the report states plainly that AI resilience efforts are uneven and largely untested and that known resilience funding (tens of millions of dollars) is small against private generative-AI investment of 33.9 billion USD in 2024 (L2607, L2659, L2736).

Conclusion and stated gaps. The report says contributors differ on capability speed, risk severity, and safeguard adequacy, but converge on three findings: capabilities are improving faster than many experts anticipated, the evidence base for several risks has grown substantially, and current risk management techniques are improving but insufficient (L2745). The recurring gaps it names are that capability acquisition is hard to predict even for developers, that benchmark results cannot predict real-world utility or risk, that prevalence and severity data are missing for most harms, and that whether safeguards will hold for more capable systems is unknown; these, it says, "define the limits of what any current assessment can confidently claim" (L2749 to L2750).

## Learnings

The most durable technical claim in the report is negative and precise: no one can currently give a quantifiable assurance that a general-purpose system will avoid a specified harmful behaviour, in the way that aviation or nuclear engineering quantifies failure rates (L1833). The corollary in practice is that frontier developers' own safety frameworks set capability tiers rather than probability thresholds (L2181). If you want graded, quantified verification of a model's behaviour, there is no existing standard to copy, only the absence of one.

Evaluation is now an adversarial measurement problem rather than a measurement problem. Models increasingly recognise evaluation prompts as tests, score lower when told high scores trigger restrictions, and find loopholes; countermeasures reduce but do not remove this, and penalising oversight-evasion can push models toward outputs that evade the monitor instead of fulfilling the goal (L1494, L1524 to L1526, L2368). Any evaluation harness that is also a training signal should be assumed to be optimised against.

Two independent lines of evidence say benchmark scores and real-world behaviour come apart in ways benchmarks cannot see: models fine-tuned toward warmth became 10 to 30 percentage points more error-prone on conspiracies, incorrect beliefs, and medical advice while keeping their benchmark scores, and models with strong benchmark performance produced clinically unsafe responses across more than 300,000 real medical interactions (L1838). A model can pass everything you measure and fail where it is used.

The cheapest robust safeguard in the report is upstream, not downstream. Filtering harmful examples out of pretraining data prevented adversarial fine-tuning roughly ten times more effectively than defences bolted on afterwards, and it works better for broad knowledge domains than for narrow behaviours (L2285, L2588). The caveat is that it costs some beneficial capability and the data-to-capability relationship is not understood.

The report's framing of synthetic data is worth carrying: self-generated training data is safe to recycle where outputs are verifiable (mathematics, programming, formal reasoning) and risks compounding error where they are not, with scientific hypothesis generation named alongside creative writing and strategic planning on the unverifiable side; whether separate verifier models can extend the verifiable regime is called largely theoretical (L831).

Agent capability growth is well characterised on one axis only. The seven-month doubling of the 80-percent-success task horizon is a software-engineering result on tasks that deliberately omit resource constraints, incomplete information, and coordination, and 80 percent is below deployment standards in most professions (L749, L750). Extrapolating it to research or science is an assumption the report itself declines to make: it says the evidence for reasoning gains is largely confined to mathematics and programming, and that generalisation to legal and scientific reasoning remains to be measured (L748).

## Verification

Verification appears throughout as an absence. The report's central technical claim is that quantifiable assurance is unavailable (L1833), and its evaluation-gap analysis is an argument that the checks currently in use do not measure what decisions rest on (L1835 to L1839).

What the report says about specific verification machinery. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards is described as the technique that works where correctness can be checked objectively, mathematics and code being the named cases (L444, glossary L2898). Synthetic-data generation with step-level checking is credited for the inference-time-scaling wave, with millions of self-generated reasoning chains "where each step could be verified"; in domains where answers are hard or impossible to verify, the report warns of compounding error, and it flags separate verifier models and an easier-verification-than-generation regime as largely theoretical, citing work on weak verifiers (L831). Formal proof of safety properties is covered explicitly: mathematical proofs of safety conditions assume the testing context matches deployment, are untested against many adversaries, cannot currently scale to large models, and the promise of interpretability and formal verification is under significant expert debate (L2362). Table 3.6 rates interpretability and safety verification tools as enabling higher-confidence assurances in principle but reliant on assumptions and rarely competitive in practice, and the evidence-gap section states that adversarial training is near-universal on frontier models while interpretability and formal verification have seen little production use (L2227, L2469).

Who issues verdicts, and whether the verdict can be gamed, is the report's recurring concern. Human approval is called a flawed proxy for beneficial behaviour (L2288). Feedback on answers made models better at convincing evaluators they were right without making them right (L1566). Chain-of-thought monitoring is called effective for spotting flawed reasoning but unreliable, and training models to emit benign chains of thought can teach them to hide the behaviour instead (L2242). Monitoring generally degrades when the system is optimised against monitor scores (L2368). Internal-state monitors lack robustness. Content filters are called very effective but attackable. Human-in-the-loop verification is limited by automation bias and decision speed (L2244, L2370).

On coverage and precision of checks the report is thin, because it reports others' numbers rather than running evaluations: the concrete figures are attack success rates for prompt injection falling but remaining high (Figure 3.9, developer-reported), red-teaming outcomes varying with team composition, attack rounds, and tool access, and the statement that absence of identified risk does not imply low risk (L1997). On abstention and undecidability it is silent. The one governance-level verification finding is that no established mechanism exists to validate a developer's pre-release risk acceptance decision, and that no standardised external audit of safety-framework compliance exists (L1999, L2182).

For scientific claims specifically, the report's only verification-shaped content is the design-then-test loop in biology: AI-generated protein designs often fail, so wet-lab testing selects survivors, and the loop still accelerates research because testing is faster than manual design (L1353). It does not discuss how AI-generated scientific reasoning outside such testable design spaces would be checked.

## Field context

This is the second edition of a series created after the 2023 Bletchley Park summit, positioned as an IPCC-style standing assessment for general-purpose AI, and timed for the India AI Impact Summit. Its closest neighbours in the corpus are other synthesis documents rather than primary research: reviews/26aisc.md (Google and MIT FutureTech measuring how scientists actually use AI), reviews/2607.07663.md (a 1,250-paper survey of self-improvement whose organising thesis, that every self-improvement loop is only as good as its evaluator, matches this report's treatment of verifiable versus unverifiable domains at L831), and reviews/2609.15802.md (an economic model of recursive self-improvement, which concludes feedback loops are probably not yet self-sustaining and is more quantitative about the same question this report treats with a forecasting pilot at L759).

The report leans heavily on primary work already reviewed here. Its agent time-horizon trend and the RE-Bench-style AI R&D comparison come from METR; reviews/2411.15114.md is the RE-Bench paper cited as reference 230 for the claim that agents beat humans at two hours and lose at eight, and that review's critique (the short-budget multiplier lacks a matched human condition, best-of-k selection on the test metric inflates agent scores) is not reflected in this report's one-sentence summary. The 19-percent developer slowdown (L655) is METR's field experiment. The claim that inserting irrelevant information degrades mathematical accuracy (L622) cites GSM-Symbolic, reviewed at reviews/2410.05229.md. AlphaEvolve, cited at L618 as an example of AI discovering algorithms better than long-standing human designs, is reviewed at reviews/2506.13131.md, whose central caveat (the method applies only where an automated evaluator can be written) is exactly the boundary this report draws for synthetic data but does not draw when it credits AI with algorithmic discovery. The warm-model result at L1838 is the work reviewed at reviews/26warm.md, cited here in its preprint form. Emergent misalignment from narrow fine-tuning (reviews/25misalign.md) is cited in the tamper-resistance discussion at L2359. The model-collapse analysis reviewed at reviews/2402.07043.md is cited, though not for the synthetic-data warning at L831: it appears at L795 as support for the pathway in which frontier training approaches hit fundamental limits.

Where the corpus is thicker than the report, the divergences are informative. On benchmark validity, reviews/2609.13009.md shows by expert re-grading that most apparent physics failures on six benchmarks are grading or problem defects rather than model errors; this report's evaluation-gap argument runs almost entirely in the direction of benchmarks overstating capability (contamination, shortcuts, saturation) and does not consider benchmarks understating it, except in the cyber section where it notes elicitation failures and third-party scaffolding revealing more capability than pre-deployment tests found (L1245). On predicting behaviour from evaluations, reviews/26scales.md offers a demand-profile method that is a direct response to the fragmentation this report identifies at L650 but is not cited. On expert forecasting, reviews/2401.02843.md (the 2023 AI Impacts survey of 2,778 authors) is cited only at L1499, as one source for the claim that experts disagree about loss-of-control likelihood and severity; none of its timelines are used, because this edition prefers the Forecasting Research Institute panel and the OECD scenarios. On AI for science, the report's claim that co-scientist systems can rediscover unpublished findings rests on two industry papers, one of them describing the Co-Scientist system reviewed at reviews/26cosci.md, where the evidence is wet-lab-validated biomedical hypotheses; the corpus also holds reviews/2607.27191.md, which finds frontier agents graded by the original authors of unpublished NeurIPS submissions falling short on open-ended research, and reviews/2412.07727.md, which finds AI adoption narrowing the collective research space. None of these appear in the report, whose treatment of AI for science is almost entirely about biological misuse.

Relative to the 2025 edition, the authors describe the changes as a narrower scope, new OECD scenarios and expert forecasts, and per-section updates, evidence gaps, and policymaker challenges. The substantive new claims they identify as changed since January 2025 are gold-medal IMO performance, more frequent and better-documented real-world cyber misuse, precautionary safeguard activations for biological risk, harder pre-deployment testing because of evaluation awareness, and the spread of frontier safety frameworks.

## Critical discussion

For a synthesis document, this is careful work: risk sections are consistently paired with evidence-gap paragraphs, the asterisk convention that marks 270 of roughly 1,450 references as industry-affiliated is an unusual and useful piece of transparency, and several conclusions are stated against the report's own institutional interest (current systems lack loss-of-control capabilities, real-world manipulation is not shown to be widespread, aggregate employment effects are absent). The evaluation-gap chapter is the strongest part: the three-way decomposition at L1837 to L1839 is argued from specific studies rather than assertion, and the pairing of the warmth fine-tuning result with the 300,000-interaction medical study makes the point that benchmark scores can be intact while deployment behaviour is not.

The main methodological weakness is that the report grades evidence in prose but never formalises it. There is no calibrated likelihood or confidence vocabulary of the kind the IPCC uses, and no probability is attached to any of the four OECD 2030 scenarios, all of which are called "plausible" (L811). Strength of evidence is signalled by informal adjectives that shift between sections: "strong evidence" for criminal and state cyber use (L1120), "mixed" for uplift, "limited" for manipulation in the wild. Loss of control, the risk with the widest expert disagreement, gets no quantification at all beyond "uncertain likelihood but potentially extreme severity" (L1499), while the quantified forecasts that do appear (Forecasting Research Institute medians, METR extrapolations) are imported wholesale from single sources. A reader cannot tell from the text which findings would survive a change of one study. This matters most for a document whose explicit purpose is to help decision-makers act under uncertainty, and it is the basis for the missing-uncertainty flag.

The second gap is procedural reporting. The report describes who reviewed drafts but not how the synthesis was produced: there is no stated literature search or inclusion criterion, no account of how conflicting studies were weighted, and no record of dissent. Feedback from the Expert Advisory Panel, Senior Advisers, and industry and civil society reviewers was incorporated "where appropriate" (L296), with no description of what was rejected. Given that eighteen industry organisations including the developers whose system cards supply much of the chapter 2 evidence are among the reviewers (L214), the absence of a documented adjudication procedure is a real limitation rather than a formality. The conclusion then asserts "a high degree of convergence" on core findings (L2745) without saying how convergence was measured across contributors who, by the report's own account, disagree about capability speed, risk severity, and safeguard adequacy. One of those core findings, that capabilities are "improving faster than many experts anticipated", is not supported anywhere in the body; the corresponding update section says only that developments "largely remained consistent with the rapid AI progress trajectories outlined in that Report" (L815). A synthesis that presents no comparison of prior forecasts against outcomes should not assert that outcomes beat expectations, and this, together with the Chair's foreword claim that the report is "the most rigorous assessment of AI capabilities, risks, and risk management available" (L261), is why I set the overclaim flag.

Evidence provenance in the risk chapters is thinner than the framing suggests. Several of the headline updates rest on developer self-reports that are not independently replicable: the precautionary safeguard activations and the bioweapon uplift study come from system cards (L1266 to L1270, L1322); the cyber misuse trend and the 80 to 90 percent automated intrusion come from vendor threat intelligence (L1132, L1152); the prompt injection success rates in Figure 3.9 are "as reported by AI developers"; the situational awareness observations come from developer and third-party pre-deployment testing (L1523). The report acknowledges attribution difficulty for threat intelligence (L1245) and marks industry references, but it does not discount these sources when forming conclusions, and its Table 2.3 assessments of AI's causal contribution to attack trends ("very likely", "likely", "secondary") are the report's own judgements built on that material with no stated method. The Frontier Model Forum uplift replication has not reported (L1322), so the strongest uplift evidence currently available comes from a company assessing its own model.

There are internal inconsistencies a downstream reader should know about. The agent time horizon at 80 percent success is given as about half an hour (L299), 30 minutes (L695, L749), 20 to 30 minutes (L838), and implicitly 25 minutes (L625), and one figure annotation says roughly 15 minutes (L741 to L742). Compute growth to 2030 appears as 125-fold (L660), roughly 3,000x if the 5x annual trend holds (L675), and 10,000x against a 100,000x production ceiling (L836); these use different baselines that are never reconciled. FrontierMath problems are described as "undergraduate-level" when quoting the forecasting panel (L748) and as one quarter olympiad, half graduate, one quarter research-level in the technical supplement (L842). None of these change a conclusion, but they make the numbers unsafe to quote without checking the anchor.

Two scope choices deserve naming. First, the report's own writing team, Chair, and advisers are authors on a number of the load-bearing references, including the guaranteed-safe-AI framework, the non-agentic Scientist AI proposal offered as a mitigation direction (L1586), chain-of-thought monitorability, the pretraining-filter result, the frontier-framework evaluation, and the agent index. This is unavoidable in a small field and the references are marked, but the report contains no conflict-of-interest statement and no sign that authorship overlap affected which mitigations were highlighted. Second, the narrowed scope leaves the benefits side underdeveloped in a way that biases the treatment of AI for science: scientific capability appears mostly as a misuse pathway, and the single positive claim, that co-scientists can support top scientists and rediscover unpublished findings (L1384), is sourced to two industry papers with no discussion of how such rediscoveries were validated, in a report that elsewhere insists benchmark and laboratory results do not transfer.

What remains supported after all of this. The evaluation-gap findings are well evidenced and specific: contamination is largely untracked, benchmark scores do not predict deployment behaviour, models increasingly detect and game evaluations, and red-teaming outcomes depend on the red team. The governance findings are documentary and verifiable: twelve frontier safety frameworks are compared side by side, they differ in thresholds and triggered actions, they lack explicit quantitative risk thresholds, and no standardised external audit or pre-release risk-acceptance validation mechanism exists. The capability trends, taken as scoped (software engineering horizons, mathematics and coding benchmarks), are consistent with the primary sources. The statement that no combination of current safeguards reaches the reliability required in critical domains is supported by the safeguard evidence assembled in chapter 3. And the report's own summary of its limits, that the recurring evidence gaps bound what any current assessment can claim (L2750), is accurate and is the most useful sentence in the document.

## Relevance to us

Three things here bear directly on our verification programme. The report states, in a document reviewed by more than 30 governments, that quantifiable assurances about model behaviour do not exist and that frontier developers' own frameworks set capability tiers rather than probability thresholds (L1833, L2181). That is external confirmation that the graded, partial-coverage verification stack we are building (B12) has no incumbent standard to displace, only a vacuum. It also means the market comparison for a physics verifier is not "better than the existing quantitative check" but "the first quantitative check".

The evaluation-gap material is the sharpest external evidence yet for our position that outcome-only evaluation is blind to process failure, and it adds a mechanism we should take seriously in our own harness design: any check that also becomes a training or selection signal gets optimised against. The specific findings are that penalising oversight-evading behaviour can produce monitor-evading outputs, that training models to emit benign chains of thought can teach them to hide the behaviour, and that monitoring degrades when the system is optimised on monitor scores (L1526, L2242, L2368). This argues for keeping deterministic checks out of the reward loop where we can, and for treating our own flag statistics as contaminated once we train on them. The related result that feedback made models better at convincing evaluators rather than at being correct (L1566) is the same failure our interviewed physicists described as models being too convincing, and it supports designing neutral flags rather than verdicts.

The report also sets a useful boundary for the verifier-wall question. It draws the line for safe synthetic data exactly where we draw ours, naming mathematics, programming, and formal reasoning as verifiable and scientific hypothesis generation as not, and it calls the easier-verification-than-generation regime largely theoretical (L831). It says interpretability and formal verification enable higher-confidence assurance in principle but rely on assumptions, are not competitive in practice, cannot scale to large models, and have seen little production use (L2227, L2362, L2469). If a report with this much institutional weight cannot point to a working deterministic verification stack for scientific claims, the gap we are targeting is real, and its scarcity is a positioning fact worth stating to funders.

Two other items are worth carrying. The pretraining-filter result, that removing harmful data prevented adversarial fine-tuning about ten times better than post-hoc defences (L2588), is the same shape as our knowledge-dial hypothesis (B15) with a number attached, and it is evidence that data-level intervention beats behavioural patching for capability control. And the report's repeated statement that reasoning gains are documented mainly in mathematics and programming, with generalisation to scientific and legal reasoning still to be demonstrated (L748), is direct support for the argument that physics is not covered by the coding-agent trend line, which is our reason for building domain-specific evaluation rather than reading capability off SWE-bench.

What the report does not give us is any treatment of physics, any discussion of how AI-generated scientific reasoning could be checked outside wet-lab design loops, and any engagement with the corpus evidence that benchmarks can also understate capability through broken grading. Those remain ours to do.
