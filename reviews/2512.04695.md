Citation: Xu, J., Sun, Q., Schwendeman, P., Nielsen, S., Cetin, E., Tang, Y. TRINITY: An Evolved LLM Coordinator. ICLR 2026. arXiv:2512.04695.

## What this paper is about

Getting several different LLMs to work together well usually means either merging their weights (which only works for compatible, open-weight models) or building an expensive scaffold that calls multiple models and stitches their outputs together. This paper proposes a much smaller, cheaper alternative: a coordinator built from a tiny (0.6-billion-parameter) language model plus a linear "head" with under 20,000 trainable parameters, which reads only the internal hidden-state representation of the current conversation (not full generated text) and, turn by turn, picks which of several external LLMs to call next and assigns it one of three fixed roles, Thinker, Worker, or Verifier. Because the coordinator's own reward signal is extremely sparse and expensive to evaluate (each attempt requires actually running the chosen external LLMs), the paper also argues, both theoretically and empirically, that a black-box evolutionary optimization algorithm is a substantially better fit for training this specific kind of tiny, high-dimensional, weakly-coupled coordination policy than standard reinforcement learning.

## Extended summary

TRINITY's coordinator has two learned components layered on top of a frozen Qwen3-0.6B backbone: a lightweight linear head (roughly 10,000 parameters) that maps the hidden state at a chosen token position to logits over which of up to seven pooled LLMs to call next and which of three roles to assign it, and a small set of singular-value scaling parameters applied to a subset of the backbone's own weight matrices (adapting only the singular values from an SVD of selected layers, not the orthogonal bases), intended to sharpen the backbone's internal representations for this specific coordination task without full fine-tuning. Because the coordinator's own generated text is never used, only its hidden state, the authors can read out the coordination decision from an early token position rather than waiting for a full generation, keeping the coordinator itself fast. At each of up to five turns, the selected external LLM is given a role-specific prompt (Thinker: produce a high-level plan or decomposition; Worker: produce concrete problem-solving progress; Verifier: judge whether the accumulated solution is complete and correct, outputting ACCEPT or REVISE), and the conversation continues until a Verifier accepts the current answer or the turn budget is exhausted.

Because training requires actually executing the chosen external LLMs (expensive per-step evaluation) and the reward is a sparse, binary, end-of-episode signal spread across roughly 10,000 weakly-interacting parameters, the authors argue this is a poor fit for standard policy-gradient RL, where noisy global returns overwhelm the weak, localized per-parameter signal. Instead they adopt separable CMA-ES (sep-CMA-ES), a derivative-free evolutionary strategy that maintains only a diagonal (rather than full) covariance matrix over the parameter distribution it samples from. The paper provides a theoretical analysis (summarized in the main text, detailed in an appendix) showing that under a specific combination of conditions relevant here, high dimensionality, a small evaluation budget, and what the authors term "block-ε-separability" (the idea that most of the useful optimization signal is concentrated within small, largely independent parameter blocks rather than requiring the optimizer to model correlations across the whole parameter vector), sep-CMA-ES's expected improvement per iteration should exceed both random search and (implicitly) noisy policy-gradient approaches, with the gap growing as training proceeds. This separability hypothesis is checked in two complementary, empirical ways: extracted coordinator hidden states are shown to be almost perfectly linearly separable by task type (a linear SVM achieves near-100% classification accuracy across four training task categories, and t-SNE visualization shows well-separated clusters), and separately, deliberately constraining the coordination head's architecture to a block-diagonal structure that maximizes independence between the different agent/role decision logits retains a substantial fraction of full performance despite an order-of-magnitude fewer parameters, both offered as evidence that the underlying optimization landscape really does decompose the way the theory assumes.

The specific training and evaluation setup mirrors (and is nearly identical in benchmark and worker-pool choice to) a closely related, concurrently-published Sakana AI paper on a different coordination mechanism (see Field context below): a pool of seven LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude-4-Sonnet, plus four open models) is coordinated across four training/evaluation tasks (MATH500, MMLU, RLPR, LiveCodeBench) and four held-out, never-trained-on tasks (AIME2025, BigCodeBench, MT-Bench, GPQA-Diamond). On the four in-distribution tasks, TRINITY outperforms a battery of existing multi-agent coordination and routing methods (MASRouter, RouterDC, Smoothie, Mixture-of-Agents, random selection) as well as individual models given a matched, expanded inference budget (5x context or 5x self-reflection turns), including a case (LiveCodeBench, 0.61 pass@1 under the paper's default constrained token budget) where several existing routing baselines actually score below random agent selection, illustrating that naive or heuristic coordination is not automatically beneficial. On the four held-out tasks, TRINITY again outperforms every individual constituent model on every task and achieves the highest average score, which the authors highlight as evidence the coordinator has learned something more general than simply memorizing which model is best for which of its four training task types. Relaxing the output-length constraint used elsewhere in the paper (needed originally for fair comparison given hardware limits serving open-weight models) and without any retraining, TRINITY's LLM selection concentrates on the three closed-source frontier models on LiveCodeBench specifically, and the resulting system reaches a new reported best pass@1 of 86.2% on the most recent LiveCodeBench release, exceeding GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude-4-Sonnet's own unconstrained scores; performance also continues improving as the allowed number of coordination turns is increased from 2 to 6.

An ablation suite isolates the contribution of each design choice: removing the singular-value fine-tuning of the backbone, removing the Thinker role specifically, and removing the full tri-role structure (leaving only agent selection) each measurably degrade performance, with the tri-role removal causing especially large drops on the more complex reasoning tasks (MATH500, RLPR); reading from the final (typically near-semantically-empty EOS) token instead of the chosen penultimate-token position causes a severe, double-digit-point collapse specifically on LiveCodeBench; and removing agent selection entirely (fixing all turns to a single agent while retaining only role assignment) substantially undermines performance, confirming that both "who" and "what role" decisions separately matter. A direct comparison against training the identical parametrization with REINFORCE, supervised fine-tuning (on collected demonstration labels), and pure random search finds sep-CMA-ES clearly superior across all four training tasks, with REINFORCE in particular collapsing to a near-uniform, largely ineffective agent-selection policy, consistent with the paper's separability-based theoretical account; supervised fine-tuning achieves competitive single-turn gains but is noted as not scaling well to the multi-turn setting because of the prohibitive cost of generating full multi-turn demonstration labels. A further baseline directly relevant to whether the coordinator's training is doing real work, rather than any capable model being able to fill the orchestrator role, prompts Gemini 2.5 Pro (chosen as the strongest available model for this role) to directly select agents and roles at each turn using the same protocol; this prompted, untrained approach underperforms the trained TRINITY coordinator by a substantial margin (64.14 vs. 70.44 average score across the four in-distribution tasks), which the authors attribute to the prompted LLM's difficulty consistently tracking and correctly weighing the specific strengths and weaknesses of seven distinct agents without the benefit of reward-driven training.

A methodologically notable appendix section (A.6) describes how the specific combination of training/evaluation datasets and the seven-model worker pool were themselves selected: rather than an arbitrary or independently-fixed choice, the authors formalize and solve a joint dataset-agent subset-selection problem that explicitly searches (via exhaustive enumeration over a shortlisted candidate space) for the combination of datasets and models that maximizes a "relative error reduction" objective, explicitly rewarding settings where no single agent dominates across all tasks and specialization can materially lower error, filtering candidate pairs first through a top-5th-percentile performance threshold before this final search.

## Learnings

The paper's most conceptually distinctive and broadly transferable contribution is demonstrating that effective multi-model coordination does not require a large, expensively-trained orchestrator model at all: a frozen 0.6B backbone's hidden states, read at a single token position, combined with a linear head of only about 10,000 trainable parameters, are shown to carry enough task-discriminating signal (via the near-perfect linear separability result) to make competent agent-and-role coordination decisions across seven very different LLMs and four-plus distinct task domains, a specific, quantified existence proof that coordination competence and raw language-generation competence can be decoupled far further than might be assumed.

A second, more methodologically transferable learning is the paper's explicit diagnosis of when a black-box evolutionary strategy should be preferred over standard policy-gradient RL for a learning problem: the specific combination of a sparse terminal reward, high per-step evaluation cost (each training step requires actually running several LLM calls), and a parameter space where most useful signal is concentrated in small, largely independent blocks (verified two independent ways here, via head-architecture ablation and via representation-space separability analysis) is a concrete, checkable diagnostic that other researchers facing a similarly expensive-to-evaluate, weakly-coupled optimization problem could apply before defaulting to RL.

A third, more cautionary and specific learning, echoed by the paper's own "LLM as Coordinator" ablation, is that simply prompting a strong, capable frontier LLM to perform the orchestration task directly is measurably worse than a small, purpose-trained coordinator, because the prompted model struggles to consistently track and correctly weigh the specific, measured strengths and weaknesses of many distinct candidate agents; this is a specific, falsifiable counter-example to the assumption that orchestration is a task any sufficiently capable general-purpose LLM can already perform well out of the box.

## Verification

The paper backs its central "block-ε-separability" hypothesis, which motivates the entire choice of training algorithm, with two independent forms of empirical evidence rather than only a theoretical argument: a direct representation-space analysis (linear SVM classification accuracy and t-SNE clustering on extracted hidden states) and a separate, architecture-level ablation (comparing head designs that vary in how much they enforce independence between decision logits) that shows performance patterns consistent with the same separability structure. The comparison of sep-CMA-ES against REINFORCE, supervised fine-tuning, and random search is conducted under stated comparable budgets rather than only reporting sep-CMA-ES's own results in isolation, and the qualitative agent-selection-distribution plots (Figure 6) provide an independent, visually-checkable corroboration of the claim that sep-CMA-ES converges to a meaningfully differentiated, high-performing agent-selection policy while REINFORCE does not. The "LLM as Coordinator" baseline is a genuine, directly relevant control that isolates whether the value of TRINITY comes from training specifically, rather than from simply having a capable model available to do the coordinating, mirroring an equivalent and equally important control in the closely related Conductor paper from the same research group.

Verification is comparatively thinner in one specific, self-disclosed methodological choice: the training and evaluation task-and-agent pool was not selected arbitrarily or independently of the paper's own hypothesis, but through an explicit search procedure (Appendix A.6) designed specifically to maximize a "relative error reduction" objective that rewards settings where no single agent dominates and specialization materially helps, filtered first through a top-5th-percentile performance threshold. This is described transparently rather than hidden, and the underlying principle (that coordination gains should be measured where genuine complementarity exists, rather than in a setting where a single dominant model already solves everything) is a reasonable design consideration, but it does mean the reported magnitude of TRINITY's improvement over single models and over other coordination baselines is demonstrated specifically on a benchmark suite that was itself selected, in part, for how well it showcases the complementarity that TRINITY's mechanism is built to exploit, rather than on an independently-fixed or held-out selection of tasks and models chosen without reference to this criterion. The paper does separately test genuine zero-shot generalization to four held-out tasks not involved in this selection process, which meaningfully mitigates this concern for the generalization claim specifically, but the in-distribution numbers that anchor the paper's core comparative claims (Figure 3, Table 2) come from this specifically-optimized combination.

## Field context

The paper positions itself against two levels of existing model-combination approaches it names directly: micro-level parameter-space model merging (weight averaging, evolved merging recipes), which it argues is fundamentally limited to open-weight, architecturally-compatible models and therefore structurally excludes the closed-source frontier models that currently define the performance ceiling; and macro-level, data-flow-space approaches operating on model outputs rather than weights, within which it further distinguishes itself from prior multi-agent scaffolding (Mixture-of-Agents, Multi-Agent Debate) and routing methods (Smoothie, RouterDC, MASRouter) by using a lightweight, learned, adaptive tri-role protocol rather than either a fixed hand-designed collaboration topology or a router restricted to picking from a small pre-specified menu of strategies. Within this corpus, this paper's closest and most directly relevant point of comparison is [2512.04388] (The Conductor, reviewed earlier in this batch), a concurrently-published paper from the same Sakana AI research group (sharing four of six authors) that tackles what is essentially the same problem, coordinating a near-identical seven-model pool across a near-identical benchmark suite (MATH500, MMLU, RLPR, LiveCodeBench for training; AIME, BigCodeBench, GPQA-Diamond among the held-out tasks), but via a structurally very different mechanism: the Conductor is a 7B model trained with RL to freely generate entire natural-language workflows (arbitrary subtask text, arbitrary topology) as its output, while TRINITY is a much smaller (0.6B-plus-10K-parameter) system trained with an evolutionary strategy to make discrete selections (which agent, which of three fixed roles) from its hidden state alone, never generating the coordination plan as text at all. Notably, both papers report a new best-ever LiveCodeBench result at almost the same time (83.93% for the Conductor's controlled setting versus 86.2% for TRINITY's unconstrained, closed-model-only setting) without citing or comparing against each other, presumably reflecting concurrent submission; a reader of this corpus encountering only one of the two papers should be aware a nearly-contemporaneous, closely related claim from the same group exists under a different mechanism, and a natural, currently-unaddressed question for future work is a direct head-to-head comparison between the two coordination paradigms (freely-generated natural-language workflows versus a constrained, discrete tri-role protocol) under identical conditions.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central engineering claim, that a very small coordinator can extract substantial value from a pool of much larger and more capable models, is well supported by a genuinely thorough evidentiary base: the separability hypothesis motivating the choice of training algorithm is checked two independent ways rather than assumed, the head-architecture and role-structure ablations isolate specific, individually-necessary design components, the sep-CMA-ES-versus-RL comparison is a real, controlled algorithmic comparison rather than an assumed advantage, and the "LLM as Coordinator" baseline directly and convincingly rules out the possibility that any sufficiently capable model could substitute for the trained coordinator without loss.

The clearest limitation is the self-disclosed dataset-and-agent selection procedure (Appendix A.6), which explicitly searches for the specific combination of training tasks and worker models that maximizes measured relative error reduction and rewards complementarity between agents; while this is a defensible design principle and is reported transparently rather than concealed, it does mean the paper's core in-distribution comparative results are demonstrated on a combination that was itself chosen, in part, because it showcases the kind of agent complementarity TRINITY's mechanism is specifically built to exploit, rather than on an independently-fixed benchmark suite. This concern is meaningfully, though not completely, offset by the separate zero-shot generalization results on four genuinely held-out tasks not involved in this selection process, which still show TRINITY outperforming every individual model; the in-distribution magnitude of improvement, however, should be read with the knowledge that the specific task-and-agent combination was optimized in part for its capacity to demonstrate this kind of gain. A second, more minor limitation is that the paper's single headline state-of-the-art number (86.2% on LiveCodeBench) is achieved in a specifically relaxed, out-of-training-configuration setting (unconstrained output length, coordinator selection narrowing to only the three most expensive closed-source models, without retraining), a materially different and more costly operating regime than the fairly-budgeted, full-seven-model comparisons that anchor the rest of the paper's claims; this is disclosed directly in the text rather than hidden, but a reader focused only on the headline number could mistake it for representative of the paper's main, cost-constrained evaluation setting. A third, smaller point is that this paper and its closely related sibling paper from the same group both claim a new LiveCodeBench record via different mechanisms without engaging with each other, leaving the relative merits of the two approaches (freely-generated natural-language workflows versus TRINITY's constrained discrete tri-role protocol) as an open, currently-unaddressed comparison.

What remains well supported after these caveats: a coordinator with under 20,000 trainable parameters, built on a frozen small backbone's hidden-state representations, can be trained to make effective, generalizing multi-agent coordination decisions across a diverse pool of much larger models, outperforming both individual constituent models under matched inference budgets and a range of existing learned routing and scaffolding methods evaluated on the identical worker pool; this is not attributable merely to having access to a capable model in the orchestrator role, since a strong untrained frontier LLM performing the identical task underperforms the trained coordinator by a clear margin; and the paper's specific claim about the optimization landscape's structure (weak, block-separable parameter coupling favoring a diagonal-covariance evolutionary method over policy-gradient RL) is supported by two independent, mutually corroborating pieces of empirical evidence rather than asserted from theory alone.
