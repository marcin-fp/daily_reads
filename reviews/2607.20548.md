## What this paper is about

Most large language models are still pretrained with AdamW, an optimizer that rescales each individual weight independently and ignores the fact that neural network weights are matrices with row/column structure. Higher-order alternatives like Shampoo, SOAP, and Muon exploit that structure and, in principle, allow larger, more stable training steps, but they have been held back by numerical instability and the difficulty of computing full matrix updates efficiently on a distributed GPU cluster. This NVIDIA systems-and-algorithms paper reports and fixes a specific instability that emerges when SOAP is scaled to large batch sizes, runs a controlled head-to-head comparison of SOAP, Muon, and AdamW at multi-billion-parameter scale on trillions of tokens, and builds a distributed-training implementation that lets these matrix-structured optimizers run efficiently inside the widely-used Megatron-LM framework without breaking apart the weight matrices they need to operate on.

## Extended summary

The paper starts from the observation that batch size is not a free lever: increasing the global batch size lets a training run parallelize across more GPUs and finish faster, but AdamW is known to hit a "critical batch size" beyond which further scaling yields diminishing or negative returns, becoming unstable or token-inefficient. This especially bites Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, since sparse top-k routing means individual experts see only a small fraction of the global batch even as the shared/dense parameters see the full batch, so MoE training specifically stresses the large-batch tolerance of its dense components. To make comparisons across optimizers fair despite their very different parameterizations, the paper adopts an "update-RMS matching" framework (from prior Kimi-Moonshot work) that transfers learning rates by keeping the root-mean-square norm of the actual parameter update consistent across AdamW, SOAP, and Muon, rather than tuning each optimizer's learning rate independently, which the authors argue would confound the comparison.

The paper's first major empirical finding is that Muon scales more gracefully than AdamW as the global batch size grows: at a baseline batch size Muon already shows lower training loss and fewer spikes, and critically, as batch size is scaled up to roughly 100M tokens, AdamW's training degrades while Muon (and separately, SOAP) maintain stability and token efficiency. The authors attribute this to AdamW's element-wise second-moment estimator becoming poorly calibrated when gradient noise decreases at extreme batch sizes, whereas Muon's orthogonalization structurally normalizes update directions independent of coordinate-wise scaling. A batch-size ramp-up schedule (starting small and doubling periodically up to a target size, with the learning rate scaled to track batch size via a square-root rule) is shown to matter in practice: naively jumping straight to a large batch size initially underperforms a smaller one, with the gap closing and eventually reversing as training progresses over a multi-trillion-token horizon, so the ramp-up schedule is necessary to realize large-batch training's eventual advantage without paying its early-training cost. Downstream benchmark evaluation (Table 5, run for a subset of the tested models: a 30B-total/3B-active hybrid Mamba-MoE and a 72B-total/8B-active hybrid Latent-MoE with multi-token prediction) shows Muon matching or beating AdamW on most reported metrics, most consistently in coding (HumanEval, HumanEval+, MBPP) and commonsense-reasoning tasks, though the pattern is not uniform: AdamW wins on MBPP+ for the smaller model and on both Math500 and GSM8K-CoT for the larger hybrid model, and Muon's own results do not scale monotonically with batch size (3x batch Muon underperforms 2x and 1x Muon on several math and reasoning metrics for the Nano-V3 model). A separate architectural finding is that applying Muon's orthogonalization to Mamba's Conv1D filter weights specifically degrades convergence and can cause instability, so the recommended practice is to selectively apply Muon only to dense linear projections and fall back to AdamW for these structurally different parameters.

The paper's second major contribution is diagnosing and fixing a specific "slingshot" instability in SOAP at large batch sizes. The reference SOAP implementation refreshes its Kronecker-factor eigenbasis only periodically (e.g., every 10 steps) and, in doing so, excludes the current step's gradient from that recalculation; this is described as harmless at smaller scale but becomes a "stale preconditioner" problem at large batch sizes, where the loss landscape shifts quickly enough that applying a preconditioner derived from outdated statistics causes oscillating gradient norms followed by loss spikes, damped and recoverable in a 621M-parameter model but catastrophic (training divergence) at 8B parameters. The fix requires two things together, not either alone: recomputing the eigenbasis via QR decomposition every single step, and including the current step's gradient in that recomputation. Combined with a further stabilization technique borrowed from recent work (KL-divergence-regularized Kronecker-factor covariance estimation, which the paper shows reduces the condition number of the factor matrices to the square root of the standard accumulation's condition number), this "KL-SOAP" variant eliminates the observed loss spikes and, in a controlled comparison against Muon on a Qwen3-30B-A3B architecture with matched tensor-parallelism and QKV-fusion settings, maintains a small but consistent edge in cross-entropy loss over most of a training run. The paper explicitly flags that it does not systematically tune the epsilon hyperparameter (which plays a numerically different but practically important stabilizing role in each of the three optimizers), using a single fixed value per optimizer throughout, and names this as an open direction.

The paper's third contribution is a systems-engineering solution to a specific distributed-training constraint: standard sharding schemes (ZeRO, FSDP) split weight tensors element-wise or into arbitrary chunks across GPUs, which is fine for AdamW's element-wise updates but incompatible with SOAP and Muon, which need access to a parameter's full, intact 2D matrix to compute Newton-Schulz orthogonalization or Kronecker-factor preconditioning. The paper's "layer-wise distributed optimizer" for Megatron-LM instead assigns whole parameter matrices (never fragments) to GPUs in a size-balanced, round-robin fashion, has each GPU compute the full optimizer update for its own assigned layers, and hides the resulting parameter-synchronization communication behind computation using bucketed, variable-sized asynchronous all-gather collectives aligned with the model's execution order, avoiding the padding or fragmentation a naive uniform all-gather would require given the resulting uneven per-GPU parameter counts. The paper reports this design retains the full, unapproximated optimizer computations (no shortcuts to accommodate sharding) while balancing memory and hiding communication latency, and both the implementation and a broader open-source optimizer-research library (`Emerging-Optimizers`) are released publicly.

The Conclusion recommends KL-SOAP over Muon in settings where memory footprint is not the binding constraint, since it showed a slight but consistent training-loss edge in the paper's controlled comparison, while noting several open engineering directions: extending the layer-wise distributed optimizer to natively support tensor parallelism and fused-tensor (QKV) splitting for SOAP; making the underlying distributed-training buffer layout itself optimizer-aware rather than optimizer-agnostic; further investigating which specific architectural components (e.g., Mamba's Conv1D filters, multi-head latent attention, low-rank adapters) are geometrically mismatched with full-rank matrix preconditioning and might warrant selectively routing certain parameters back to AdamW; and addressing a numerical-precision concern that arises as models scale, where the increasingly heavy-tailed distribution of small singular values in weight-gradient matrices becomes more susceptible to floating-point noise under BF16-precision Newton-Schulz iterations specifically.

## Learnings

The specific, mechanistically-diagnosed "slingshot" instability in SOAP, caused by the interaction of two individually-plausible-seeming implementation choices (infrequent eigenbasis refresh and excluding the current gradient from that refresh) that is harmless at small scale but catastrophic at large batch size and large model scale, is a concrete and directly transferable lesson for anyone implementing or adapting a Kronecker-factored or preconditioned optimizer: an implementation detail that looks like an innocuous computational shortcut can hide a scale-dependent failure mode that only appears once training moves well beyond the regime in which the shortcut was originally validated, and the specific diagnostic signature here (oscillating gradient norms immediately preceding loss spikes) is a reusable early-warning pattern to watch for.

The finding that Muon's orthogonalization, beneficial for standard dense linear projections, actively degrades convergence and can cause instability when applied to a structurally different parameter type (Mamba's Conv1D filters) within the same hybrid architecture is a specific, generalizable caution against applying a matrix-structured optimizer uniformly across an entire model: different parameter types can have genuinely different appropriate geometric treatments, and a hybrid or selective optimizer-routing strategy (applying the structure-aware method only where its structural assumption actually holds) may be necessary rather than optional as architectures diversify beyond pure dense transformers.

The batch-size ramp-up finding, that a large target batch size trained naively from the start of training initially underperforms a smaller batch size and only surpasses it later in the token horizon, once training has run long enough for the higher-order optimizer's large-batch advantage to compound, is a specific, transferable caution for interpreting any large-batch training comparison: an early-training snapshot comparison could easily and misleadingly favor the smaller-batch run, and a fair evaluation of large-batch training benefits needs to track the full training horizon rather than an early checkpoint.

## Verification

The paper's methodological core, update-RMS matching to transfer learning rates fairly across three optimizers with very different parameterizations, is a specific, disclosed control against the common failure mode in optimizer-comparison papers where one method benefits from more careful or more extensive learning-rate tuning than its baselines; the paper also explicitly reports testing Nesterov momentum and finding no improvement before settling on plain EMA momentum for both SOAP and Muon, a disclosed negative result rather than a silently-omitted hyperparameter search. The SOAP instability fix is verified at two different model scales (621M and 8B parameters) with the failure mode shown to worsen with scale rather than merely being demonstrated once, and the specific two-part fix (per-step QR plus current-gradient inclusion) is shown to be necessary as a combination, since the paper explicitly reports that increasing eigenbasis-update frequency alone, without including the current step's gradient, is insufficient. The Muon-vs-AdamW large-batch comparison and the SOAP-vs-Muon comparison are each run under a controlled setting (matched tensor-parallelism blocking mode, and matched QKV-fusion handling in the latter case) specifically to isolate the algorithmic difference of interest from confounding structural choices.

The main verification gaps are the absence of any reported run-to-run or seed variance anywhere in the paper, despite several of its central comparative claims resting on differences the paper itself characterizes as modest (KL-SOAP's edge over Muon is explicitly described as "slight," and several Muon-vs-AdamW downstream benchmark gaps in Table 5 are within a point or two); without repeated runs, a reader cannot distinguish these specific reported margins from ordinary training-run-to-training-run noise, which is a known source of variability in large-scale pretraining generally. The downstream benchmark comparison in Table 5 is also run for only two of the several architectures tested elsewhere in the paper, and even within that limited comparison the pattern is not uniform in Muon's favor, since AdamW outperforms Muon on MBPP+ for one model and on both Math500 and GSM8K-CoT for the other, and Muon's own scaling across batch-size multipliers is non-monotonic on several metrics; the paper's abstract and narrative framing ("SOAP and Muon consistently outperform AdamW") is somewhat stronger than what this specific, limited-scope downstream table on its own shows, even though the paper's separate and more extensively replicated training-loss curves do support consistent stability and efficiency gains at the loss level. Finally, the epsilon hyperparameter, which the paper's own cited prior work identifies as capable of affecting optimizer stability and cross-scale transfer, is explicitly left untuned and fixed at a single value per optimizer throughout, so the reported comparisons could in principle shift with more careful per-optimizer, per-scale epsilon tuning, a limitation the authors themselves flag directly.

## Field context

The paper positions itself within a rapidly growing lineage of Shampoo-derived optimizers (Eigen-corrected Shampoo, KL-Shampoo, DASH) and Muon variants (Muon+, Muon++, AdaMuon, NorMuon, and roughly a dozen other cited concurrent proposals), arguing its specific contribution is not a new algorithmic variant but a large-scale empirical study plus the systems engineering needed to actually run these matrix-structured methods at frontier scale without approximating away their benefits under standard distributed-training sharding schemes (contrasted directly against concurrent systems work like veScale-FSDP's flexible ragged sharding and Canzona's asynchronous matrix-based optimizer framework, both of which the paper discusses as alternative approaches to the same underlying tensor-parallelism-versus-full-matrix-access tension). Within this corpus, no directly comparable pretraining-optimizer paper is present to cross-reference; the closest thematic neighbor is a position paper arguing for an emerging "mechanics of learning" theoretical program describing training dynamics at an aggregate level, which this paper's own empirical phenomena (AdamW's critical-batch-size degradation, the batch-size-dependent slingshot instability in SOAP, and the differential geometric mismatch of orthogonalization with certain architectural components) would be exactly the kind of concrete, mechanism-level training-dynamics observations such a theoretical program would eventually need to explain, though this paper itself makes no theoretical claims and the connection is not drawn by either paper directly.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central systems and stability contributions are well-supported: the SOAP slingshot instability is diagnosed with a specific causal mechanism (stale preconditioner statistics), demonstrated to worsen with scale rather than asserted, and fixed with a two-part intervention shown to be necessary as a combination rather than either part alone; the update-RMS matching protocol is a genuine, disclosed safeguard against the most common confound in optimizer comparison papers (uneven tuning effort); and the layer-wise distributed optimizer is a concrete, open-sourced systems artifact addressing a real and previously under-addressed constraint (full-matrix access under tensor parallelism) rather than a purely theoretical proposal.

The paper's headline framing that "SOAP and Muon consistently outperform AdamW" is somewhat stronger than the downstream benchmark evidence (Table 5) fully supports on its own: that table covers only two of the several architectures the paper otherwise trains, and within it AdamW wins on specific math and code-quality metrics for both models, while Muon's own performance does not scale monotonically with batch-size multiplier on several reasoning and math metrics; the paper's stronger and more thoroughly replicated evidence for "consistent" superiority is at the level of training loss and stability under scaling batch size, and a reader should distinguish that well-supported claim from the somewhat less consistent downstream-task picture. Separately, the absence of any reported variance across repeated training runs means that several of the paper's more modest reported margins (a "slight" KL-SOAP edge over Muon; single-point-or-two gaps on several downstream metrics) cannot currently be distinguished from ordinary run-to-run training noise using the evidence presented, a limitation common to large-scale pretraining research generally but still worth naming given how much of the paper's comparative narrative rests on specific numeric orderings.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the mechanistic diagnosis and fix for SOAP's large-batch instability is a genuine, reproducible, and scale-validated engineering contribution independent of how the optimizer-comparison numbers ultimately settle; the batch-size ramp-up finding and the Mamba-Conv1D architectural-mismatch finding are each specific, well-isolated observations rather than incidental side notes; and the layer-wise distributed optimizer is a concrete, released systems contribution that removes a genuine practical barrier (full-matrix access under standard tensor-parallel sharding) to using these optimizers at scale at all, regardless of exactly how large their downstream accuracy advantage over AdamW ultimately proves to be under further, more extensively-replicated study.
