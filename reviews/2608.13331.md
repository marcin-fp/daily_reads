Citation: Damon Falck, Samer Sabri, Anja Surina, Thom Foster, Anya Sims, Sam Devlin, Dylan Rogers, Tantum Collins, Kaloyan Aleksiev, Louis Kirsch, and Edward Hughes. Training AI Scientists to Replicate Research. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13331v1

## What this paper is about

Replicating a published result is scientifically important but underspecified — a paper never records every detail of how an experiment was actually run, so replicating it requires the same kind of judgment-under-uncertainty as open-ended research, not just following instructions. The authors build Replica, an automatically generated space of 310 "replicate this redacted figure" tasks drawn from 100 well-known ML and AI-for-science papers (1990-2026), together with an automatically generated per-task grading rubric judged by a coding agent. They use this to reinforcement-learn Faraday, a 27B-parameter model that directs a much larger frontier coding agent (GPT-5.5, ~5T parameters) as a tool, and show it out-replicates both Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 running on their own.

## Extended summary

**Task construction (Replica).** Given a paper, a three-stage vision-language pipeline (Gemini 2.5 Pro) finds every main-text results plot, localizes its bounding box, and irreversibly redacts it from the PDF; each redacted figure becomes one task (caption + gold plot + figure-redacted paper), with 242 training tasks from 100 ML papers and 68 held-out test tasks from AI-for-science papers. Every task is manually inspected and filtered for quality. The agent gets a 60-minute wall-clock budget and a 1/7 MIG slice of an H200 GPU, and is explicitly instructed to produce the most faithful scaled-down replication when full scale is infeasible.

**Reward.** Because replication quality is not verifiable in the way a unit test is, the authors use Claude Opus 4.7 (with the gold plot hidden) to auto-generate a task-specific rubric covering five dimensions: visual match, support for the scientific claim, faithfulness of the underlying experiment to the paper's method, appropriate use of the compute budget, and scientific integrity (not cheating). A Codex GPT-5.5 judge, given the same workspace, tools, and materials the agent had plus the gold plot, scores each dimension continuously in [0,1] and the judge rubric itself is hidden from the agent during training (to discourage rubric-gaming rather than genuine replication).

**Post-training.** Faraday is Qwen3.6-27B, LoRA fine-tuned (rank 128) with a modified GRPO: leave-one-out advantage baseline, DAPO's token-level loss and asymmetric clip-higher, IcePop token-level discrepancy masking, a small KL penalty, and — critically for stability — the mean of three independent judge scores per rollout plus judge-generated turn-level credit-assignment weights that redistribute (not rescale) the advantage across a rollout's turns. Training proceeded through five stages, starting with a cheaper coding-agent tool (GPT-5.4 mini) and a 30-minute horizon, later tail-patching to GPT-5.5 and a 60-minute horizon.

**Judge validation.** A held-out human study (76 rankings, 19 PhD-level ML participants) compared the per-task rubric judge against a constant-prompt baseline judge on tasks selected for maximal judge disagreement: participants sided with the rubric judge on 63% of disputed pairs, more often than chance but not significantly so (p=0.109). The rubric judge showed higher agreement between two independent draws of itself (Kendall τ=0.66) than either two draws of the baseline judge (0.46) or two humans (0.30), and lower noise share at every sample count tested.

**Main results.** Faraday outperforms both Claude and Codex (mean over 8 rollouts) on 73% of in-distribution (ML) tasks and 60% of out-of-distribution (AI-for-science) tasks, an upward shift of the whole score distribution rather than a few outlier wins, with the gap holding across ten research topic categories. A 24-generation automated prompt-optimization run on the Codex baseline fails to close the gap. A separate human study (41 rankings, 11 participants), restricted by design to cases where the judge rated Faraday's margin over both baselines at ≥0.2, found humans preferred Faraday to Claude in 80% and to Codex in 88% of these cases (both p<0.01) — but the authors are explicit that this design cannot support a claim about average human preference across the full task distribution.

**Generalization checks.** On 8 held-out tasks scaled up to a full 8-hour/8-GPU replication budget (no scale-down), Faraday beats Claude on average and on 5/8 tasks, with the rubric judge unvalidated by humans at this scale. A partially-trained Faraday checkpoint that only ever saw the weaker coding-agent tool during training still benefits from a stronger one substituted at evaluation time. An ablation shows turn-level credit assignment is not a marginal refinement: removing it from a late-stage checkpoint causes rapid entropy spike and training collapse within ~50 steps (Appendix B.1), and training without the coding-agent tool at all also collapses, at consistently lower reward, after ~300 steps (Appendix B.2).

**Self-disclosed caveats.** The authors state plainly that they do not treat pretraining-corpus contamination of paper details as invalidating their results, arguing the object of study (the replication process) was never itself recorded or trainable data, and that figure redaction specifically targets decontaminating the agent's context and detecting reverse-engineering/cheating. They separately disclose a conflict of interest (some corpus papers are by the authors or people they know, or from companies whose models they used) and state the identical automated pipeline was applied regardless.

## Learnings

Redacting the target output from the paper itself, rather than supplying a separate held-out benchmark, is a clean way to build a large, scalable, difficulty-graded task space out of existing literature without hand-authoring each task — the pipeline here (find plot → localize → redact → verify by hand) is a reusable recipe for other "reconstruct the missing piece of a real artifact" task spaces.

Turn-level, judge-generated credit assignment that redistributes (rather than rescales) the advantage within a rollout is demonstrated, not just argued, to matter for long-horizon RL stability in a non-verifiable domain: the ablation shows outright training collapse without it, which is a stronger and more specific result than most credit-assignment ablations in this literature (the paper itself notes a concurrent work, Xie et al. 2026, tried a similar idea and reported negative results, making this a genuine point of disagreement worth tracking).

The "coding agent as a tool" (CAT) pattern — training a small model to direct a much larger frontier coding agent rather than to write code itself — is demonstrated to transfer across coding-agent versions without retraining (Appendix A.2): a checkpoint trained only against a weaker tool still gains from a stronger one substituted at evaluation time, suggesting the trained skill is about task decomposition and delegation rather than tool-specific prompting.

## Verification

Unusually thorough for a paper introducing its own LLM-as-judge reward: the authors run two separate pre-registered-selection human studies (judge-vs-human agreement on disputed cases, and agent-preference agreement on high-margin cases), report a null-hypothesis significance test for each, and are explicit about what each study design can and cannot support. The headline judge-validation result is honestly reported as non-significant (63% agreement, p=0.109) rather than rounded up to a positive claim, even though the paper's own section header ("Rubric judge reliably captures human taste") reads more confidently than that specific number supports — the surrounding noise/consistency results (Kendall τ comparisons, judge-noise-share curves) are the stronger part of that verification claim. The main capability claim (Faraday beats Claude/Codex) is verified against two closed frontier models under matched task materials, time, and compute, with mean-over-8-rollouts scores and ±1 SEM bands reported per task. What is not independently verified: the rubric judge's reliability at full-scale (8-hour/8-GPU) task settings or on the "innovation" (counterfactual-claim) task variants — both explicitly flagged by the authors as unvalidated extrapolations of the judge.

## Field context

This work sits in the AI-Scientist / autonomous-research line (The AI Scientist, AI co-scientist, Agent Laboratory, SciAgents) but targets a narrower, more measurable sub-problem — paper replication — which the authors argue admits a better-grounded reward than end-to-end paper generation or open-ended discovery, where prior LLM-judge approaches mirror noisy peer review. Within replication benchmarks specifically, the paper positions itself at the "give the agent only the paper, judge by rubric" end of a construct-validity spectrum that includes benchmarks giving agents a finished reproduction to just score, or a masked reference implementation graded by unit tests; it explicitly builds on and claims to scale up prior per-task-rubric approaches (citing concurrent/prior work by name) rather than being the first to try this.

This corpus's other reviews from this same batch on AutoResearch-style systems — Spark-to-Paper ([2608.11924.md](2608.11924.md)) and the world-model RL training-efficiency paper ([2608.12564.md](2608.12564.md)) — sit at different layers of the same broader problem: Spark-to-Paper automates end-to-end paper writing with a frozen backbone and deterministic gates, the world-model paper speeds up RL training of an AutoResearch policy's reward signal, and this paper is itself an instance of successfully RL-post-training an AutoResearch agent (using a rubric-based LLM judge as the reward, the same category of "learned reward signal" whose bias/noise concerns the world-model paper analyzes for a different reward source). Notably, this paper's own reported instability-without-turn-level-credit finding is a concrete real-world instance of the long-horizon RL instability that the world-model paper's convergence theory is built to characterize in the abstract, though the two papers address different failure sources (reward learned from a simulated environment vs. reward learned from an LLM judge).

## Critical discussion

The central capability claim — Faraday out-replicates Claude and Codex on a majority of tasks in and out of distribution — rests on a comparison that is well-controlled on the axes that matter most (same task materials, time and compute budget, same rubric judge, mean over 8 rollouts, reported per-task with SEM bands), and the negative result of the prompt-optimization control (24 generations of automated prompt search fails to close the gap) is a genuine, non-trivial piece of evidence against the claim that Faraday's advantage is just better prompting.

Three things temper how far the result can be pushed. First, the reward signal and the primary point of comparison are entangled: Codex GPT-5.5 is simultaneously the coding-agent tool Faraday directs, one of the two baselines Faraday is compared against, and the judge that scores every rollout including Faraday's own and Codex's. The paper's human studies partially address this by checking judge-human agreement independently, but the fact that one of the two systems being beaten is also (in a different role) the grader is a same-family-judge concern worth naming explicitly, even though the authors did not design the judge to favor Codex's own outputs and the human studies suggest the judge is not simply favoring itself (`same-family-judge`). Second, the paper's own strongest self-reported validation result for the judge is a non-significant one (63% human-rubric-judge agreement on disputed cases, p=0.109, n=76 rankings from 19 raters) — a real and useful signal, but not the "reliably captures human taste" framing of its section header, and this uncertainty is not propagated into the confidence with which the main capability claim is stated (`missing-uncertainty`). Third, several of the paper's more striking generalization claims explicitly rest on very small samples the authors themselves flag as preliminary: the full-scale (8-hour/8-GPU) generalization result is 8 tasks with one rollout each and an unvalidated judge at that scale, and the "innovation" counterfactual-task result (19/20 preferred) uses a judge "never validated on imagined tasks" per the authors' own words (`narrow-evaluation`).

What remains well supported after these caveats: within the Replica task distribution, under matched conditions, Faraday's rubric-judge score is higher than both frontier baselines across the great majority of tasks and topics, this advantage is not reachable by prompt optimization alone, and turn-level credit assignment is causally necessary for stable training of this kind (demonstrated by an actual collapse, not just an ablation delta). The claims that extend furthest beyond the core result — full-scale generalization, "innovation" beyond replication, and the strength of judge-human alignment — are each honestly flagged by the authors as preliminary rather than settled, which is worth crediting even as it limits how much weight those specific claims can bear.
