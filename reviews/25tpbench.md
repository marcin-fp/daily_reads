Citation: Chung, D. J. H., Gao, Z., Kvasiuk, Y., Li, T., Münchmeyer, M., Rudolph, M., Sala, F., and Tadepalli, S. C. Theoretical physics benchmark (TPBench)—a dataset and study of AI reasoning capabilities in theoretical physics. Machine Learning: Science and Technology 6, 030505 (2025).

## What this paper is about

Can current AI models actually help with real theoretical physics research, rather than just physics homework? TPBench is a new benchmark of 57 original problems, spanning undergraduate exercises up to genuine research-level sub-steps in high-energy theory and cosmology, built specifically to probe this question with problems that cannot simply be looked up online. Instead of relying on another language model to judge whether an answer is correct, the authors require every answer to be a runnable piece of code, so it can be checked automatically and exactly. Testing ten current models on this benchmark, the authors find that the newest "reasoning" models have essentially solved the undergraduate tier and made real progress at the graduate level, but research-level problems remain mostly unsolved, and even partial successes there often come from models recalling a memorized literature answer rather than working through the derivation.

## Extended summary

TPBench problems are drawn from unpublished research notes, private coursework, and a small number of recent publications, and are deliberately constructed or modified so their answers cannot be found by a web search. Problems are organized into five difficulty levels (easy undergraduate, undergraduate, easy graduate, graduate, and research), with 8, 13, 11, 14, and 11 problems respectively, and span cosmology (33.3%), high-energy theory (31.6%), general relativity (7.0%), and other areas including astrophysics, electromagnetism, quantum mechanics, and statistical/classical mechanics (28.1% combined). Research-level (level 5) problems are explicitly scoped as sub-steps that could appear as a nontrivial part of a publication, not as stand-ins for an entire paper. Ten problems (two per level) are released publicly with solutions; roughly 50 are kept private specifically to guard against future training-data leakage, and the authors report generally querying models through APIs rather than chat interfaces for this reason, though they note that some early-project queries did go through a chat UI before this leakage concern was fully addressed.

The central methodological contribution is an automatic, non-LLM verification pipeline: every problem requires the final answer as a Python function with a specified signature, and a separate grading agent extracts the model's code and the expert's code, executes both across multiple parameter combinations (including edge cases such as negative or complex arguments), and compares outputs numerically within a floating-point tolerance. The authors explicitly considered and rejected using an LLM as the equivalence-checker, stating plainly that LLMs are often unable to check mathematical equivalence of expressions reliably. This auto-verification approach constrains the dataset to problems whose final answer is a plain algebraic expression, deliberately excluding final answers that are tensors, derivatives, integrals, or manifolds, since building a general-purpose verifier that correctly handles the many equivalence classes such objects admit (index symmetries, integration-by-parts and up-to-total-derivative equivalences, and so on) remains an open problem; such problems are reserved for future dataset iterations once such verifiers exist. A separate, complementary AI-based holistic grading scheme uses GPT-4o to assign letter grades (A-D) by comparing a model's full solution against the expert solution, similar to how a teaching assistant grades partial credit. Cross-checking the two grading schemes (Table 5) shows a strong overall correlation (82.2% of A-graded solutions pass the numeric auto-verifier, versus 43.6% of B, 6.4% of C, and 1.0% of D), but with real disagreements at the edges, roughly a fifth of A-graded solutions actually fail the numeric check, mostly because the grader could not verify equivalence of an overly complex expression, while some low-graded solutions are numerically correct but insufficiently justified. Cross-checking against limited human grading, the authors find GPT-4o holistic grading works reasonably well at difficulty levels 1-2 but is not reliable at levels 4-5, and they therefore use the auto-verified, answer-only metric as their paper's core reported result rather than the holistic grades.

Evaluating ten models zero-shot with five attempts per problem (Table 3), the reasoning-trained models (o1, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) essentially solve levels 1-2 (95-100%), reach roughly 76-87% at level 3, drop to 34-57% at level 4, and solve only 7-18% of level-5 research problems, with o1 edging out o3-mini specifically at the research level, which the authors attribute tentatively to greater literature knowledge to draw on. Mid-range non-reasoning models (GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3) plateau around easy-graduate difficulty and essentially fail above it; small open-weight models (Llama-3.1-8B/70B, Qwen2.5-7B/72B, QwQ-32B) cannot solve anything above undergraduate level. OpenAI's Deep Research agent, tried on a subset of research problems including one of the public level-5 problems, solved none of them, which the authors use partly as a check that the problems are not simply findable online. A separate experiment (section 3.3) prompted models to write and verify calculation steps with SymPy in a MathChat-inspired setup; this occasionally helped on low-difficulty problems but more often disrupted the reasoning chain and produced worse results on harder ones, with models struggling to identify which specific steps were even checkable with SymPy, a result the authors note is consistent with FrontierMath's independent finding that advanced models rarely make productive use of an offered Python-verification affordance.

The failure-mode analysis (section 4) organizes observed errors into four categories with specific worked examples. Background-knowledge recall is a genuine strength (models correctly infer unstated but standard definitions, as in a cosmological halo-bias problem) but is unreliable for precise technical facts (QwQ misstates Clebsch-Gordan coefficients; GPT-4o confuses which particle-in-a-box eigenstates are even versus odd). Algebraic mistakes are frequent even in simple steps: dropped factors of i, sign errors, incorrect vector-identity application, and incomplete Taylor expansions (in one example, o1 and o3-mini fail to correctly compute a fourth-order expansion term but succeed once explicitly instructed to write and execute SymPy code for that specific step). Logical mistakes include misjudging the validity domain of a technique, illustrated by a Taylor-expansion problem where models expand around the wrong point despite superficially checking for finiteness, missing that the relevant radius of convergence is zero at the boundary they chose. Hallucinations are illustrated by cases where a model invents an ad hoc "rule" (for instance, asserting that terms which multiply to exactly zero should instead be kept separate and combined later) apparently to force its calculation toward an answer it half-recalls from training. On the hardest research-level steps, the authors report that o1, o3-mini, and DeepSeek-R1 correctly identify the relevant physical setup and its connection to known literature (for example, the free Wess-Zumino model's supersymmetry transformations) but then switch from explicit derivation to reciting a memorized final answer with vague phrases such as "after a lengthy calculation," in several cases missing a problem-specific deviation from the standard literature setup (an extra factor in a given Yukawa coupling, a different sign convention) that a genuinely worked derivation would have caught. The paper's own summary of this pattern: current models "resemble a student with superhuman literature knowledge but low intellectual rigor and technical expertise."

The discussion section is candid that there is no sharp boundary between genuine reasoning and memorization for problems that are logically similar to known ones, and notes that problem difficulty was not tuned to a specific target error rate, since o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1 appeared only after most problems were finalized. Future directions discussed include scraping verifiable problems automatically from new arXiv papers (found to be difficult in theoretical physics specifically, since calculations are spread across a paper rather than clearly tagged as in formal mathematics), building auto-verifiers for the excluded classes of mathematical objects, better and more deliberate symbolic-tool integration, uncertainty-aware inference so a model could flag which parts of a derivation it is unsure about, diagrammatic and spatial reasoning (Feynman diagrams, integration contours), using TPBench-style data for supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning (deliberately deferred due to leakage risk), fully open-ended "derive an interesting result" tasks, and a call for the theoretical-physics community to build its own shared, leakage-controlled training and evaluation resources rather than ceding this entirely to industry labs with private data.

## Learnings

Where a genuine, executable notion of answer equivalence exists, it is a stronger and more trustworthy check than an LLM judge, even a strong one: the authors report directly that LLMs are often unable to reliably verify mathematical equivalence of algebraic expressions, which is why they built a Python-execution-based verifier rather than trusting an LLM grader for their headline metric, and why they restrict the dataset to problems whose final answer is simple enough for that executable check to actually work.

Reasoning models attempting a hard, multi-step research problem often do not fail by visibly getting stuck; they fail by silently switching from explicit derivation to reciting a half-remembered literature answer once intermediate steps become complex, sometimes signaled by a compressed, hedge-laden phrase like "after a lengthy calculation," and sometimes missing exactly the problem-specific modification (an extra factor, a different sign convention) that would distinguish the actual problem from the standard textbook or literature case it resembles. This is a concrete, checkable signature: an abrupt transition from explicit symbolic manipulation to an unexplained final formula is itself worth flagging, independent of whether that formula happens to be correct.

Providing a verification tool is not the same as a model using it productively: prompting models to check their own calculation steps with SymPy occasionally helped on easy problems but more often disrupted the reasoning chain on hard ones, with models struggling to identify which specific step was even amenable to symbolic checking. This echoes an independent finding in FrontierMath that advanced models rarely make use of an offered Python-verification affordance even when explicitly encouraged to, suggesting that naive tool-availability is not sufficient and that knowing when and how to invoke a symbolic check is itself a nontrivial capability gap.

## Verification

Verification is this paper's central methodological contribution, not a side note. The primary check is a deterministic, execution-based equivalence test: models must return a Python callable, and a separate non-LLM agent executes both the model's and the expert's functions across multiple parameter combinations, including edge cases, comparing outputs numerically within a stated tolerance. This design is explicitly chosen over an LLM-based equivalence judge, with the authors stating that LLMs are often unreliable at checking whether two algebraic expressions are actually the same. Coverage is intentionally limited: the auto-verifier only covers problems whose final answer is a plain algebraic expression, and the authors are explicit that extending auto-verification to tensors, integrals, and manifold-valued answers, each of which admits multiple genuinely equivalent representations (index symmetries, up-to-total-derivative equivalences, and so on), is unsolved and reserved for future work. A second, complementary check, AI-based holistic letter grading by GPT-4o, is used to assess reasoning quality beyond the final answer, and the paper directly cross-validates this second check against both the auto-verifier and limited human grading (Table 5, section 3.2), finding it correlates reasonably well overall but is specifically unreliable at grad and research difficulty, a finding the authors act on by using the auto-verified metric as their core result rather than the holistic grades. This is a rare case where a paper's verification-of-its-own-verifier step directly shapes which result it treats as authoritative, rather than reporting a judge's output uncritically.

## Field context

TPBench positions itself directly against FrontierMath, sharing its design principles (auto-verifiability, rich per-problem metadata, a difficulty range from high-school to research level) while noting that FrontierMath provides limited public information about its own difficulty distribution and about which specific problems current models have solved, a transparency gap TPBench aims to close by publishing per-difficulty, per-model results. It also distinguishes itself from Humanity's Last Exam, which includes some theoretical-physics problems but is constrained to numerical or multiple-choice answers with no continuum of difficulty and is not specifically designed to probe theoretical-physics reasoning, and from prior narrower work evaluating LLMs on symbolic calculations within a single sub-area of quantum many-body physics. Relative to formal theorem-proving benchmarks (PutnamBench, MiniF2F, FIMO, ProofNet), TPBench targets symbolic derivation with a checkable numeric final answer rather than formalized proof, a format the authors argue is more representative of how theoretical-physics results are actually expressed in the literature. Within this corpus, its central qualitative finding, that models on hard problems shift from derivation to literature recall, sometimes missing a problem-specific deviation from the standard case, is a concrete, mechanistic instance of the broader theme (also visible in this corpus's reviews of 24selfcor and the chain-of-thought faithfulness literature discussed in 2604.27251) that a model's stated reasoning process can diverge from what actually produced its answer.

## Critical discussion

The auto-verification design is a genuine methodological strength, and the paper earns credit for validating rather than merely asserting it: the decision to avoid an LLM equivalence-judge is backed by a direct comparison against a holistic AI grader, which the authors show, through cross-checking against both the numeric verifier and limited human grading, to become unreliable specifically at the graduate and research difficulty levels where it would matter most. This is a candid, self-critical use of a verification-of-the-verifier step, and it directly determines which metric the paper treats as authoritative.

That auto-verifier's coverage is also its most important disclosed limitation: restricting the dataset to problems with a plain algebraic final answer, while a reasonable and explicitly stated scoping decision given that tensor, integral, and manifold equivalence-checking remains unsolved, means the benchmark's "research level" results describe a slice of theoretical-physics difficulty selected in part for verifiability, not the full space of final-answer complexity that appears in the field (a point the domain breakdown itself reflects, with general relativity making up only 7.0% of problems, likely because GR final answers are disproportionately tensorial). The headline research-level numbers (7-18% for the strongest models) rest on only eleven level-5 problems, each attempted five times; the paper's disclosure of per-problem attempt-to-attempt standard deviation in Table 3 is a genuinely good practice most benchmarks skip, but an eleven-problem sample remains a narrow base for the paper's most quoted claim, and the authors are candid that problem difficulty was not tuned against any specific target error rate because frontier models changed mid-project. The paper's most interesting qualitative finding, that models switch from derivation to literature recall on hard steps, is demonstrated through a small number of well-chosen worked examples rather than a systematic count across the full problem set, which the authors present as a qualitative observation rather than a quantified rate; that is an honest scoping of the claim, but it means the reader cannot tell from this paper alone how pervasive versus occasional this specific failure mode is.

What remains well supported: a clear, monotonic difficulty gradient, in which reasoning-trained models now solve nearly all undergraduate problems, most easy-graduate problems, roughly half of graduate problems, and a small minority of research problems, demonstrated with disclosed per-problem variance across ten diverse models and consistent with the broader pattern the paper cites in adjacent mathematics benchmarks (MATH nearly saturated, FrontierMath mostly unsolved). The deterministic, execution-based verification design is a real and validated methodological contribution, not merely an assumption, and the qualitative failure-mode taxonomy, background-knowledge strength, algebraic slips, validity-domain misjudgment, hallucinated ad hoc rules, and literature-recall substituting for derivation on hard steps, is grounded throughout in specific, reproducible worked examples rather than assertion.

## Relevance to us

This paper sits closer to our own mission than almost anything else reviewed in this corpus, and is worth treating as a primary reference rather than an adjacent data point. Its auto-verifier is a working, concrete instance of exactly the deterministic, execution-based check our own PVRE-line work is trying to build and generalize, and its self-imposed scope boundary, restricting to problems whose final answer is a plain algebraic expression because tensor, integral, and manifold equivalence-checking remains unsolved, maps directly onto our own observation that family assignment, not the oracle math itself, is usually the harder bottleneck, and onto our own list of open verification targets around symmetry and exterior-calculus equivalence. Their direct statement that LLMs are unreliable at checking mathematical equivalence is independent, converging evidence for our standing design rule that a verification verdict should come from a deterministic procedure rather than an LLM acting as judge, and their own cross-validation of their holistic AI grader against both the numeric verifier and human grading, finding it specifically unreliable exactly where it matters most (grad and research level), is a template worth imitating in our own verification-of-the-verifier practice, rather than trusting a judge model's output because it is a strong model. The literature-recall-over-derivation failure mode they document, models silently substituting a memorized answer for an actual step-by-step derivation once the problem gets hard, sometimes missing a problem-specific deviation from the standard textbook setup, is a precise, mechanistic version of the "believe the text 1:1" and "confidently wrong on the parts that matter" concerns our own interviewed future users raised, and it gives us a concrete, checkable symptom (a sudden compression from explicit manipulation to a bare final formula, or a hedge phrase like "after a lengthy calculation") rather than an abstract worry to design around. Their negative result on naive SymPy tool-integration, which disrupted reasoning more often than it helped and echoes FrontierMath's independent finding that models rarely make productive use of an offered verification tool, is a concrete caution for our own tool-integrated reasoning work (B23): wiring a computer-algebra system into an agent's loop is not sufficient on its own, and knowing when and how to invoke it may need to be engineered deliberately rather than assumed to emerge from simply offering the tool. Finally, their preference for a small number of well-controlled, leakage-resistant, privately held research-level problems over a larger noisy public set, including preferring API access over chat-UI queries specifically to limit training-data contamination, is directly relevant to how we should curate our own held-out physics evaluation sets if we want them to remain meaningful as models continue to improve.
