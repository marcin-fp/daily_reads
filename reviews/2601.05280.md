Citation: Zenil, H. On Solomonoff Induction in Large Language Models and the Limits of Self-Improving: The Singularity Is Not Near Without Symbolic Model Synthesis. arXiv preprint arXiv:2601.05280v2, 2026.

## What this paper is about

This is a formal position paper, not an empirical one, connecting two AI-safety-adjacent debates. First: are large language models, trained by next-token cross-entropy, secretly implementing Solomonoff induction — a formal, uncomputable ideal of optimal sequence prediction that weights every possible generating hypothesis by how short its description is? Second: does "recursive self-training" (a model generating data that trains its successor) supply the positive-feedback loop that classic "intelligence explosion" arguments (Good, Vinge, Kurzweil) require for a technological Singularity? The paper's answer to both is "not by the training objective alone." It proves, in a simplified but explicit mathematical setting, that pure self-imitation under log-loss has no built-in direction of improvement, that recursive resampling can lose diversity for a specific, identifiable reason, and that cross-entropy's population-level optimum never contains the program-length weighting that defines Solomonoff induction — regardless of how much compute or data is thrown at the same fixed objective. The paper then argues what would be needed instead: a functionally-defined capability it calls "symbolic model synthesis" — constructing candidate generative mechanisms and testing their consequences against something the system does not control.

## Extended summary

The paper opens with the KL-divergence identity for log-loss: if a successor model R is trained by minimizing expected log-loss on samples drawn from a predecessor's distribution Q, the population-level optimum is exactly R = Q. Pure self-imitation is therefore an identity operation, not an improvement direction; any observed gain from a "recursive self-training" pipeline must come from something else added to the loop. The paper then analyzes finite-sample resampling directly: on a finite alphabet, repeatedly replacing a distribution with the empirical frequencies of N_t fresh multinomial draws from it reduces expected Gini-Simpson diversity by a factor of (1 - 1/N_t) at each step, and — under a divergent-sum condition on 1/N_t — drives the process almost surely to a random degenerate vertex (total concentration on one outcome), formalizing one specific, narrow mechanism behind empirically observed "model collapse" in the synthetic-data literature.

A separate model captures external correction: mixing a fixed reference distribution P with the current model Q_t using a per-generation weight α_t. The key result is that the discrepancy from P is not controlled by whether α_t itself stays bounded away from zero, but by the cumulative product β_t of all retention factors (1 - α_s) up to that point; the paper gives explicit schedules where a vanishing grounding fraction (α_t = 1/(t+2)) still guarantees convergence to P, while a different vanishing schedule (α_t = 1/(t+2)²) does not, refuting an overly strong version of the "grounding fraction must never vanish" worry. A further combined condition couples the grounding schedule with the sample-size schedule to give mean-square convergence to P even when the grounding fraction itself vanishes, provided the sample size grows fast enough to keep finite-sampling noise negligible relative to the shrinking correction.

The paper's information-theoretic core distinguishes a global conservation law from an operational accessibility question. The data-processing inequality and Levin's algorithmic-information non-growth results are both accepted as true and unchallenged: no computable procedure can create information about an unknown mechanism M from nothing. But the paper argues these bounds are "the floor of the discussion, not its conclusion," because they say nothing about which finite, resource-bounded procedure will actually make already-present information operationally usable. This is illustrated with a toy example (a one-hot vector encoding an unknown position among n slots; a learner allowed to inspect only the first b coordinates has genuinely less operational access to the underlying value even though the full vector always determines it), formalized as a monotonically increasing "accessible information" quantity as the inspection or computation budget grows, with no violation of the underlying conservation law.

This distinction is then applied directly to the Solomonoff-induction question. Introducing a general curation/reweighting function w(x) applied to an observational distribution P, and a hypothesis-class restriction to a representable family Q_A, the paper shows the population optimum of likelihood training is always a KL-projection onto Q_A of the reweighted distribution P_w — a function of the supplied (possibly curated) training distribution and the model's representational capacity, with no explicit program-length (2^-|p|) weighting term anywhere in it. More compute applied to the same fixed objective only reduces the remaining optimization gap toward this same target; it does not introduce a Solomonoff-style mixture over generating programs. The paper explicitly engages two recent papers making the stronger claim that LLM training does approximate Solomonoff induction (Young and Witbrock, 2024; Wan and Mei, 2025, and its 2026 successor), arguing their central construction — wrapping a trained LLM in arithmetic coding and a prefix-code construction on a universal machine — does define a legitimate but partial contributor to Solomonoff-style program mass, but that this extra coding-construction machinery, not the cross-entropy objective itself, is where the inductive structure actually enters, and that their argument additionally requires an unverified closeness assumption between their constructed surrogate mass and the true universal semimeasure.

The paper then turns to mechanism discovery and causality, distinguishing fitting an observational distribution well from identifying which generative mechanism produced it (citing Pearl): multiple mechanisms can be observationally indistinguishable on the data seen so far and diverge outside it, illustrated with a toy example (the sequence 2, 4, 6, 8 is compatible with "generate successive even numbers," implying 10, 12, ..., but also with infinitely many other programs that agree on the observed prefix and diverge later). The proposed remedy, "symbolic model synthesis," is defined functionally rather than architecturally: any system — neural, symbolic, or hybrid — that can construct, manipulate, and test explicit generative structures (programs, equations, causal models, executable simulators) against evidence or formal checks counts, regardless of implementation. The author's own prior toolkit (the Coding Theorem Method for finite algorithmic-probability estimation via enumerating small programs; the Block Decomposition Method for scaling those estimates to larger objects by decomposition; Algorithmic Information Dynamics for perturbation-based structural analysis) is offered as one concrete route, alongside an explicit catalogue of four distinct failure modes these or any such tools can have (a representation failure when no concise mechanism exists in the chosen language; a budget failure when one exists but is out of reach; an identification failure when multiple mechanisms remain compatible with the evidence; and a search/verification failure when an accessible mechanism is not found or tested well) and a repeated caution that none of these tools is a "causal oracle."

The final substantive section reframes the Singularity debate around a distinction between autonomy (needing less human labor) and endogenisation (a loop whose targets, evaluators, and evidence are all generated from the same model family with no independent correctness signal). Self-play (chess, Go) and formal theorem proving are argued not to be counterexamples to the paper's thesis even when 100% of training trajectories are self-generated, because the game rules or a sound proof checker still supply a genuinely external, non-circular success criterion; the harder, explicitly unresolved case the paper names is "evaluator endogeneity" — broad domains where the evaluator itself (a reward model, an LLM judge, a benchmark) is trained, tunable, or gameable rather than a fixed invariant like a chess win condition. The paper states its own falsifiability condition directly: a purely distributional recursive system achieving sustained, externally-verified improvement across changing domains without any functionally equivalent mechanism-construction step would refute the thesis; conversely, systems that combine explicit mechanism search with independent verification and outperform matched-compute self-training-only baselines would support it. Concrete experimental designs are proposed (holding observations fixed while scaling a CTM machine class and runtime budget and comparing against neural and Bayesian baselines on held-out prediction, extrapolation, and calibration; recursive-training experiments that separately track replacement, accumulation, replay, and correction and report absolute retained real-data counts, not just percentages), though none of these experiments are run in this paper.

The paper's own Discussion and Limitations section is unusually extensive and explicit: the exact mathematical results (self-imitation identity, finite-resampling diversity decay, grounding convergence) are stated as deliberately simplified toy models that do not capture optimizer dynamics, misspecification, architecture changes, or realistic data curation pipelines; the introduced "distributional displacement" quantity is explicitly stated not to measure intelligence, labor, or epistemic value; the algorithmic tools (CTM, BDM, AID) are noted to inherit their own well-known limitations (uncomputability of exact Kolmogorov complexity, BDM's decomposition-dependence, AID's representation- and perturbation-scheme-dependence); and the paper explicitly disclaims deriving any date or probability for AGI, ASI, or the Singularity, or claiming symbolic model synthesis is either uniquely implementable one way or sufficient by itself for general intelligence under any particular definition.

## Learnings

Under an exact log-loss objective, training a successor to reproduce a predecessor's own output distribution has a population-level optimum equal to the predecessor's distribution itself — a fixed point, not an improvement direction. Any actually observed capability gain from a "recursive self-training" pipeline must therefore be attributed to something else present in the pipeline (finite-sample noise, an external data mixture, a curation or filtering step, an evaluator), not to the act of recursive self-training as such; this is a clean, general, and checkable point worth applying whenever a claim attributes capability gains to "the model training on its own outputs" without specifying what non-circular signal entered the loop.

Whether a per-generation "fraction of real/external data" needs to stay bounded away from zero to prevent long-run drift in a recursively-retrained system is the wrong framing; the paper shows explicitly (with worked, contrasting schedules) that what determines convergence is the infinite cumulative product of retention factors across the whole schedule, which can vanish to zero total effect even while, or fail to vanish even though, the instantaneous per-step fraction itself goes to zero. Any argument about how much real data a synthetic-data pipeline "needs" should be stated in terms of this cumulative quantity, not the instantaneous fraction at any single step.

A global information-conservation bound (the data-processing inequality, or an algorithmic non-growth theorem) can be simultaneously true and nearly irrelevant to choosing between two competing finite methods, because both can sit below the same ceiling while differing enormously in how much of that bounded information each can actually make operationally accessible under its own specific resource constraints. This is a reusable move for evaluating any argument of the shape "method X cannot work because of information-theoretic limit Y" — the limit may hold and simply not be the operative bottleneck; the paper's own worked example (a value fully determined by a fixed data object, but only partially accessible to a learner with a bounded inspection budget) makes the distinction concrete rather than purely rhetorical.

## Verification

There is no trained model or benchmark in this paper to verify in the usual sense; what is directly relevant here is the paper's own argument about verification as a general architectural requirement. Its central claim is that a self-improving loop needs an acceptance criterion for candidate changes that is "not reducible to reproducing the current output distribution" — that is, a genuine check external to the generator itself — and it explicitly sorts existing systems by whether they have this property: self-play with fixed game rules, theorem proving against a sound checker, and code optimization scored by execution or a trusted benchmark all qualify, even when nearly all their training data is self-generated, whereas a loop where the same model family supplies proposal, training target, and judgment does not. This generalizes the "is there an oracle" verification question from a single model's answer to the level of an entire self-improvement architecture, which is a useful frame-level extension of the same underlying concern. The paper also states its own falsifiability condition plainly (the paragraph in Section 7 beginning "This formulation also makes the thesis falsifiable") and proposes concrete, checkable experimental designs in Section 8 for testing the thesis empirically — a good practice for a purely argumentative paper to include — though none of these proposed experiments are actually carried out here; the paper is exclusively a mathematical and conceptual argument, with no reported empirical results of its own.

## Field context

The paper engages by name with the specific recent literature making the stronger claim it contests: Young and Witbrock's "Transformers as approximations of Solomonoff induction" (2024) and Wan and Mei's "Large language models as computable approximations to Solomonoff induction" (2025) together with its 2026 ICLR-submission successor "as a computable surrogate to Solomonoff induction," as well as Grau-Moya et al.'s prior work meta-training neural predictors directly on program-generated Universal-Turing-Machine sequences. It positions itself as refining rather than refuting the Wan and Mei construction specifically, by identifying exactly where the extra inductive structure in their argument actually enters (an added coding-construction wrapper, not the cross-entropy objective). On the model-collapse side, it engages the recent synthetic-data literature (Shumailov et al.'s Nature paper on collapse under naive recursive replacement; Dohmatob et al.'s scaling-law framing of collapse; Gerstgrasser et al.'s accumulation-based rebuttal to collapse being inevitable; and several further correction, replay, and token-editing mitigation papers), explicitly arguing these results are not mutually contradictory because they concern different update operators (replacement versus accumulation versus correction versus token editing) — a genuinely clarifying move given how often this body of work gets flattened into a single blanket claim about synthetic data. The algorithmic-information toolkit invoked as the constructive half of the argument (Coding Theorem Method, Block Decomposition Method, Algorithmic Information Dynamics) is drawn from the author's own prior published research program, situating this paper as an argument for how and where those specific tools could plug into a recursive-self-improvement architecture rather than as a neutral literature survey. None of these specific precedents are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first purely formal/philosophical AI-safety-adjacent argument paper in the corpus, distinct in kind from both the empirical ML papers and the pure-physics theory dissertation reviewed earlier.

## Critical discussion

The core mathematical results are correct as stated but are explicitly idealized toy models: multinomial resampling on a finite alphabet with a fixed reference distribution is a substantial simplification of how neural language models are actually trained (gradient descent on a parametric family, with optimizer state, regularization, early stopping, and typically far from the infinite-data population limit the KL-identity argument assumes). The author states this directly in the Discussion ("the equations isolate mechanisms rather than simulate a frontier training pipeline"), which is honest and appropriately scoped, but a reader could still over-read the crisp closed-form results as more directly predictive of real fine-tuning pipelines than the paper itself claims; the results are best read as existence proofs of specific dynamics under stated idealized conditions, not as a quantitative model of any particular production training run.

The central "symbolic model synthesis" proposal is deliberately defined functionally rather than architecturally — the paper states a transformer that "internally learns to construct executable models, calls tools to test them, revises them after failed predictions" would already satisfy the requirement, regardless of what it is called internally. This keeps the thesis general and, as stated, falsifiable, but it also means the paper's constructive proposal is markedly less specific than its critical argument against equating cross-entropy training with Solomonoff induction: almost any successful self-improving system could, after the fact, be redescribed as having satisfied the functional requirement, which makes the positive half of the thesis harder to test in practice than the negative half, even though the paper explicitly tries to specify testable conditions for both.

The paper's treatment of self-play and tool-use as clear non-counterexamples is persuasive for narrow tasks with an unambiguous, externally fixed success criterion, but its own discussion of "evaluator endogeneity" names, without resolving, exactly the harder case most relevant to frontier LLM development: broad, open-ended domains where the evaluator itself (a reward model, an LLM-as-judge, a benchmark suite) is trained, tunable, or gameable rather than a fixed invariant like a chess win condition. Naming this as the central unresolved difficulty rather than claiming to solve it is intellectually honest, but it does mean the paper's cleanest illustrative examples (chess, theorem proving) are easier cases than the RLHF, LLM-judge, and self-critique pipelines its own introduction opens by discussing, and the gap between the two is acknowledged rather than closed.

The concrete tools proposed as the constructive half of the argument (CTM, BDM, AID) are drawn entirely from the author's own prior published work, cited plainly rather than concealed, and the paper explicitly disclaims that these tools are uniquely necessary. This is a normal and legitimate pattern for a position paper advancing a specific research program, but it is worth noting plainly that the tool selection reflects the author's own established direction rather than a neutral survey of candidate implementations of "symbolic model synthesis."

## Relevance to us

This connects to our interest in self-critique and self-training loops (see TAXONOMY.md's note that "independent or stronger critics" matter because "same-model self-critique is weak") at a more general, formally-argued level than we usually operate at. The paper's central point — that a system training on its own generated outputs with no external check converges toward self-consistency rather than toward improvement, and that more compute on the same fixed objective narrows an optimization gap rather than introducing a new inductive principle — is a specific, mathematically-argued reason to expect stagnation or drift in any closed self-training or self-distillation loop we might consider, unless an independently-specified check (a solver, an execution harness, a physical-law consistency test, a held-out oracle) is genuinely part of the loop rather than generated by the same model family. The vanishing-grounding-fraction result is also useful vocabulary for framing how much "real" versus synthetic data a fine-tuning pipeline needs: the paper's own analysis says the relevant question is the cumulative retained influence of grounding across the whole training schedule, not the instantaneous fraction at any one step. The paper's proposed remedy — construct a candidate mechanism, derive testable consequences, check them against something the system does not control — is essentially a general-purpose formalization of the verification-oriented stance already central to our own thinking, stated at the level of recursive-self-improvement architectures broadly rather than physics problem-solving specifically, so it is best read as independent corroboration of a position we likely already hold rather than as introducing a new mechanism we would need to adopt.
