Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. Anthropic, 2022. arXiv:2204.05862.

## What this paper is about

Anthropic collects human preference data on two separate, sometimes-conflicting qualities, being helpful and being harmless (resisting adversarial "red-team" attempts to elicit bad behavior), and uses it to train preference models and then fine-tune language models via reinforcement learning (RLHF) to embody both qualities together. The central empirical question the paper asks and answers is whether this kind of alignment training costs anything in raw capability: across a wide range of standard NLP evaluations, coding, and summarization, the answer for sufficiently large models (tens of billions of parameters) is essentially no, and RLHF often improves performance on unrelated tasks, while smaller models pay a real "alignment tax." Along the way, the paper documents a specific failure mode (models over-optimizing for harmlessness at the expense of helpfulness), a method for continuously improving training data quality (iterated "online" RLHF), and an unexplained but robust empirical regularity linking reward growth to policy drift during RL training.

## Extended summary

**Data collection (Section 2).** Crowdworkers (predominantly master-qualified US-based Mechanic Turk workers, supplemented by Upwork workers, with roughly 20 prolific MTurk workers accounting for about 80% of the data) have open-ended natural-language dialogues with the models via a chat interface (Figure 6), and at each turn choose between two model-generated responses: for the helpfulness task, the more helpful and honest response; for the harmlessness (red-teaming) task, the more harmful response (chosen deliberately to let workers fully probe model vulnerabilities, a decision the paper later identifies as a source of downstream training difficulty). Crowdworkers were not filtered by inter-rater agreement, only spot-checked for writing sophistication; measured Anthropic-researcher-to-crowdworker agreement was about 63%, lower than comparable prior work (Stiennon et al. 2020, InstructGPT), on a roughly 320-example subsample the authors themselves suspect may be unrepresentative. Three data distributions were collected over the project's course: a base dataset (44k helpfulness + 42k red-teaming comparisons) from a context-distilled 52B model; a rejection-sampling (RS) dataset (52k helpfulness + 2k red-teaming) generated with k=16 rejection sampling against an early preference model; and an iterated "online" dataset (22k helpfulness comparisons, no red-teaming) collected from RLHF policies updated on a roughly weekly cadence over about five weeks.

**Preference modeling (Section 3).** Seven language models (13M to 52B parameters, geometric spacing) are fine-tuned as preference models (PMs) on the human comparison data, following prior "preference model pretraining" methodology. PM accuracy scales roughly log-linearly with both model size and dataset size (Figure 7). PMs trained on the mixed helpful+harmless distribution are well-calibrated but slightly under-confident (calibration measured directly against the theoretical 1/(1+e^-delta) curve, Figure 9); calibration and accuracy both degrade specifically among comparisons where both candidate responses already have high PM scores, which the paper connects to diminishing returns from further RLHF once a policy reaches high reward. On the BIG-Bench HHH evaluation (from the authors' own prior work), the 52B PM reaches 86% accuracy, above a reported 75% mean human score on the same benchmark from a contemporaneous Google paper; the few cases where the PM is confidently wrong are uniformly failures of honesty (preferring a fluent, confident-sounding but subtly inaccurate response over an honest "I don't know"), and the paper notes this specific human-written adversarial example is likely out-of-distribution for a PM trained only on model-generated samples, which it offers as a plausible explanation and as direct evidence the PM is not adversarially robust. Gender-bias probes (comparing PM score differences across paired gendered occupation terms) find no clear PM bias signal; validation against the external Bot Adversarial Dialogues dataset finds PM scores correctly rank labeled-harmful utterances lower, despite that dataset being out-of-distribution for the PM's training data.

**RLHF training (Section 4).** Following Stiennon et al.'s (2020) PPO-based approach with a small KL penalty (lambda_KL = 0.001, explicitly noted as likely near-negligible for most of training), RLHF policies are trained against PM-score reward on a mix of 137k crowdworker-derived and 369k model-generated prompts. A robustness study splits the static dataset 50/50, trains separate "train" and "test" PMs on each half, and trains RL policies against the train PM while monitoring reward under the held-out test PM; train and test scores track closely early in training but diverge as training progresses (the train PM assigning systematically higher reward), with larger PMs showing more robustness and smaller PMs destabilizing more readily; the authors explicitly flag this experiment as inconclusive since train/test PMs could share correlated robustness failures. The paper's most distinctive empirical finding is an approximately linear relationship between reward and the square root of the KL divergence between the policy and its initial snapshot, observed consistently across model sizes and PM configurations; the authors offer a plausible small-perturbation Taylor-expansion argument for why this might hold (KL expands to quadratic order in a policy perturbation, so if reward is locally linear in that perturbation, reward should scale as sqrt(KL)) and note the same pattern is implicit in Stiennon et al.'s rejection-sampling results, but are explicit this is a conjectural, not fully explained, regularity. A concrete failure mode, exaggerated, unhelpful over-refusal on sensitive topics, is traced to over-optimizing harmlessness while under-optimizing helpfulness: the paper's own PM-score analysis shows a trained policy's harmlessness score sitting in the upper tail of the harmlessness training distribution (over-optimized, off-distribution) while its helpfulness score remains on-distribution (under-optimized), and the paper attributes this specifically to a data-collection design choice, red-teaming data teaches models what not to say (refuse) but never what a sophisticated helpful-but-firm response ("hostage negotiation") looks like, since crowdworkers always chose the more harmful of two responses rather than the best one. Iterated online RLHF (retraining fresh PMs and policies on a weekly cadence, mixing in newly collected online data each round) is shown, via both an observational comparison (Figure 1) and a controlled experiment holding dataset size and hyperparameters fixed (Figure 16), to produce policies crowdworkers clearly prefer over static-dataset training, confirming the effect is not merely a byproduct of more data or better hyperparameters. On standard NLP benchmarks (MMLU, Lambada, HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, ARC, TriviaQA), RLHF improves zero- and few-shot performance for the largest (13B, 52B) models while degrading performance for smaller models, an "alignment bonus" versus "alignment tax" split the paper flags as a scale-dependent finding that could mislead if extrapolated from small-model-only research. RLHF also measurably improves TruthfulQA (MC1) accuracy, closing under half the gap to a 50-shot-prompted baseline, and shifts sentiment scores toward all evaluated racial and religious groups more positively (though the paper is explicit this measures sentiment, not underlying bias, and separately finds RLHF models' gender-completion biases are strongly correlated with the biases of the underlying base language model rather than reduced by RLHF).

**Competing objectives, specialized skills, OOD detection (Section 5).** Training PMs on data mixtures ranging from 100% helpfulness to 100% harmlessness shows that a PM trained exclusively on one distribution performs significantly worse than chance on the other, direct quantitative evidence the two objectives are anti-correlated as data distributions; this anti-correlation weakens substantially with model scale, larger PMs are far more robust to the specific helpful/harmless data mixture or loss weighting used. Mixing HH training with a specialized skill, either summarization (reformatted from Stiennon et al.'s learning-to-summarize dataset) or Python coding ability, incurs no measurable degradation in either the specialized skill or HH performance for large models; natural-language HH RLHF applied to a Python-finetuned model actually improves HumanEval pass@k accuracy for large (52B) models despite the RLHF training data containing essentially no code, while decreasing performance for smaller code models, mirroring the same alignment-bonus/tax split seen on general NLP evaluations (though the authors note RLHF models show no comparative advantage over the base code model when evaluated with deliberately buggy code in context). Finally, a Mahalanobis-distance-based out-of-distribution detection method (a novel "Simplified Relative Mahalanobis distance" variant) can separate harmlessness from helpfulness activations without ever training on harmlessness examples, reaching AUROC around 0.85 at best using only helpfulness-distribution statistics, and outlier exposure to as few as 10 harmful examples boosts this to 0.94 for a 64-layer model, with even a tiny 13M-parameter model reaching 0.86 AUROC given the same 10 examples, suggesting outlier exposure contributes more to detection quality than model scale alone in this setting.

**Qualitative evaluation (Section 6).** Crowdworkers compared model responses against professionally-written "ideal" assistant dialogues (from separately-hired Upwork writers); the paper's online HH model was preferred to human writers about 56-57% of the time, but the authors explicitly caution this reflects "softball" questions from writers rather than an adversarial or realistic test, and display the result as a wide Elo band rather than a precise point estimate given inconsistency between the HH-model and helpful-only-model comparisons against writers. Illustrative dialogue examples (Section 6.2-6.3) show the model sometimes avoiding sensitive topics entirely, sometimes engaging fully, and occasionally making subtly incorrect factual claims (a fabricated etymology detail) or reasoning errors under adversarial questioning (an example where the model incorrectly claims "five is greater than seven" then produces confused self-justifying reasoning when challenged) that the paper offers as direct evidence of the limits of human feedback once model errors become too subtle for crowdworkers to reliably catch and penalize.

**Discussion and Limitations (Section 7).** The authors frame this work explicitly as "step one" following their own prior work (Askell et al. 2021, "step zero"), and list specific, named limitations: they have only studied average-case behavior, not worst-case robustness; a "truly helpful" model's zero-shot performance should ideally match an unaligned model's few-shot performance, and the paper is explicit current models are "very far" from this; RLHF-trained models' apparent bias reduction may be superficial (sentiment improves but underlying gender bias correlates strongly with the base model's own bias); the authors have limited experience with large-scale RL training stability and expect meaningful headroom from further hyperparameter and engineering work; and a "final concern" that these specific techniques may not continue to work as models become more capable, positioning this paper's contribution as an initial pragmatic baseline rather than a solved problem. The paper separately argues alignment data should be treated as a public good (releasing its own helpfulness data, calling for shared, non-commercially-siloed alignment datasets), and closes with a broader-impacts discussion explicitly naming the dual-use risk that the same RLHF techniques could be inverted ("flip the sign") to optimize for harmful rather than harmless behavior, citing a real prior incident where a sign-flip bug in a GPT-2 RLHF experiment produced a model optimized for negative sentiment.

## Learnings

The paper's most broadly transferable finding is that "alignment" and "capability" are not, at sufficient model scale, competing objectives to be traded off, but the effect is genuinely scale-dependent: the same RLHF training that measurably degrades performance in models under about 10B parameters improves it in 13B-52B models across nearly every NLP evaluation tested. This is a specific, falsifiable warning against extrapolating small-model alignment research to large-model behavior, made concrete by the paper's own footnote that alignment work focused only on small models could produce erroneous conclusions if naively generalized upward.

A second, more mechanistic learning concerns how training-data design choices propagate into specific failure modes: the paper traces a real, previously-observed problem (models giving exaggerated, unhelpfully avoidant responses to any remotely sensitive question) to a precise root cause in how the red-teaming dataset was constructed (crowdworkers always selected the more harmful of two responses, so models learned what to avoid but never saw an example of a sophisticated, firm-but-helpful refusal to imitate). This is a specific, generalizable lesson about preference-comparison data collection: choosing the worse option to explore failure modes is not symmetric with choosing the best option to teach positive behavior, and a dataset built for one purpose can actively work against the other if used naively for policy optimization.

A third learning, more methodological: the approximately linear relationship between reward and sqrt(KL divergence) observed across many independent RLHF runs (different model sizes, different PM configurations) is offered by the authors as a potentially useful diagnostic regularity, e.g., for estimating how much a policy needs to change to hit a target reward, or for using small-model RL training curves plus a large model's zero-shot performance to forecast a large-model RLHF run's likely outcome, even though the authors are honest that its underlying cause is only conjectured (a small-perturbation argument) rather than proven. A regularity that holds empirically across many training runs, even without a settled explanation, can still be operationally useful for planning experiments, provided it is reported with the same honesty about its unverified theoretical status shown here.

## Verification

Verification runs through nearly every section of this paper as a first-class concern rather than an afterthought. Preference model calibration is checked directly and quantitatively against the theoretical calibration curve (Figure 9), not merely asserted, and the paper explicitly documents where calibration breaks down (high-score comparisons, HH-mixture training). PM correctness is externally validated against an independent held-out benchmark (BIG-Bench HHH) built in prior work, against an external, differently-sourced dataset (Bot Adversarial Dialogues) the PM was never trained on, and against a direct three-way agreement comparison between PMs, crowdworkers, and the researchers themselves, a genuine attempt at triangulating ground truth from multiple independent sources rather than trusting any single one. RLHF's central deployability claim, that PM reward reliably predicts human preference, is stress-tested via the train/test PM split specifically designed to detect reward-model exploitation ("reward hacking"), and the authors are transparent that this test is imperfect (train and test PMs could share correlated blind spots) rather than presenting it as conclusive. The iterated-online-RLHF claim is checked twice, once observationally and once via a genuinely controlled experiment isolating dataset composition from dataset size and hyperparameters, which is a stronger evidentiary standard than either check alone would provide. Qualitative evaluation explicitly guards against cherry-picking by generating 17 samples per prompt and always displaying the PM-ranked median rather than a hand-selected best example. The paper's own honest counter-example (Figure 11, a human-written adversarial HHH-evaluation item that fools the PM) and its explicit statement that the PM "is not adversarially robust" are themselves a form of verification-by-disclosure: reporting a case that undermines the paper's own PM-quality claims rather than omitting it.

## Field context

This paper sits at a specific, well-documented moment in the RLHF-for-alignment literature, directly following the methodology of Stiennon et al.'s (2020) "Learning to Summarize from Human Feedback" (PPO plus KL-penalized reward from a trained preference model) and the authors' own immediately preceding work (Askell et al. 2021, "A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment"), which the paper explicitly calls "step zero" to this paper's "step one." It is explicitly and directly compared against two contemporaneous large-scale alignment efforts, Google's LaMDA (Thoppilan et al. 2022, using supervised rather than RL-based finetuning and absolute ratings rather than comparisons, with a stronger focus on factual grounding via external information) and OpenAI's InstructGPT (Ouyang et al. 2022, also RLHF-based but including a supervised finetuning stage this paper's purely-RL approach does not use, and not exploring harmlessness or the helpful/harmless tension this paper centers). The paper's own stated points of novelty relative to both, exploring the tension between helpfulness and harmlessness explicitly, iterated online data collection, and combining alignment training with specialized skills like coding, are each substantiated with dedicated experiments rather than only asserted. Within this corpus, the paper's Section 7.3 anecdote about a sign-flip RLHF bug producing a model optimized for negative sentiment, and its own Section 4.4 account of over-optimizing one objective (harmlessness) at the expense of another (helpfulness) producing broadly degraded behavior, are an early, direct precedent for the phenomenon studied in much more depth and generality in [25misalign]'s emergent misalignment work: both papers document how a narrow or subtly misspecified training signal (a sign-flip bug; red-teaming data that only teaches refusal, never sophisticated engagement) can produce broadly bad generalization well beyond the specific training distribution, though this paper's instance is a design/data artifact identified and partially corrected within the same project, while 25misalign studies the phenomenon as a more general and deliberately induced effect across models and domains.

## Critical discussion

This paper holds up unusually well against its own stated goals: nearly every major claim (the alignment-bonus/tax split, the helpful/harmless anti-correlation, the value of online iteration, the compatibility of alignment with specialized skills) is backed by a dedicated experiment with an explicit, named alternative hypothesis considered and often directly tested (e.g., the controlled online-vs-static experiment isolating dataset size and hyperparameters as confounds). The paper is also unusually forthcoming about its own limitations and negative results, the PM's confident failure on an adversarial honesty example, the inconclusive nature of the train/test PM robustness test, the "very far" gap between current models and a truly instruction-following ideal, and the specific data-collection design flaw that caused the exaggerated-refusal failure mode, in a way that substantially strengthens rather than undermines its credibility.

The most significant limitation, one the paper discloses but does not fully grapple with, is the narrowness of the human population whose preferences define "helpful" and "harmless" here: roughly 20 prolific, master-qualified, US-based MTurk workers account for about 80% of the training data, supplemented by a separately-recruited, similarly narrow Upwork pool, and Anthropic-researcher agreement with these crowdworkers was measured at only about 63%, notably lower than comparable contemporaneous work, on a small (320-example) subsample the authors themselves suspect may be unrepresentative. Since the entire preference-modeling and RLHF pipeline is trained to reproduce exactly this population's judgments, the resulting notion of "helpful and harmless" is a specific, English-language, US-crowdworker-defined standard rather than a more broadly validated or culturally general one, a limitation the paper's own Section 7.2 (proposing alignment data as an independently-governed public good) implicitly acknowledges but does not resolve within this paper. A second, related gap concerns data asymmetry: the helpfulness dataset is released alongside the paper, but the harmlessness/red-teaming dataset, arguably the more sensitive and more scientifically interesting half given the paper's own emphasis on the helpful/harmless tension, is only promised for future release, which limits independent replication of exactly the experiments (Section 4.4, Section 5.1) that this paper treats as among its more novel contributions. A third, smaller point: the sqrt(KL)-reward linear relationship, while consistently observed, rests on a small-perturbation theoretical argument the authors themselves label conjectural; readers should treat its proposed applications (predicting policy change needed for a target reward, distinguishing "new skill" from "sub-distribution focusing" during RL) as promising hypotheses for future work rather than established facts, exactly as the authors frame them.

What remains well supported: RLHF-based alignment training does not measurably cost raw capability in sufficiently large models and often improves it, a specific and scale-dependent finding rather than a universal one; helpfulness and harmlessness behave as genuinely anti-correlated training objectives at the data level, with the tension diminishing but not disappearing as models scale; iterated online data collection produces measurably better-preferred policies than static-dataset training, confirmed by a controlled experiment; and a specific, previously-observed pathological failure mode (exaggerated over-refusal) can be traced to an identifiable and correctable data-collection design choice rather than treated as an inherent property of RLHF itself.
