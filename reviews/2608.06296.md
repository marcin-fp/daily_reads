Citation: Li, Y., Wang, B., Liang, Y., Tian, Y., Fu, D., Vasconcelos, N. On-Policy Self-Distillation without Any Supervision. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06296v2

## What this paper is about

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) lets a single language model improve itself by having a "teacher" version of itself, conditioned on the ground-truth solution, teach a "student" version that only sees the problem, training on the model's own rollouts rather than an external corpus. Despite being called self-distillation, this still needs an outside ingredient: a labeled solution for the teacher to condition on. This paper asks whether that last piece of external supervision can also be removed, and answers yes: instead of a gold solution, the model samples several of its own solution attempts, takes whichever answer wins a majority vote as a stand-in "pseudo-solution," and then distills that consensus-conditioned version of itself into the (smaller number of) attempts that disagreed with the vote, achieving continual self-improvement with no ground-truth labels, external teacher, or environment feedback anywhere in the loop.

## Extended summary

The method, U-OPSD, follows a three-step recipe per training prompt. First, sample G independent rollouts (G=8 in the main experiments, matching prior OPSD's own generation budget) from a frozen copy of the current policy and extract each rollout's final boxed answer. Second, take a majority vote over the valid (parsable) answers to form a pseudo-answer; rollouts agreeing with this vote form the "agreeing" set and the rest form the "disagreeing" set, and a self-consistency score (the fraction of all sampled rollouts, valid and invalid, that agree with the vote) must clear a threshold (tau=0.5, an absolute majority) or the prompt is skipped entirely for that step, contributing no gradient. Third, the longest agreeing rollout is selected as a pseudo-solution and substituted for the usual ground-truth solution in the standard OPSD objective: a "teacher" version of the model, now conditioned on both the problem and this pseudo-solution, has its next-token distribution distilled (via forward KL) into the "student" version that sees only the problem, applied specifically along the prefixes of the disagreeing rollouts, so the correction is targeted exactly at the trajectories that led away from the consensus answer. Because prompts where the model can't reach a confident vote, or where all rollouts already agree, contribute no training signal, the method automatically concentrates training on the model's own "competence frontier": problems it can usually solve consistently but still sometimes gets wrong, without any external difficulty labels or curriculum design.

Experiments use four Qwen3 model configurations (4B and 8B, each in "thinking" and "non-thinking" mode) plus two instruction-tuned models (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and the larger Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 mixture-of-experts model), trained on a 30k-problem subset of OpenThoughts (using only the problem statements, never the solution field) and evaluated on five competition-level math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, HMMT25 at avg@12; MATH500 and AMC23 at avg@4). Baselines trained on the identical prompt set include supervised SFT and GRPO (both using gold answers/solutions), a from-scratch rerun of supervised OPSD (using the same released code and hyperparameters as this paper, for a matched comparison), and three label-free RL baselines (TTRL, RENT, Intuitor) run under the identical rollout budget as U-OPSD.

In non-thinking mode, U-OPSD improves over the base model by 8.5% (Qwen3-4B) and 10.7% (Qwen3-8B) on the five-benchmark average, and outperforms every supervised baseline including gold-label OPSD (by 3.2% and 2.3% respectively) despite using zero ground-truth solutions; the label-free RL baselines under the same rollout budget improve over the base model by at most 1.5%, which the paper reads as evidence that consensus is a substantially more effective conditioning signal for dense token-level distillation than the same consensus reduced to a scalar reward for policy-gradient RL. In thinking mode, the picture is more modest: U-OPSD improves over the base model by only 1.9-2.2%, and roughly ties supervised OPSD (ahead by 0.9% at 4B, essentially level at 8B), which the paper attributes to thinking-mode base models already starting from much higher accuracy (74.9% and 76.1% average) with correspondingly less headroom, combined with thinking-mode rollouts being much longer, so a fixed 150-step training budget covers far fewer completed votes per unit of compute spent. On the two instruction-tuned models, U-OPSD again beats gold-label OPSD on average (by 1.1% and 1.7%), including on the substantially larger 30B-A3B mixture-of-experts model, without any model-specific hyperparameter retuning, offered as evidence the recipe transfers across scale without bespoke adjustment.

An extensive ablation suite probes the method's specific design choices. A diagnostic on 64 training prompts finds 96.3% of rollouts yield a parsable answer, 94.0% of prompts clear the self-consistency threshold, and of those, 86.7% of the resulting pseudo-labels happen to match the actual gold answer (checked only for analysis, never used in training), with fewer than 10% of valid rollouts disagreeing with their prompt's vote, meaning the distillation signal is concentrated on a small, targeted subset of trajectories. Sweeping the self-consistency threshold tau finds a monotone relationship where looser thresholds (more prompts admitted) perform better within the tested range, with the paper's own chosen default (0.5) not actually being the best setting in this sweep. Sweeping the number of rollouts per prompt G finds diminishing and non-monotonic returns (G=4 and G=8 perform similarly; G=12 gives a further gain; G=16 gives back roughly half of that gain), with G=8 retained mainly to keep the comparison cost-matched against prior OPSD's own budget rather than because it is the empirically best choice. Freezing the teacher at the initial policy (matching prior OPSD's setting, used for the main comparison) is shown to be outperformed by letting the teacher track the student via an exponential moving average, with the best decay value gaining several points over the frozen default. A separate ablation crossing which agreeing rollout the teacher conditions on against which disagreeing rollout gets distilled finds both axes favor the longest rollout, but most reasonable combinations land within about 2 points of the best; critically, stripping the teacher's reference down to just the bare pseudo-label answer (rather than the full agreeing reasoning trace) costs 10-16 points and drops below the base model's own accuracy entirely, showing the teacher must condition on a full solution trace, not merely a final answer, for the distillation to help rather than hurt. On the choice of divergence computation, full-vocabulary logit distillation clearly beats a sampled-token-only variant under pseudo-label supervision (by a wider margin than was previously reported under gold supervision), while restricting the teacher's distribution to its top-100 largest entries performs comparably to the full vocabulary at reduced compute cost. On the choice of divergence metric, forward KL (teacher-to-student) is confirmed as clearly the best choice; using the symmetric Jensen-Shannon divergence performs no better than the untrained base model, and reverse KL causes training to diverge outright, with the paper documenting the specific failure mode in detail: generation lengths explode from an average of 2.7k characters at step 25 to the full 99k-token ceiling by step 125, with the fraction of rollouts yielding a parsable answer collapsing from 99% to 33%, diagnosed on inspection as a loss of proper termination (repeated phrases or bracket nesting running out the token budget) rather than a loss of coherent reasoning per se.

The paper's own stated limitations are direct and specific. The method's scope is confined to one model family (Qwen3, 4B/8B) and one domain (competition mathematics with automatically checkable, canonicalizable final answers), and the majority-vote mechanism itself requires an extractable answer, so extending it to open-ended generation would need a softer notion of consensus than exact-match voting. The size of the advantage over supervised OPSD is explicitly regime-dependent: large in non-thinking mode, reduced to a near-tie in thinking mode, and the paper is careful to frame this as consensus being "a stronger replacement for gold solutions" specifically in the regime it measured, not a uniform improvement. The method's quality is bounded by base-model competence, since majority voting can only ever reproduce whichever answer the model already favors most often; the paper's own probe found 13.3% of in-domain pseudo-labels were wrong relative to the actual gold answer, and it explicitly states it did not test training dynamics under deliberately corrupted or adversarial voting patterns. Most notably, the paper explicitly discloses that "seed-replicated error bars for every training setup are pending and will be added in a revision," meaning the version reviewed here reports point estimates (best score across five evaluation samples per benchmark, but a single training run per configuration) without across-seed training variance for any of its main comparisons.

## Learnings

The core mechanism, using agreement among a model's own independently-sampled rollouts as an internally-generated stand-in for an externally-supplied reference solution, then distilling the resulting privileged-context teacher specifically into the disagreeing (minority) rollouts rather than into all rollouts uniformly, is a concrete, reusable pattern for building supervision out of nothing but a model's own generation diversity in any domain with a checkable-but-not-necessarily-labeled final answer, distinct from simply using majority vote as a scalar reward signal (as label-free RL methods do) or as a pseudo-label to imitate directly (as standard self-training does).

The specific, well-isolated finding that stripping the teacher's conditioning context down to the bare pseudo-label answer (rather than the full reasoning trace that produced it) causes the method to perform worse than the untrained base model is a transferable design lesson for any self-distillation or privileged-context method: a "teacher" that merely knows the correct final answer without a coherent derivation path apparently teaches the student to shortcut toward the answer rather than to reproduce the reasoning that makes the answer trustworthy, so the privileged context's value lies specifically in the trace, not the label.

The detailed, mechanistically-diagnosed failure mode under reverse-KL distillation (a gradual, one-directional collapse into non-terminating repetition as measured directly through generation length and parse-success-rate trajectories, rather than a vague "training became unstable") is a specific, reusable diagnostic pattern for anyone debugging divergence-based distillation objectives: tracking generation length and answer-parseability over training steps can distinguish a genuine loss of coherent reasoning from a narrower loss of termination behavior, which point to different underlying causes and different fixes.

## Verification

The paper's ablation program is genuinely extensive and covers most of the method's actual design surface: the self-consistency threshold, the number of rollouts per prompt, the teacher-update strategy, which specific rollout serves as the teacher's reference versus the distillation target, the divergence computation strategy (full-vocabulary versus sampled-token versus top-k truncation), and the choice of divergence metric are all swept individually with the resulting numbers reported rather than asserted, including at least one negative result reported in mechanistic detail (the reverse-KL training collapse). The main comparison against supervised OPSD uses the authors' own rerun of OPSD's released code under matched conditions (same prompts, same rollout budget where applicable) rather than citing OPSD's previously published numbers directly, which is a more rigorous basis for the head-to-head comparison than comparing against numbers produced under different infrastructure or hyperparameters. The paper is also candid that its own default hyperparameter choices (tau=0.5, G=8) are not the best-performing settings found in its own sweeps, explaining the discrepancy as a deliberate choice to keep the main comparison cost-matched against prior work rather than to showcase the method's best possible result, which is a disclosure that argues against cherry-picking.

The clearest and most directly self-disclosed verification gap is the explicit statement that seed-replicated error bars for every training setup are pending and not yet included in this version, meaning every reported number in Tables 1-5 (including some fairly close margins, such as the 8B thinking-mode tie with OPSD at 77.99 vs. 77.97) reflects a single training run per configuration rather than an estimate with quantified across-seed variance; this matters more than usual here precisely because several of the paper's headline comparisons (particularly in thinking mode) are numerically close. A second gap, also self-disclosed, is that the method's robustness to a genuinely unreliable or adversarially-corrupted majority vote is untested: the paper reports the empirical wrong-pseudo-label rate observed in its own runs (13.3%) but does not report what happens to training stability or final accuracy if that rate were deliberately pushed higher, leaving open how much headroom the method has before consensus-based supervision degrades rather than merely underperforms gold supervision.

## Field context

The paper positions itself directly in the on-policy self-distillation lineage it traces from OPD (which needs a separate, typically stronger external teacher model) through OPSD (which removes the separate teacher but still needs a ground-truth solution for the same model's teacher-mode conditioning), explicitly framing its own contribution as removing the last remaining external dependency, and distinguishes itself from a parallel body of self-rewarding RL work (confidence-based or self-consistency-based scalar rewards for policy-gradient optimization, represented here by the TTRL, RENT, and Intuitor baselines) by arguing that consensus is more effective as rich, dense conditioning context for token-level distillation than as a compressed scalar reward, a claim its own head-to-head comparison under matched rollout budgets directly supports. Within this corpus, the closest connection is [reviews/2606.18195.md](2606.18195.md) ("Learning from the Self-future," on-policy self-distillation for diffusion language models), reviewed earlier: that paper pursues the same underlying goal, removing the externally-supplied reference solution from on-policy self-distillation, but for a structurally different model class (diffusion language models, which generate by iteratively unmasking tokens rather than strictly left-to-right) and via a different mechanism (revealing the student's own already-generated final answer as a suffix to construct the privileged teacher context, rather than this paper's majority-vote-across-independent-rollouts approach); read together, the two papers show that the specific bottleneck this batch of work has converged on, that on-policy self-distillation's last external dependency is the privileged solution the teacher conditions on, admits multiple independently-developed, architecture-specific solutions (suffix-revelation for diffusion models here, majority-vote consensus for autoregressive models in this paper) rather than a single universal fix.

## Critical discussion

The paper's ablation coverage and its use of a matched, self-rerun OPSD baseline rather than borrowed published numbers are genuine methodological strengths, and its willingness to report a training-collapse failure mode (reverse KL) in specific, diagnostic detail, along with an explicit admission that its own default hyperparameters are not the empirically best ones found in its own sweeps, both count as unusually candid reporting for a method paper introducing a new technique.

Two limitations are worth weighing directly, both already disclosed by the authors themselves. First, and most significantly, every reported number rests on a single training run per configuration, with seed-replicated variance explicitly stated as "pending" for a future revision; several of the paper's central comparisons, especially the thinking-mode results where U-OPSD and gold-label OPSD are separated by margins as small as 0.02 points, cannot currently be distinguished from run-to-run training noise using the evidence presented, and a reader should treat the thinking-mode "tie" finding as provisional until that variance data is available. Second, the method's core assumption, that majority-vote agreement is a trustworthy stand-in for ground truth, is intrinsically bounded by whatever the base model already tends to believe, and the paper's own 13.3% observed wrong-pseudo-label rate combined with the explicit statement that deliberately corrupted-vote training dynamics were not tested leaves open a plausible failure mode this batch of self-training and self-rewarding literature has flagged elsewhere: a self-reinforcing method could in principle entrench a confident, popular, but incorrect answer pattern over a longer training horizon than the 150 steps tested here, and the paper's short training window (which the paper's own analysis of thinking-mode headroom suggests may already be under-training the harder regime) does not test whether this specific risk would grow with substantially longer training.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the core mechanism, that a full reasoning trace behind a majority-vote answer is necessary for the distillation to help (as shown directly by the label-only ablation collapsing below the base model), and that consensus is a more effective conditioning signal for dense distillation than for scalar-reward RL (as shown by the large gap between U-OPSD and the label-free RL baselines under an identical rollout budget), are both supported by targeted, well-isolated ablations rather than only the aggregate benchmark comparison; and the paper's own honest characterization of where the method's advantage is large (non-thinking mode, models with meaningful headroom) versus where it merely matches supervised methods (thinking mode, models already near a performance ceiling) is a more calibrated and more useful claim than an unqualified "U-OPSD beats gold-label supervision" framing would have been.
