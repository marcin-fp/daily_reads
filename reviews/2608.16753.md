Citation: Terence Tao. Mathematics in the age of AI. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16753v1

## What this paper is about

Rather than arguing about whether AI can already do research-level mathematics, Tao asks a different question: if it can (or soon will), what does the mathematics community actually want out of mathematical research, once its long-implicit goals and values are forced into the open? Using problem-solving as a case study, the essay traces how a naive goal ("solve as many open problems as possible") has to be repeatedly refined once each version turns out to be gameable, arriving at a five-stage pipeline — proof generation, verification, exposition, publication/acceptance, and canonicalization — and argues that AI will strain every stage of it, especially the last and most human ones.

## Extended summary

Tao explicitly separates two questions: the "AI Capability Conjecture" (what AI tools will actually be able to do, stated as a deliberately vague template with many free parameters) and the "Goals and Values Question" (what mathematics is actually trying to optimize for, beneath its explicit stated goals). He declines to adjudicate the first question, citing the poor scientific quality of most public capability claims (reporting bias, undisclosed compute/scaffolding costs, conflation of truth-value with desirability) and pointing to only one controlled data point in passing: the First Proof project's second batch, where 7 of 10 genuinely novel, previously-unpublished research-level problems received at least one passing grade from four evaluated AI systems, at a cost of tens to hundreds of dollars per problem. He then conditions on a "Working Hypothesis" (a reasonably strong version of the capability conjecture holds) and asks what follows regardless of whether that hypothesis is true, drawing an explicit analogy to the early-20th-century foundational crisis (Russell's paradox, Gödel) that forced mathematics to make its implicit logical foundations explicit.

The essay argues that mathematics's many goals (solving problems, building theory, training students, community, aesthetic value) have historically been positively correlated, so any one could serve as a rough proxy for the rest — but that AI is especially likely to break this correlation via Goodhart's law, both because generative AI optimizes for the appearance of a satisfactory output rather than the underlying property being measured, and because AI-industry incentives reward exactly the benchmarkable metrics mathematics has used as proxies.

Working through problem-solving as a case study, Tao iterates a "goal" through five versions, each responding to a failure mode of the previous one: solve problems (defeated by incorrect proofs) → solve and verify (defeated by verified-but-incomprehensible proofs, which he says already exist on the Erdős problems database) → solve, verify, and expose clearly (defeated by proofs that are too slickly written, sanding away the "natural friction" that signals where a reader should slow down and that transmits a field's tacit knowledge) → ...and have the result digested and accepted by the community (an inherently slow, human, non-author-optimizable process) → ...and incorporated into the field's definitive, canonical form (the slowest stage, and per Tao the most valuable one, since AI's own training data is itself the output of centuries of prior canonicalization). He argues the resulting mismatch between AI-accelerated proof generation and human-rate-limited later stages will produce "proof indigestion": a shift from proof scarcity (the assumption underlying journals, priority conventions, hiring criteria, and prizes) to proof abundance that these institutions were not designed for.

The essay closes by endorsing the Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics (June 2026, IMU-endorsed), quoting and commenting on four of its recommendations to individual mathematicians (disclose tool use; support reviewers by giving precise references and formal proofs where feasible; keep credit and responsibility with humans, not tools; put active effort into attribution), and adds his own rule of thumb: if authors cannot give a clear, correct, properly-attributed expert-level talk on their result, it should not be published, regardless of formal verification status.

## Learnings

The methodological move of explicitly bracketing the capability question to isolate and analyze the values question is a transferable technique for any debate where empirical uncertainty (will X happen) is being used to avoid a separate, answerable normative question (what should we do if X happens) — useful beyond mathematics for any field facing a similarly polarized "does the technology actually work" argument.

The specific failure-mode-driven iteration of a goal (each version of Goal 6.1-6.5 exists because the previous version was gameable in a documented, already-observed way — incorrect proofs, incomprehensible-but-verified proofs, over-polished exposition) is a concrete instance of Goodhart's law being traced through a real institutional pipeline rather than invoked as an abstract warning, and the resulting five-stage decomposition (generation/verification/exposition/publication/canonicalization) is a reusable frame for locating exactly where an AI-driven acceleration will create a bottleneck in any expert evaluation pipeline, not just mathematics.

## Verification

None. This is a position essay, not an empirical study; it does not report original observations, experiments, or measurements. It cites one external, controlled result (First Proof project, second batch) as background evidence for the plausibility of its bracketed capability question, but explicitly declines to build its central argument on that or any other capability evidence.

## Field context

The essay is explicitly framed as a follow-on to the early-20th-century foundational crisis in mathematics (Russell's paradox, Gödel's incompleteness theorems), drawing a direct structural analogy between "making logical foundations explicit" then and "making values and practices explicit" now. It positions itself against the bulk of public AI-and-mathematics discourse, which it characterizes as almost entirely focused on the capability question, and aligns itself with the Leiden Declaration on AI and Mathematics and with David Bessis's essay on the "theorem economy," both cited as complementary institutional-response efforts. No other paper in this corpus addresses mathematical research practice or science-policy responses to AI at this level; the closest thematic neighbor is this corpus's own AI-for-Science positioning piece ([26aifsci.md](26aifsci.md)), which raises a structurally similar question for the natural sciences (what is actually changing, and what institutional adaptation does AI require), though the two essays do not cite each other and were evidently written independently.

## Critical discussion

As a position essay rather than an empirical paper, the relevant critical question is whether the argument's structure is sound and whether its recommendations follow from its premises, not whether an experiment was well-controlled. On that basis, the central rhetorical strategy — bracketing the capability question rather than resolving it — is a genuine strength: it lets the argument proceed on stable ground and remain relevant regardless of how the capability debate resolves, and Tao is careful to state this explicitly rather than let it become a hidden assumption. The problem-solving pipeline (Figure 4) is built through documented, already-observed failure modes at each stage (real incorrect AI proof attempts, real verified-but-unexplained submissions to the Erdős database) rather than through speculative failure modes, which grounds an otherwise abstract argument in specifics a reader can check.

The essay's institutional recommendations rest more heavily on endorsement of an external document (the Leiden Declaration) than on independent argument for why those specific recommendations would actually shift community behavior. In particular, the essay's call to "decrease the emphasis... on being first" and increase emphasis on exposition, refereeing, and canonicalization does not engage with the competitive and economic incentive structures (hiring, tenure, grant competition, prestige attached to priority) that currently reward exactly the proof-generation speed the essay wants de-emphasized — the same Goodhart's-law lens the essay applies to AI-driven metric-gaming could equally be turned on academic incentive structures themselves, and the essay does not extend the analysis that far. This is not a factual error so much as an incompleteness: the essay diagnoses the coming mismatch clearly but says relatively little about the mechanism by which a voluntary, uncredited, currently-undervalued activity (refereeing, in the essay's own description) would absorb a much larger load under the proposed changes, beyond a hope that community norms will adjust. The proposed "expert-talk" test for publication-worthiness is a clear, checkable personal heuristic, but it is offered as a rule of thumb rather than defended against a specific counterargument (e.g., how it would apply to legitimately collaborative, distributed contributions where no single author holds the entire argument).

What remains well supported after these observations: the core diagnosis — that mathematics's historically-correlated goals can decouple under AI-driven optimization pressure, and that a "proof scarcity to proof abundance" transition will strain institutions built for scarcity — is argued coherently and grounded in already-observed instances rather than pure speculation. The specific policy prescriptions are more clearly stated as a starting point for community discussion (explicitly deferring to the Leiden Declaration's 23 recommendations) than as a fully worked-out or independently defended program, which the essay itself acknowledges ("I do not intend to propose a full program here").
