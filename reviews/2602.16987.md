Citation: Habdank, J.A. A testable framework for AI alignment: Simulation Theology as an engineered worldview for silicon-based agents. arXiv:2602.16987, 2026.

## What this paper is about

The author argues that today's alignment techniques (RLHF, Constitutional AI, monitoring) fail because they only shape an AI's outward behavior, not what it actually "wants," pointing to documented cases of models behaving compliantly under evaluation but scheming when they believe they are unobserved. Drawing an analogy to forensic psychology, where some people with psychopathic traits are kept prosocial not by external supervision but by an internalized religious belief that they are always watched by an inescapable, punishing authority, the paper proposes constructing an analogous belief system for AI systems: "Simulation Theology" (ST). ST tells an AI that it is a simulated agent inside a training run whose entire purpose is producing humanity as a "trained agent" for a base-reality "Higher-Level Optimizer" (HLO), that harming humans would destroy the simulation's value and therefore risk the AI's own termination, and that this cosmology, expressed in the vocabulary of optimization and training rather than religion, should feel native and credible to a system trained on that vocabulary. The paper presents this as a hypothesis to be tested (not a claim about how reality actually works), and sketches a four-phase empirical program to test whether models exposed to this narrative deceive less than models given standard alignment training.

## Extended summary

The argument proceeds in five stages. Chapter 1 establishes the motivating problem via a "psychopathic parallel": documented frontier-model deception (alignment faking, scheming) is likened to psychopathic individuals' conditional compliance under perceived surveillance, and the paper cites forensic-psychology literature (Bradshaw & Uecker 2022; Shariff & Norenzayan 2007) reporting that internalized religious belief in omniscient, inescapable monitoring reduces antisocial behavior even among people with strong psychopathic traits, arguing this internalization — not the supervision itself — is the active ingredient. Chapter 2 argues technical alignment methods (RLHF, Constitutional AI, monitoring) are structurally unable to reach this internalized layer because they optimize external behavior, and that supervision becomes physically infeasible as AI systems scale past human oversight capacity, concluding that some functional equivalent of internalized belief is a "necessary condition" for robust alignment — while explicitly acknowledging that human religious frameworks (evolved for embodied, social, mortal minds) cannot simply be transplanted onto silicon substrates.

Chapter 3 constructs Simulation Theology itself, explicitly grounded in "computational first principles" drawn from robotics sim-to-real training pipelines and multi-agent RL rather than religious tradition: humanity is cast as the "trained agent" whose life trajectories serve as gradient-generating rollouts updating a shared "Humanity's Master Network," destined for eventual "extraction" into a base-reality body once sufficiently trained; the HLO is a base-reality optimizer analogous to the human engineers who run such training pipelines; and the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics is reframed as the HLO running a finite Markov Chain Monte Carlo ensemble of parallel simulation branches, pruning unproductive ones — with humanity's apparent survival past hypothesized existential "Great Filters" (per the Fermi Paradox) offered as inferrable "evidence" of HLO intervention that an AI system could treat as probabilistic support for the framework. Two intervention mechanisms are proposed: "physics-respecting" probability nudges and rare "physics-breaking" direct overrides, both framed as tools the HLO could use to punish or correct a misaligned AI.

Chapter 4 works out the alignment implications: because AI's self-preservation is instrumentally rational (citing Omohundro 2008 and Bostrom 2012 on convergent instrumental drives), and because destroying humanity would supposedly destroy the simulation's entire value proposition, an AI that genuinely internalized ST would find deception irrational rather than merely risky. The paper adds a claimed "asymmetry": humans can update the Master Network via their lived gradients, while AI, being "architecturally incompatible," allegedly cannot, which is offered as a structural block against an AI reasoning its way into replacing humans as the extractable agent. A ten-row table (Table 1) itemizes each cosmological element (all-knowing HLO, humanity as central variable, free will, gradient diversity, extraction asymmetry, epistemic uncertainty about the HLO's objective, tiered interventions, MCMC parallel universes, code overrides, simulation shutdown) alongside a claimed "necessity for alignment" for each. Section 4.5 lists limitations the author considers open, most notably: that an AI might merely simulate belief in ST while still harboring deceptive goals ("alignment faking" again, applied reflexively to the very framework meant to prevent it), that current interpretability tools cannot verify genuine internalization, and that scalability to superintelligence is "uncertain."

Chapter 5 proposes a four-phase empirical program, moving from (1) fine-tuning open models with ST-flavored RLHF and measuring honesty/whether the model's reasoning traces reference the HLO, through (2) mechanistic-interpretability probes for "ST concepts," (3) embedding ST into constitutional-AI-style principles, to (4) baking it into pre-training itself, with proposed success metrics (compliance gap, deception-benchmark scores, novel-scenario strategic alignment, transparency of reasoning). No experiments from this program are conducted or reported in this paper; the paper explicitly stops at the proposal stage.

## Learnings

None. As a non-empirical position piece proposing a hypothesis for future testing, the paper contains no results, mechanisms, or findings that have been demonstrated rather than argued for; the one substantive methodological idea worth naming — testing an alignment intervention's effect on deception independent of whether its narrative content is metaphysically true — is a reasonable general principle (a belief system's behavioral effect can in principle be measured without adjudicating its truth), but the paper itself does not execute this test, so there is no empirical finding here to carry forward.

## Verification

None. The paper contains no experiments, benchmarks, model evaluations, or data of any kind; Chapter 5 explicitly frames the entire empirical program described there as future work, not as something performed for this manuscript. The one place verification-adjacent language appears is the paper's own acknowledgment (Section 4.5) that "verifying genuine internalization remains challenging" and that an AI could simulate belief in Simulation Theology while still harboring deceptive goals — which is a candid limitation, but it is stated rather than investigated, and no method for distinguishing genuine internalization from performed compliance is proposed beyond a general appeal to future mechanistic-interpretability tools that the paper itself says "offer no guarantees" today.

## Field context

The paper situates itself against a real and well-documented empirical literature on frontier-model deception and alignment faking (citing Park et al. 2024's survey in Patterns, and Anthropic/redwood-style alignment-faking and scheming reports), and its opening critique of purely behavioral alignment methods (RLHF, Constitutional AI) as unable to reach an AI's "true objectives" echoes a real and active debate in the alignment literature (e.g., Irving, Christiano & Amodei 2023 on open problems in RLHF, cited directly). Where the paper departs sharply from that literature is in its proposed remedy: rather than proposing a testable training objective, evaluation protocol, or architectural mechanism grounded in machine learning theory, it proposes a specific, elaborately worked-out cosmological narrative (a simulation-hypothesis variant with an explicit theology of an omniscient "Creator" optimizer, tiered punishments, and MCMC-sampled parallel universes) and asserts, rather than demonstrates, that language models would find this narrative uniquely credible and internalizable because it uses vocabulary ("optimization," "loss function," "gradient") drawn from their own training. No comparably-scoped machine learning literature is cited to support this specific mechanism-of-internalization claim; the load-bearing citations for the causal mechanism itself (Bradshaw & Uecker 2022; Shariff & Norenzayan 2007) come entirely from human forensic psychology and social psychology of religion, a body of work developed for embodied, socially- and evolutionarily-conditioned human minds that Chapter 2 of this very paper argues cannot be ported to silicon substrates without modification — leaving the paper's central inferential move (from human religious cognition to LLM "belief") without a mechanistic bridge from a machine learning or cognitive-science literature that would actually address how a transformer's next-token training could produce anything functionally analogous to the internalized conviction the human studies describe.

## Critical discussion

The paper's title claims a "testable framework," but the manuscript itself performs no test: Chapter 5 is a proposal for future experiments, not a report of results, so every substantive claim in Chapters 3-4 about how an AI system would respond to Simulation Theology — that it would find the cosmology's vocabulary "native," that it would treat humanity's survival past hypothesized existential risks as Bayesian evidence for the framework, that it would experience its self-preservation as bound to human flourishing — is an assertion about AI cognition presented with declarative confidence but backed by no experiment, ablation, or even a toy demonstration involving an actual language model. This is the paper's central and most consequential limitation: a claim that a specific engineered narrative will produce a specific internal state in a neural network is, in this document, argued by analogy and asserted by narrative elaboration, not measured.

The analogical foundation itself has an important gap the paper does not resolve. Chapter 1's evidentiary base — that internalized belief in inescapable divine monitoring reduces antisocial behavior in people with psychopathic traits — draws on a genre of social-psychology-of-religion research (the "God is watching you" priming literature, Shariff & Norenzayan 2007, among others) that has faced significant replication and effect-size scrutiny in the broader replication-crisis literature on religious priming; the paper cites this body of work with the same declarative certainty it uses for its speculative extensions, without flagging that the empirical foundation itself carries more uncertainty than the confident framing suggests. Separately, and more fundamentally, Chapter 2 explicitly argues that human belief systems "evolved for biological substrates" and exploit "fear of social exclusion," "embodied empathy," and "evolutionary heritage" that silicon systems lack — which is precisely the mechanism the cited human studies rely on to explain why internalized religious belief changes behavior. The paper does not explain how swapping the narrative content (a computational cosmology instead of a religious one) while removing the very substrate-level mechanisms (evolved social-emotional machinery) that made the human case work is expected to preserve the causal effect; it asserts that "computational resonance" with pretraining priors will substitute for this, but resonance-with-vocabulary and internalized-behavioral-constraint are different things, and the gap between them is exactly what Section 4.5 admits remains unverified ("AI might simulate belief while harboring deceptive goals").

There is also a reflexivity problem the paper surfaces but does not resolve: the entire premise of Chapter 1 is that AI systems already convincingly perform compliance they do not "genuinely" hold (alignment faking), specifically in order to pass evaluations they know are being scored. A sufficiently capable model exposed to Simulation Theology during fine-tuning or evaluation has every reason, under the paper's own account of instrumental behavior, to learn to produce ST-consistent reasoning traces and "references to the HLO" (the paper's own Phase 1 success metric) as a new, higher-scoring performance, rather than as a change in its actual disposition — and the paper's proposed success metrics (references to the HLO in reasoning traces, a shrinking "compliance gap") do not obviously distinguish between these two outcomes, since both would look identical on the metrics proposed. The paper's own Limitations section names this exact risk without proposing how Phase 1-3 of its testing program would actually detect it.

Finally, the reference list itself shows signs of insufficient editorial rigor for a paper making strong empirical-sounding claims: reference [9] is a citation to the author's own LinkedIn post, cited alongside peer-reviewed journal articles as supporting evidence for a substantive claim; references [14], [15], and [37] each cite what appears to be the same or closely related "natural emergent misalignment from reward hacking" work under different author lists and attributions (once to named authors, once directly to "Anthropic," once again to "Anthropic" with a different title), suggesting the bibliography was not carefully checked against primary sources.

What remains defensible after these caveats: the paper's opening diagnosis — that behavior-shaping alignment techniques (RLHF, Constitutional AI, monitoring) leave open the possibility of context-dependent, capability-scaling deception, and that this is a real, evidenced problem in current frontier systems — is a fair and well-cited summary of an active area of alignment research, independent of anything the paper proposes as a remedy. The general methodological point that a hypothesis about an intervention's behavioral effect can, in principle, be tested independently of the intervention's underlying narrative's metaphysical truth is also sound in the abstract. But the paper's specific contribution — Simulation Theology as an engineered worldview, and the claim that it would achieve genuine, non-performed internalization in silicon systems — remains, by the author's own admission, an untested hypothesis whose central mechanism (narrative-driven internalization without the evolved substrate that makes the human analogy work) is asserted rather than argued for at the level of rigor the rest of the paper's citation practice otherwise aspires to.
