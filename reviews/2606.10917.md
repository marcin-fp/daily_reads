## What this paper is about

Training an LLM agent with reinforcement learning usually means the environment is a fixed, external thing: it hands out tasks and rewards, but it never adapts to what the agent is actually bad at. This paper proposes having a single LLM play both roles at once — agent and environment — so that training can bootstrap itself without a separate environment model or task generator. As the agent, the model predicts what will happen after each of its actions and gets rewarded (or not) for being right about that; as the environment, the same model looks at the agent's failed attempts, diagnoses what kind of mistake it made, and specifically pulls up more training tasks that share that same failure pattern. The result, Role-Agent, is tested on household-task navigation (ALFWorld), simulated online shopping (WebShop), and search-based question answering, and consistently beats the strongest prior RL-training baseline (GiGPO) by a few points.

## Extended summary

Role-Agent has two components layered on top of a GRPO-style RL training loop (specifically built on GiGPO, which introduces state-grouped advantage estimation by hashing and clustering textually-similar states within a batch of rollouts, then computing advantages within each state group in addition to the usual trajectory-level advantage).

World-In-Agent (WIA): at each interaction step, after generating an action, the same policy is prompted to predict the environment's state H steps into the future. The gap between this prediction and the actual future state (measured via Longest Matching Subsequence over the textual state descriptions, following GiGPO's state-similarity convention) becomes a predictive reward in [0,1]. This predictive reward is combined with the task reward multiplicatively rather than additively — accurate prediction preserves/amplifies an action's existing credit, inaccurate prediction discounts it, but a predictive reward alone cannot create credit for an action that didn't earn any task reward. The stated intent is to prevent trajectories that "got lucky" on the outcome reward from being credited for producing merely plausible-looking (but not verified-correct) intermediate predictions. A hyperparameter α balances the resulting state-level advantage against the standard trajectory-level advantage.

Agent-In-World (AIW): for every failed trajectory, the same LLM is prompted (in its environment-feedback role) to identify the failure mode — a categorical label from a fixed per-benchmark taxonomy (e.g., ALFWorld's includes entity_confusion, repetitive_exploration, wrong_receptacle; 11 unique modes are used across ALFWorld training) — plus a natural-language reflection describing the failure and a retrieval query. These (task, failure-mode) pairs accumulate in an offline library; when constructing subsequent training batches, the LLM retrieves tasks sharing similar failure modes to the ones just observed and reweights the training-data distribution toward them, explicitly by underlying error pattern rather than surface task similarity or random failed-task replay.

Experiments use Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B-Instruct backbones (VeRL framework, 8×H20 GPUs) across ALFWorld, WebShop, and seven QA datasets (single-hop: NQ, TriviaQA, PopQA; multi-hop: HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, MuSiQue, Bamboogle), against closed-source models (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro), prompting baselines (ReAct, Reflexion), RL baselines (PPO, RLOO, GRPO, and the direct predecessor GiGPO), and (QA-only) search-specific RL baselines (R1-Instruct, Search-R1, ZeroSearch, StepSearch). On ALFWorld/WebShop (Table 1), Role-Agent achieves the best average success rate at both the 1.5B scale (90.9%/71.9%, vs. GiGPO's 86.7%/65.0%) and 7B scale (93.8%/77.1%, vs. GiGPO's 90.8%/72.8%), with the largest relative gains concentrated in compositionally harder subtasks requiring multi-step memory (Look: +11.0pp; Pick2: +13.6pp at 7B scale). On search-QA (Table 2, Qwen2.5-3B, trained only on NQ and HotpotQA), Role-Agent reaches the best average (45.8% vs. GiGPO's 42.1%), with larger relative gains on out-of-domain multi-hop datasets (2Wiki +8.2pp, MuSiQue +5.2pp) than on in-domain NQ, where Role-Agent actually slightly underperforms GiGPO (40.1 vs. 42.0) — attributed by the authors, without independent verification, to Role-Agent generalizing rather than overfitting to the training distribution.

An ablation (Table 3, 1.5B scale) removes AIW or the predictive reward individually: both hurt performance (AIW removal is the larger drop, -5.0pp on WebShop specifically), but both ablated variants still beat GiGPO on average, which the paper reads as evidence the two components are complementary rather than redundant sources of the same gain. A failure-mode accumulation analysis (Figure 4) shows the total recorded failure count growing quickly early in training (996 at step 15) then saturating more slowly (3,931 at step 150), with repetitive exploration, wrong-target-location, and wrong-receptacle failures dominating — interpreted as evidence AIW captures a genuinely diverse set of weaknesses rather than one dominant error type. A hyperparameter sweep finds α=1.0 (balanced trajectory/state-level advantage weighting) and H=5%·T_max (prediction horizon) as a "Pareto optimum," with performance degrading sharply for larger H (attributed to context-window crowding and speculative long-horizon guessing that risks reward hacking of the predictive-reward term itself). A running-dynamics comparison shows Role-Agent converging to a higher ceiling than GiGPO with substantially reduced train-inference policy mismatch (a lower-variance gradient-estimation benefit). An efficiency breakdown (Figure 6) attributes only ~5.2% extra per-step computation to the additional prediction/reward/retrieval machinery, since state comparisons only involve short state descriptions and the failure-mode library remains small. Appendix Table 7 reports three-run standard deviations for GRPO/GiGPO/Role-Agent specifically on ALFWorld/WebShop, and Appendix B.2 reports that on 200 ALFWorld rollouts, predictive reward correlates with outcome reward at point-biserial r=0.41 (p<0.01) and rises from ~0.60 at initialization to the mid-to-high 0.70s near convergence, offered as evidence the predictive-reward signal tracks genuine improvement in state-prediction quality rather than being noise.

## Learnings

The most transferable design idea is the multiplicative (rather than additive) combination of the predictive/world-model reward with the task reward: by construction, a trajectory that failed the task cannot be rescued by producing plausible-sounding state predictions, since the predictive term only modulates (amplifies or discounts) credit that the task reward already granted. This is a specific, implementable safeguard against a known failure mode of auxiliary process rewards — that an easily-gamed auxiliary signal becomes an independent source of reward hacking — and is more concrete than a general caution to "be careful with auxiliary rewards."

A second learning is the framing of curriculum/data-distribution adaptation around failure mechanism rather than task surface similarity or outcome alone: retrieving replay tasks by shared underlying error pattern (e.g., all tasks that tend to trigger entity confusion between similarly-named objects) rather than by textual similarity to the failed task or simple failed-task replay is a specific, reusable idea for self-curriculum design in agent training, and the paper's own ablation (AIW removal being the single largest degradation) suggests this mechanism-level targeting is doing real work beyond what more generic data reweighting would achieve.

A third, narrower learning from the hyperparameter sensitivity study is the specific failure mode identified for over-long prediction horizons: performance degrades sharply once the prediction horizon H grows past a small fraction of the maximum episode length, attributed to both context-window crowding (predictions displacing action-planning capacity) and a form of reward hacking where long-horizon predictions become speculative rather than grounded — a concrete, mechanism-level caution for anyone adding a self-supervised auxiliary prediction task to an agent's context, independent of the specific benchmark.

## Verification

Verification here operates mostly at the level of showing the paper's core mechanisms do what they claim rather than checking claims against external evidence. The clearest piece of internal verification is the point-biserial correlation (r=0.41, p<0.01) between predictive reward and outcome reward reported in Appendix B.2, plus the reported rise in average predictive-reward value across training — together offered as evidence that the LMS-based state-prediction proxy is tracking something real rather than being an easily-satisfied, uninformative signal. This is a reasonable sanity check, but it falls short of directly validating the proxy metric itself: Longest Matching Subsequence over textual state descriptions is a syntactic overlap measure, and the paper does not report how well LMS-based match/mismatch judgments align with a human or independent-model judgment of whether a predicted state is actually semantically correct (in contrast to, for example, 2606.04075 in this batch, which explicitly validates its own LLM-judge pipeline against independent human annotators). Given that the environment states in these benchmarks are described as "short and templated" (the paper's own justification for using a high 0.9 similarity threshold, inherited unmodified from GiGPO), LMS-based matching may be reasonably reliable here, but this reliability is asserted by design choice rather than independently measured. The ablation study (Table 3) and the appendix's three-run standard deviations (Table 7, though limited to GRPO/GiGPO/Role-Agent rather than the full baseline set in Tables 1-2) are the paper's main tools for arguing the reported gains are not noise, and the reported deviations (roughly ±0.5-1.8 points) are small relative to the reported multi-point gaps over GiGPO specifically, which is the paper's most tightly matched comparison (same backbone, same state-grouping mechanism, same hyperparameters where shared).

## Field context

The paper builds directly on and is positioned as an extension of GiGPO (Feng et al. 2025), from which it inherits the state-grouping/state-level-advantage mechanism wholesale (including GiGPO's 0.9 similarity threshold, kept unmodified "for fair comparison"), and against which every headline comparison in the paper is drawn. Within the broader self-evolving-agents literature it cites (EvolveR's self-distilled principles; MAE's Proposer/Solver/Judge role decomposition; Agentevolver's self-questioning/self-navigation/self-attribution), Role-Agent's explicit differentiator is that prior self-evolving approaches keep their auxiliary roles entirely on the agent side while treating the environment as fixed, whereas Role-Agent extends self-evolution to the environment itself by having the same model also diagnose and reshape the training distribution — a genuinely distinct axis of self-evolution (environment-side rather than only agent-side) relative to the cited comparison set. Within this corpus, Role-Agent's core design tension — using a single model to both generate a signal (here, state predictions and failure diagnoses) and evaluate/consume that same signal for its own training — is a milder, RL-training-time instance of the same-family self-evaluation pattern this batch has repeatedly flagged in inference-time and evaluation-time settings (2605.28655's same-backbone peer-critique in AutoScientists; 2606.04075's shared simulator/judge model), though here the risk is scoped narrowly (the model rates its own future-state predictions and its own failure causes, rather than judging final output quality), and the paper's multiplicative reward-combination design is itself a partial mitigation against the most obvious failure mode (rewarding plausible-but-false self-predictions) that those other papers' designs do not equivalently guard against.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central comparison (Role-Agent vs. GiGPO) is tightly controlled — same backbone, same state-grouping mechanism and threshold, same hyperparameters where shared — which is a real methodological strength, and the reported gains (roughly 4 points average per the abstract) are corroborated by an ablation showing both individual components still beat GiGPO alone and by three-run standard deviations (Table 7) that are small relative to the reported gaps for the ALFWorld/WebShop comparisons specifically. That said, several comparisons carry weaker guarantees than the headline framing might suggest. The search-QA comparison (Table 2) is explicitly flagged by the authors themselves as not fully controlled — "search-agent baselines differ in training and evaluation protocols" — so the reported +3.7-point average gain over GiGPO there should be read with the same caution the authors themselves apply, treating ALFWorld/WebShop as the more direct evidence. Most of the paper's result tables (Table 1, Table 2, and the closed-source/prompting rows entirely) report no variance or confidence estimate at all; only Table 7 in the appendix reports three-run standard deviations, and only for three of the roughly ten methods compared in the main tables (GRPO, GiGPO, Role-Agent), leaving the significance of gaps against PPO, RLOO, ReAct, Reflexion, and the closed-source models unquantified.

The paper's one explicit negative result — Role-Agent underperforming GiGPO on NQ specifically (40.1 vs. 42.0) — is explained by the authors as reflecting "stronger generalization capabilities rather than overfitting to the training set," a plausible but untested post-hoc interpretation offered without an independent diagnostic (e.g., directly measuring in-domain vs. out-of-domain performance gaps for both methods, or an ablation isolating which component of Role-Agent specifically trades NQ performance for cross-domain transfer). A single negative result explained by an untested hypothesis is not by itself a serious flaw, but it is worth flagging as an unverified claim rather than a demonstrated mechanism. Separately, the core WIA verification metric (LMS-based textual state matching) is validated only via its correlation with outcome reward, not via any check that it tracks genuinely correct future-state understanding as opposed to superficial textual overlap — a validation gap that matters more here than it might elsewhere, since the whole justification for the predictive-reward mechanism is that it teaches "environment-aware reasoning" rather than a narrower textual-matching skill, and the paper's own Limitations section separately flags that the inherited similarity threshold "limits cross-task generalization," an acknowledgment that the underlying textual-matching mechanism has known scope limits the paper does not fully characterize.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the tightly-matched comparison against GiGPO on ALFWorld and WebShop, backed by ablations showing both components contribute independently and by reported run-to-run stability, is a credible demonstration that the dual-role co-evolution mechanism provides real, non-trivial gains over the strongest directly-comparable prior method in this specific setting; the efficiency analysis quantifying the mechanism's overhead (~5.2% extra compute) as small relative to reported gains is a concrete, checkable claim rather than an assertion; and the qualitative failure-mode case study (Figure 5) provides an interpretable, mechanistically legible illustration of what the AIW retrieval mechanism is actually doing, beyond the aggregate benchmark numbers.
