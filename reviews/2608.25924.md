## What this paper is about

Language models got broad, unexpected capabilities from a simple recipe (next-token prediction at web scale). This white paper asks the analogous question for vision: could exposing a model to enough visual experience, under the right objective, produce comparably broad "visual general intelligence" (VGI) — and if so, what would that objective, that experience, and that evaluation actually look like? Rather than answering with one theory, ten contributors from academia and industry (assembled around a CVPR 2026 workshop) each stake out their own position, and the paper's editors synthesize where these views agree, disagree, and leave open questions.

## Extended summary

Ten numbered sub-essays (Section 2.1-2.10) each argue for a different candidate mechanism or emphasis:

- **Geirhos**: generative video models, trained at scale on web video with a next-frame objective, will become general-purpose visual foundation models, because generation is a harder, less shortcut-prone objective than discriminative classification; he cites his own prior work showing Veo 3 performs a wide range of visual tasks (edge detection, segmentation, maze-solving) zero-shot via image-to-video generation.
- **Raghunathan**: creativity — measured as coherent, structurally diverse, and original (not just fluent) outputs — should be a first-class evaluation target, illustrated by controlled algorithmic-creativity tasks where multi-token/diffusion objectives outperformed next-token training on solution diversity.
- **Asano**: VGI requires continual, label-free, lifelong learning from an unfolding visual stream, not a frozen model retrained periodically on ever-larger static datasets; he sketches a multi-module "artificial visual brain" (perception, world model, memory, task interface) rather than one homogeneous network.
- **Ramanan et al.**: intelligence will be multimodal (vision plus touch/audio/proprioception, motivated by robotics examples where blind, proprioception-only policies already work well), generative (subscribing to "learn to generate in order to recognize"), and efficiency-constrained (citing OpenAI's discontinuation of Sora as evidence that current generative-video costs are unsustainable outside a few frontier labs); stated explicitly as speculative "bets."
- **Fouhey**: from direct experience deploying vision models for scientific discovery (solar physics, evolutionary ecology), argues the field underestimates how much of real deployment is validation without conventional ground truth, handling instrument systematics, and integrating domain knowledge — problems current CVPR practice and incentive structures are not well set up to reward.
- **Davison**: reframes visual SLAM as "Spatial AI," arguing the key open problem is the computational structure (what is stored/computed where, and how it maps onto increasingly parallel, sparse, locality-respecting hardware) needed for persistent, efficient, real-time world representations.
- **Du**: visual intelligence is embodied intelligence — perception, generative world models, action, active exploration (citing classical evidence from Held and Hein's kitten experiment), and continual post-deployment adaptation must form one closed loop, illustrated with his group's own work on object-centric state estimation, video-conditioned robot planning, and self-improving world models.
- **Shangzhe Wu & Jiajun Wu**: photorealistic generation is not evidence of physical understanding — a model that renders a convincing falling cup has shown it knows what falling cups look like, not that it represents mass or friction; they advocate recovering explicit compositional structure (entities, intrinsics, extrinsics, relations, dynamics) that can be edited, simulated, and verified, including via LLM coding agents that write and test executable object/scene programs.
- **Liu**: argues vision lacks language's ready-made symbolic hierarchy (words/phrases/sentences), making visual learning harder to compress and possibly requiring orders of magnitude more effective compute/data before useful abstractions emerge; proposes "vision-native" systems that actively decide when and where to look and at what resolution, rather than passively ingesting a fixed input.
- **Kataoka et al.**: proposes that VGI may emerge from the convergence of three objectives — sequential prediction, open-ended generation, and reconstruction — as visual analogues of anticipation, imagination, and hidden-structure inference respectively.

**Synthesis (Section 3).** The editors group these into overlapping themes (scaling/generative modeling; creativity/imagination; continual learning; persistent spatial representations; embodiment/action; multimodality/efficiency; scientific discovery; compositional structure; vision-native processing) and explicitly surface the central tension in the collection: several contributors treat large-scale generative modeling as the likely path to VGI, while Shangzhe Wu & Jiajun Wu explicitly counter that photorealistic generation alone does not demonstrate the physical understanding that would make such a claim meaningful. The paper does not resolve this tension, instead presenting it as one of the field's open questions. It also argues that VGI evaluation should extend beyond static task accuracy to transfer, continual adaptation, active information-seeking, persistent world knowledge, creativity, and physical consistency, while explicitly stating that no single benchmark is likely to capture all of these.

**Conclusion.** The paper declines to endorse one path, stating explicitly that this plurality of views is itself one of its central conclusions, and frames the more immediate open question as whether intelligence can be observed to emerge from visual experience at all, prior to asking whether such intelligence would generalize toward AGI.

## Learnings

The paper's own internal disagreement — Geirhos's generation-as-sufficient-evidence position versus Shangzhe Wu & Jiajun Wu's explicit rebuttal that visual plausibility and physical/causal understanding are dissociable — is a genuinely useful, concretely stated fault line for anyone evaluating claims about "emergent world understanding" from generative video or image models: a model can be judged by whether its outputs look right (a perceptual-fidelity criterion) or by whether its internal state supports intervention, editing, and simulation (a structural/causal criterion), and these can diverge, as illustrated by the paper's own falling-cup example.

Fouhey's account of what actually goes wrong when vision models meet real scientific deployment (validation without ground truth, systematics that models "latch onto... and emulate faithfully" alongside real signal, the need to reconcile results with existing domain knowledge rather than compare against a benchmark leaderboard) is a specific, experience-grounded list of failure modes that is more concrete and actionable than a general "domain shift" caveat, and is directly relevant to any AI-for-science system evaluated primarily by benchmark accuracy rather than by scientist acceptance.

## Verification

None, in the sense of this paper reporting original controlled experiments of its own. Individual contributor sections cite empirical results from the contributors' own prior published work (e.g., Geirhos's zero-shot Veo 3 task battery; Raghunathan's controlled algorithmic-creativity comparisons; Fouhey's solar-physics and evolutionary-ecology deployments; Du's object-centric and self-improving-world-model experiments) as supporting evidence for each individual position, but the white paper itself does not run, replicate, or adjudicate between these results — it assembles and juxtaposes them.

## Field context

This white paper sits alongside, and explicitly updates, the "scaling laws + emergent abilities" narrative established for language models (GPT-3, Wei et al.'s emergent-abilities framing, Sutton's "Bitter Lesson"), asking whether an analogous story holds for vision. Several of its themes connect directly to work already reviewed in this corpus from this same batch: Du's and Davison's emphasis on persistent, generative world models for planning and state estimation is the same broad concept this corpus's Looped World Models review ([2606.18208.md](2606.18208.md)) and the WMRL paper ([2608.12564.md](2608.12564.md)) and Large Discovery Models ([2608.15669.md](2608.15669.md)) each instantiate in a narrower, task-specific setting (respectively: architectural fidelity of a learned simulator, replacing RL environment execution with a world model, and replacing raw LLM confidence with a calibrated surrogate) — this white paper is explicitly a broader, more speculative framing exercise for the same underlying idea (a model of the world used to guide prediction and action) rather than a competing technical claim.

## Critical discussion

As a workshop-derived collection of individual position statements rather than a single argued thesis, the relevant critical question is less "is the evidence sufficient" (evidence standards vary deliberately and explicitly by contributor, from Geirhos's and Fouhey's citations of specific published results to Ramanan's and Liu's self-labeled speculative "bets") and more "does the synthesis handle the resulting heterogeneity honestly." On that count, the paper does this well in one respect and less well in another. It handles the central substantive disagreement (generation-as-evidence-of-understanding versus generation-as-insufficient-evidence) transparently, naming it explicitly in Section 3.1 and leaving it open rather than picking a side. It handles the evidentiary heterogeneity less explicitly: Table 1's summary and the Section 3 discussion present all ten positions with roughly equal narrative weight, without distinguishing which claims are backed by the contributor's own cited controlled experiments versus which are stated as personal hunches the contributor explicitly flags as possibly wrong (Ramanan: "some may turn out to be wrong"; Davison implicitly via speculative hardware-mapping claims). A reader skimming only Table 1 could mistake the collection's breadth for a uniform level of evidentiary support across all ten rows.

Separately, the contributor set is self-selected around a specific workshop and skews toward particular research programs (generative video, embodied/spatial AI, compositional structure recovery) and institutions (seven of twenty-one authors are affiliated with Oxford's VGG); the paper does not claim to be a representative survey of the field's views, and its own framing ("perspective of the computer vision community," not a poll of it) is appropriately modest about this, but it is worth noting that no contributor argues the skeptical case — that visual scaling may simply not produce LLM-like emergent generality, or that the entire "path to AGI" framing is premature or unfalsifiable in the near term. The paper's Conclusion gestures at this caution itself ("it may be premature to ask whether VGI has already become a path to general intelligence"), but this acknowledgment comes from the editors rather than from a dedicated skeptical contribution within the collection.

What remains valuable after these observations: the paper is explicit and consistent about not converging on a single definition or claim, which is the right posture for a field-framing document at this stage, and its concrete, named tensions (generation-vs-structure; scale-vs-diversity of data; static evaluation vs. continual/active evaluation) are more useful to a reader than a false consensus would be. The specific empirical results contributors cite from their own work remain individually credible pointers into the literature, but should be read and weighted as evidence for that contributor's narrower claim, not as evidence for the collection's broader, unresolved thesis about a path from vision to AGI.
