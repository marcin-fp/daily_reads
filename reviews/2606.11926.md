## What this paper is about

Current coding agents like Codex or Claude Code can run for hours on a research task, but they mostly just execute longer, not smarter — each trial is treated as an independent attempt with no structured memory of what previous failures and partial successes should teach the next one. This paper introduces Arbor, a system that gives a research agent a persistent "hypothesis tree" as external memory: a long-lived coordinator maintains competing research directions on this tree, dispatches short-lived executors to test individual hypotheses in isolated sandboxes, and only promotes a change to the working best artifact once it is verified on held-out data rather than just on the feedback used to search. Across six real research-improvement tasks and a standard ML-engineering benchmark, Arbor beats single-trajectory Codex and Claude Code baselines under matched compute.

## Extended summary

The paper formalizes the target setting as Autonomous Optimization (AO): given an initial artifact, an objective, a development evaluator that provides free iterative feedback, and a held-out test evaluator, an agent must improve the artifact without step-level human supervision, using only the development evaluator during search. Arbor's mechanism, Hypothesis Tree Refinement (HTR), represents research state as a tree where each node binds a hypothesis, the artifact version realizing it, the evaluation evidence it produced, and a distilled insight. A persistent coordinator runs a six-step loop (Observe, Ideate, Select, Dispatch, Backpropagate, Decide): it reads the tree, proposes child hypotheses under a chosen parent, selects promising pending leaves, dispatches them in parallel to short-lived executors that each implement one fixed hypothesis in an isolated git worktree and return a score/result/insight/branch reference, writes that evidence back into the tree, propagates distilled insights upward toward the root, and decides whether to prune a falsified subtree or promote a candidate. Promotion goes through a held-out merge gate: a candidate is only merged into the current-best artifact if it improves the held-out test evaluator, not merely the development evaluator used during search.

Six AO tasks are built from real research settings: Optimizer Design and Architecture Design (model training, based on NanoGPT-Bench and Karpathy's "autoresearch" codebase), Terminal-Bench 2.0 and BrowseComp (harness engineering, improving an agent's control logic), and Search-Agent and Math-Reasoning Data Synthesis (improving a data-generation pipeline, scored by a pass@4-pass@1 gap under a GPT-5.5 ReAct evaluator). Baselines are Codex (GPT-5.5) and Claude Code (Claude Opus 4.6), each given the same initial material, objective, evaluator, and a 48-hour wall-clock budget via their official long-running "/goal mode." Arbor (coordinator and executors both on Claude Opus 4.6 by default, 20 coordinator cycles, max tree depth 2) achieves the best held-out result on all six tasks, with the largest gaps on BrowseComp (67.67% held-out accuracy vs. 53.33% for Claude Code and 50.00% for Codex) and Math-Reasoning Data Synthesis (+19.79-point held-out pass-gap vs. +7.29 and +5.21). On MLE-Bench Lite, Arbor with a Gemini-3-Flash backbone ties the best same-backbone Any Medal rate (81.82%) among cited leaderboard systems while reaching a higher gold rate; switching only the backbone to GPT-5.5 (same controller, depth, and adapter) raises Any Medal to 86.36% and gold to 77.27%, the highest reported in the paper's comparison table. Most MLE-Bench Lite competitor numbers are adopted from the official leaderboard and a separate AI-Scientist paper rather than independently re-run by the authors.

A concrete overfitting illustration on Terminal-Bench motivates the held-out gate: Claude Code reaches the highest development pass rate (75.00) but its held-out score drops to 71.70, while Arbor's lower development score (72.22) corresponds to the best held-out score (77.36). Component ablations on MLE-Bench Lite (Claude Opus 4.6 backbone) show all variants reach 100% valid submissions (so the gap is not execution failure), but removing the tree structure drops Any Medal from 81.82% to 63.64%, and removing upward insight propagation while keeping the tree drops it further to 54.54% — insight feedback matters more than tree structure alone. A cross-task transfer study freezes a BrowseComp-optimized search harness and evaluates it cold on two unseen tasks (HLE, DeepSearchQA): held-out accuracy improves from 45.33% to 67.67% on BrowseComp itself, and the frozen harness also improves HLE (25.50%→31.50%) and DeepSearchQA (61.00%±6.76%→69.00%±6.41%) without further task-specific optimization. Backbone-generality tests show Arbor's gains hold with a lighter Gemini-3-Flash backbone too, though the best backbone is task-dependent (Claude Opus 4.6 best on BrowseComp, GPT-5.5 best on MLE-Bench Lite). Token-consumption analysis reports Arbor uses 20.12M-43.19M tokens across the six tasks, "a comparable scale" to the single-trajectory baselines, and the authors attribute Arbor's gains to how the budget is organized (maintaining competing hypotheses, isolated execution, evidence comparison) rather than to spending more tokens.

The paper's stated methodology (Section 5.2) says stochastic methods are run three times with Avg@3 and standard deviation "unless otherwise specified," but the main results table (Table 2) reports single point estimates for Init/Codex/Claude Code/Arbor with no visible variance; the transfer-study figure (Fig. 3b) does show a ± range for one task (DeepSearchQA), suggesting the repeated-run protocol was not applied uniformly across all reported numbers. Authors' stated limitations (Appendix A): the six-task suite covers model training, harness engineering, and data synthesis but not the full diversity of research problems (e.g., kernel optimization, pretraining data-mixture design, or biology/mathematics/physics domains where hypotheses are harder to specify and validate); the AO interface optimizes a single scalar objective rather than realistic multi-dimensional research goals (performance, robustness, interpretability, safety); agent idea generation remains far from expert-researcher ability and can reverse-engineer solutions from observed scores rather than reasoning from first principles; long-horizon search is bottlenecked by systems-engineering costs (prompt caching, evaluator scheduling, parallel worktree execution) as much as by idea quality; and Arbor inherits the underlying LLM's capability ceiling. ## Learnings

The core architectural pattern — a persistent coordinator holding a compact, durable hypothesis tree (hypothesis + artifact version + evidence + distilled insight per node) paired with short-lived, stateless executors that materialize one hypothesis each in an isolated worktree — is a reusable design for any long-horizon agentic search problem where local execution benefits from isolation and parallelism but global strategy needs state that survives individual-executor context limits and outlives any single conversation window. This generalizes the "durable external state over a lossy context window" idea beyond scientific-research automation specifically.

The held-out admission gate (promote a candidate only if it improves a held-out test evaluator, using the development evaluator solely to guide search) is a concrete, transferable defense against a failure mode the paper demonstrates quantitatively: on Terminal-Bench, the baseline with the highest development score (Claude Code, 75.00) has a lower held-out score (71.70) than Arbor, whose development score is lower (72.22) but whose held-out score is highest (77.36). Any autonomous self-improvement loop that optimizes only against its own feedback signal is exposed to this same gap without an equivalent held-out check.

The ablation finding that removing insight propagation hurts more than removing the tree structure entirely (Any Medal: 54.54% without insight feedback vs. 63.64% without the tree vs. 81.82% full system) is a specific, actionable design lesson for hierarchical-search systems generally: organizing experiments into a hierarchy is not sufficient by itself — the mechanism that converts raw results into reusable, abstracted lessons conditioning future proposals is the more load-bearing component of the two.

## Verification

The paper's main internal verification device is the dev/test split itself, and it is demonstrated concretely rather than merely assumed: the Terminal-Bench dev/test divergence for Claude Code versus Arbor is a real, quantified illustration of the overfitting risk the held-out gate is meant to catch. The cross-task transfer check (a BrowseComp-optimized harness evaluated cold on HLE and DeepSearchQA) is a genuine out-of-distribution generalization test rather than an in-distribution re-evaluation, and it reports one of the two OOD tasks (DeepSearchQA) with a variance range. Two gaps limit how far this verification extends. First, the paper's own stated reliability protocol (Avg@3 with standard deviation for stochastic methods, Sec. 5.2) does not appear applied to the headline Table 2 comparison, which reports single point estimates for every method on every task — so it is not possible to tell from the reported numbers whether gaps like the 5.66-point Terminal-Bench held-out margin (77.36 vs. 71.70) exceed run-to-run noise for these single-trajectory, non-deterministic agent systems. Second, the MLE-Bench Lite comparison (Table 3) combines Arbor's own freshly-run numbers with competitor numbers "adopted from the official... leaderboard and the AI-Scientist paper," a common benchmark-leaderboard practice but one that does not guarantee identical compute budgets, prompts, or backbone-access conditions across the compared systems.

## Field context

The paper positions Arbor relative to two adjacent lines it names directly (Section 2.1): end-to-end automated-research pipelines and agentic tree search over research plans (The AI Scientist, AI Scientist-v2, AIDE, R&D-Agent, LoongFlow, MARS, DataMaster), and systems that instead search or evolve the agent's harness itself (AutoHarness, Meta-Harness, AHE) — Arbor's distinguishing move is storing persistent research state as an explicit hypothesis tree that separates a long-lived coordinator from isolated short-lived executors, rather than growing a single context window or running a flat, unstructured search log. Within this corpus, [reviews/2603.28052.md](2603.28052.md) (Meta-Harness) is a directly cited predecessor in the harness-search sub-line this paper distinguishes itself from. Its baseline pool (Codex, Claude Code as long-horizon single-agent references, one configuration each) overlaps with the broader coding/research-agent evaluation ecosystem covered elsewhere in this batch, including [reviews/2606.05405.md](2606.05405.md) (Agents' Last Exam) and [reviews/2606.07591.md](2606.07591.md) (ResearchClawBench), both reviewed earlier in this same batch and both likewise using Codex- and Claude-Code-family systems as reference points; neither this paper nor those two directly test whether Arbor's advantage would persist against a harness-diversity sweep rather than one Codex and one Claude Code configuration. The paper's central overfitting illustration (development-score leader losing on held-out test) is thematically related to ResearchClawBench's own finding that a system's polish along secondary dimensions (Professionalism) does not track the metric that actually matters (RADS) — both are instances of a proxy/target divergence risk under autonomous self-evaluation, addressed through different mechanisms (an explicit held-out data split here, versus a multi-dimension judged rubric there).

## Critical discussion

Arbor's central claim — that organizing long-horizon search as a persistent hypothesis tree with held-out admission outperforms single continuous-trajectory coding-agent baselines under matched wall-clock and token budgets — is supported across a genuinely varied set of six real task types (model training, harness engineering, data synthesis), plus a standard external benchmark (MLE-Bench Lite) and a real out-of-distribution transfer test, which is a broader evidentiary base than a single task type would provide.

Two comparability concerns temper how far the numerical margins should be trusted. First, Codex and Claude Code are each run as one long, single continuous trajectory via their official long-running mode, while Arbor is architecturally a branching multi-executor search process with an explicit merge gate; the paper's own internal ablations (with/without tree, with/without insight feedback) isolate which parts of Arbor's own design drive its gains, but there is no equivalent "many independent long trajectories from Codex or Claude Code, keep the best one" control — so part of Arbor's edge could in principle reflect having an explicit branching-and-discard search process at all, independent of the specific hypothesis-tree/insight-propagation mechanism the paper credits, though the ablations do at least show that a flat, non-hierarchical version of Arbor's own search underperforms the full tree design. Second, the paper's stated repeated-run reliability protocol (Avg@3 with standard deviation) is not visibly applied to the headline Table 2 comparison, so several of the reported margins between Arbor and the baselines (particularly the smaller ones, such as the 1.13-2.63 percentage-point gains on the two model-training tasks) cannot be checked against run-to-run variance from the reported numbers alone. The MLE-Bench Lite leaderboard comparison additionally mixes self-run and externally-reported numbers, a widely accepted but real limitation on strict comparability.

What remains solidly supported after these caveats: a held-out admission gate measurably prevents at least one concrete instance of development-set overfitting that a single continuous agent trajectory did not avoid (the Terminal-Bench case); the ablations convincingly show that within Arbor's own architecture, both hierarchical structure and upward insight propagation contribute independently and their combination outperforms either alone; and the frozen-harness transfer result is genuine evidence that Arbor's search can produce generalizable design changes rather than only benchmark-specific overfitting, since the transfer tasks were never used during the harness's optimization.
