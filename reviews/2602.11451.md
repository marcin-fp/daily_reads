## What this paper is about

Looped Transformers save parameters by applying the same block of layers over and over instead of stacking distinct layers, and prior work shows they pick up a useful "latent reasoning" ability from this repetition. The catch is that these models are trained and tested with one fixed number of loops; if you try to run them with fewer loops at inference time to save compute, their internal representations don't degrade gracefully — they just stop being useful, because the model never learned to produce something sensible partway through its own iterative process. This paper asks: can a single looped model be trained once and then run at any number of loops a user picks afterward, with quality scaling smoothly with however much compute the user gives it? Its answer, LoopFormer, borrows an idea from diffusion models — explicitly telling each iteration how far along a normalized "time" axis it is and how big a "step" it's taking — plus a training trick that forces short and long loop-counts to agree with each other, so that stopping early still gives you something coherent rather than a stalled, half-finished computation.

## Extended summary

LoopFormer applies a shared k-block Transformer stack Φ_k repeatedly, up to a maximum of L iterations. Each iteration i is conditioned on a pair (t_{i-1}, Δ_i): the cumulative normalized time reached so far and the size of the step about to be taken, where the full sequence of step sizes for a given run (a "trajectory") must sum to 1. Both scalars are encoded via sinusoidal Fourier features and small MLPs, summed, and used (via a zero-initialized AdaLN-style modulator, borrowed from Diffusion Transformers) to scale the RMSNorm outputs and gate the residual connections around the shared block's attention and FFN sublayers — so the same weights behave differently depending on where in the trajectory the current loop sits.

Training combines three losses: a standard next-token loss on the full L-step trajectory, a next-token loss on a randomly sampled shorter S-step trajectory (S drawn uniformly from {1,...,L-1}, with its own randomly-sampled step schedule), and a stop-gradient consistency loss that pulls the shorter trajectory's final hidden state toward the (detached) full-trajectory's final hidden state — a form of self-distillation within the loop, directly analogous to shortcut/consistency training in one-step diffusion models. Both auxiliary losses are weighted at 0.1. At inference, a user picks any budget M ≤ L and a step schedule summing to 1, and the model runs M conditioned iterations with no retraining required.

Experiments use ~1B-parameter GPT-style decoders (NanoGPT configuration, d=2048) trained on a deduplicated Pile subset for 25B tokens (Chinchilla-consistent for this scale), varying the number of shared blocks k∈{1,2,3} and maximum loop count L∈{8,12,24}. Baselines split into fixed-depth (Base: non-looped Transformer; Base-Loop: standard looped model with no time conditioning; TMLT: looped model conditioned only on loop index, from prior work) and depth-elastic (Base-Loop-EE: naive early-exit on the basic looped model; Base-Loop-EE-Cons: early-exit plus the paper's own consistency loss, without time/step conditioning; TMLT-EE: TMLT plus early-exit and consistency training). This baseline design specifically isolates the paper's two candidate contributions — the consistency-loss training scheme and the time/step conditioning mechanism — since Base-Loop-EE-Cons has the loss but not the conditioning, and TMLT-EE has index-based conditioning but not the paper's specific (time, step-size) parameterization.

At the main evaluated configuration (3⊗8, i.e., 3-layer shared block, up to 8 loops), across three inference budgets (24×, 12×, 6× relative FLOPs), LoopFormer achieves the best perplexity and reasoning accuracy among all depth-elastic models at every budget tested, and at the full 24× budget it also narrows much of the gap to the non-looped Base model and to the fixed-depth TMLT baseline (10.28 vs. Base's 9.49 and TMLT's 10.38 perplexity on the Pile). Depth-elastic baselines (Base-Loop-EE-Cons, TMLT-EE, Naive-Loop-EE) degrade sharply as the budget shrinks (e.g., TMLT-EE's Pile perplexity rises from 10.7 at 24× to 15.79 at 6×), while LoopFormer degrades more gracefully (10.28 → 14.3) though it still trails the corresponding fixed-depth Base models trained specifically for each budget. A separate layer/loop scan (k∈{1,2,3}, L∈{8,12,24}) confirms perplexity and reasoning both improve with more layers per block and more loops, and that LoopFormer preserves this scaling trend under budget constraints where other depth-elastic baselines flatten or collapse.

Representation-dynamics analysis (four metrics: anisotropy, curvature, prompt entropy, cross-step CKA similarity) is used to diagnose why early-exit baselines fail: those models show flat curves and high cross-step CKA (little representational change across loop iterations, i.e., stagnation), while LoopFormer shows curvature and entropy rising through mid-depth and tapering near the final step, and progressively lower CKA between distant loop steps — interpreted as evidence LoopFormer's representations keep evolving usefully across loops rather than converging prematurely. A trajectory-schedule study exhaustively enumerates all valid step schedules for a fixed budget (M=4 for a 3⊗8 model; M=6 for a 2⊗12 model) and finds substantial performance spread across schedules at identical compute (~1.4 perplexity points, ~1.3 accuracy points for the first case; nearly 3 perplexity points for the second), with the best schedules consistently allocating coarser (larger) steps early and finer (smaller) steps late in the trajectory.

Two appendix results qualify the main comparison. First (Appendix B.1), LoopFormer's dual-trajectory training objective (computing both the full L-loop pass and a sampled shorter pass every batch) costs approximately 1.5× the training FLOPs of the fixed-loop and vanilla baselines (measured at ~1.3× wall-clock slowdown on 4×H100s) — a cost the paper states is shared by the other depth-elastic baselines (TMLT-EE, Base-Loop-EE) as well, since they use the same consistency-training scheme, but not by the fixed-depth baselines (Base, Base-Loop, TMLT) that appear in the same Table 1 comparison. Second (Appendix B.3), when LoopFormer is instead trained for fewer iterations (34k vs. 50k, ~8B fewer Pile tokens) specifically to match total training FLOPs against the fixed-depth baselines, its perplexity advantage over TMLT narrows to being merely "on par" (10.71 vs. TMLT's 10.38) rather than clearly ahead, though it retains elastic-depth capability that TMLT lacks.

## Learnings

The most transferable design idea is porting shortcut/consistency training from one-step diffusion models into the token-generation setting to solve a structurally similar problem: in diffusion, the goal is making a few-step sampling trajectory approximate a many-step one; here, the goal is making a shallow loop-count approximate a deep one, and the paper shows the same self-distillation mechanism (stop-gradient consistency loss between a short and a long trajectory sharing the same starting point) transfers to this setting. This is a specific, implementable recipe (not just an analogy) for anyone wanting "elastic compute" behavior in an iteratively-applied module, beyond looped Transformers specifically.

A second learning is the diagnostic use of representation-geometry metrics (anisotropy, curvature, prompt entropy, cross-step CKA) to distinguish "the model is using its extra loop steps" from "the model has converged to a fixed point and is wasting compute" — naive early-exit adaptations of looped models show flat/high-CKA signatures (stagnation) on exactly the same metrics where LoopFormer shows continued evolution, giving a concrete, checkable signature for representational collapse that other adaptive-depth or adaptive-compute architectures could apply as a diagnostic before or alongside benchmark evaluation.

A third, more specific and immediately actionable learning is the trajectory-schedule finding: at a fixed inference compute budget, how you spend that budget (the shape of the step schedule, not just its total size) measurably affects quality, and the best schedules consistently take larger steps early and finer steps late — a concrete, practitioner-usable heuristic (coarse-to-fine allocation) for anyone deploying a budget-conditioned iterative model, rather than a purely qualitative observation that "schedule matters."

## Verification

The paper's verification approach for its central "no stagnation" claim is a multi-metric convergent-evidence strategy rather than a single number: anisotropy, curvature, prompt entropy, and cross-step CKA are four different, independently-motivated geometric/information-theoretic measures, and all four tell the same qualitative story (flat/stagnant for early-exit baselines, evolving for LoopFormer), which is stronger evidence than any single metric alone would provide, especially since the paper explicitly and correctly labels this analysis as correlational rather than causal in its own Discussion section, appropriately bounding the claim rather than overselling a mechanistic explanation. The paper is also unusually transparent about a specific methodological asymmetry in its own headline comparison: Appendix B.1 discloses that LoopFormer's training costs ~1.5× the FLOPs of the fixed-depth baselines shown in Table 1 (though it also notes the other depth-elastic baselines share this same cost), and Appendix B.3 separately reports what happens under a training-FLOPs-matched comparison specifically — where LoopFormer's advantage over TMLT narrows to roughly parity rather than a clear win. This is a genuine, disclosed limitation rather than a hidden one, and the fact that the authors ran and reported the less-favorable, more rigorously controlled comparison in the appendix is a mark of good-faith reporting; the concern for a reader is less about honesty and more about which comparison the paper's own narrative and abstract lead with.

## Field context

The paper builds most directly on Time-Modulated Looped Transformers (TMLT; Xu & Sato 2024), explicitly extending TMLT's timestep-only conditioning to a (time, step-size) pair and adding the shortcut-consistency training scheme that TMLT lacks; the TMLT-EE ablation (TMLT plus early-exit plus the paper's own consistency loss) is the single most informative baseline in the paper because it isolates exactly how much of LoopFormer's advantage comes from the richer (time, step-size) conditioning versus the shared consistency-training recipe both models now use. It also positions itself against Mixture-of-Recursions (Bae et al. 2025), which the paper distinguishes on the axis of granularity: MoR routes/varies loop count per-token, while LoopFormer commits to a single sequence-level budget chosen once per generation — a design choice the paper's own Discussion explicitly flags as a limitation (no token-level adaptivity) rather than presenting as a strict advantage. The paper's core motivating observation — that looped/recurrent architectures trained at one fixed depth degrade badly when evaluated off-distribution at other depths — is a specific instance, in the architecture space, of a pattern this batch has repeatedly observed in other spaces: a system optimized against one fixed evaluation condition (a fixed loop count here; a fixed reward signal or judge elsewhere, as in 2606.04075 and 2605.20025) generalizes poorly the moment that condition shifts, and LoopFormer's shortcut-consistency training is a concrete architectural response to exactly this generalization problem, applied to compute-budget shift specifically rather than to a reward or evaluation-signal shift.

## Critical discussion

The paper's Table 1 headline comparison — where LoopFormer beats other depth-elastic baselines and narrows the gap to fixed-depth models — is not training-FLOPs-matched against the fixed-depth Base/Base-Loop/TMLT rows shown in the same table: LoopFormer's dual-trajectory objective costs roughly 1.5× the training compute of those fixed-depth baselines (Appendix B.1), and the paper's own appendix (B.3) shows that under an explicitly FLOPs-matched training regime, LoopFormer's advantage over TMLT specifically narrows to rough parity rather than a clear win (10.71 vs. 10.38 perplexity). This does not undermine the paper's core, more specific claim (elastic-depth models specifically benefit from time/step conditioning plus consistency training, evidenced by beating the other depth-elastic baselines that share the training-cost handicap), but it does mean the paper's broader framing — that LoopFormer "closes much of the gap" to fixed-depth models — should be read with the training-cost asymmetry in mind, since a fixed-depth TMLT trained with 1.5× the compute LoopFormer actually uses per the FLOPs-matched appendix comparison would likely retain at least part of the advantage the main table shows it having in the unmatched setting.

A second, more general point is that every quantitative result in the paper — Table 1's cross-budget comparisons, the layer/loop scaling curves, the exhaustive trajectory-schedule study, the representation-geometry metrics — appears to reflect a single training run per configuration, with no reported seed variance or confidence intervals. Given that several of the reported comparisons in Table 1 involve margins of roughly half a point to a point or two on downstream accuracy (e.g., LoopFormer's 44.81% average accuracy vs. TMLT-EE's 44.03% at the 24× budget), and given that all experiments are run at a relatively modest ~1B-parameter, 25B-token scale on a single dataset family (The Pile plus two held-out perplexity sets and ten zero-shot benchmarks), a reader should treat the specific numeric ranking among the closely-performing depth-elastic baselines as suggestive rather than statistically established, and should be cautious about extrapolating the reported trends to substantially larger model or token scales that this study does not test.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the qualitative, larger-margin finding that naive early-exit adaptations of looped models suffer severe degradation under reduced compute budgets while LoopFormer degrades much more gracefully is demonstrated consistently across multiple budget levels (24×/12×/6×), multiple (k, L) configurations, and is independently corroborated by the representation-geometry analysis showing a mechanistically plausible cause (stagnation vs. continued evolution) rather than resting on benchmark numbers alone; the trajectory-schedule finding (coarse-early, fine-late schedules outperform others at fixed budget) is demonstrated through an exhaustive enumeration rather than a cherry-picked comparison, which is a methodologically strong way to establish that specific, narrower claim; and the paper's own transparent reporting of the training-FLOPs asymmetry and its effect under a matched-compute regime (Appendix B.3) is itself evidence the paper's central, narrower contribution (conditioning plus consistency training helps specifically within the depth-elastic model class) is more robust than the broader framing relative to fixed-depth models might suggest on a first read of the main table alone.
