## What this paper is about

Transformers are famously bad at multi-digit addition, and this paper argues the root cause is not that addition itself is hard for a neural network to learn, but that transformers struggle to keep track of which digit sits in which column, exactly the alignment a human performs automatically when doing long addition on paper. The paper's fix, Abacus Embeddings, is a learned positional embedding that explicitly tags each digit with its position relative to the start of its own number (so all "hundreds-place" digits get the same positional tag, regardless of how long the overall number is), and the paper shows this single change, combined with two architectural additions (input injection and a recurrent "looped" transformer design), lets a small model trained from scratch on numbers up to only 20 digits long correctly add numbers up to 100 digits and beyond, a substantially larger jump in generalization than any prior published method, with the same underlying trick also transferring to multiplication and array sorting.

## Extended summary

The paper's core diagnosis is that standard positional embedding schemes (absolute, rotary, or even the strong length-generalization-focused FIRE embedding) do not give a transformer an explicit, easy-to-use signal for a digit's significance within a specific number, which is exactly the information needed to correctly align digits from two different operands before adding them column by column. Prior work addressed this with explicit index hints inserted into the input and output text (e.g., writing "a6b7c5" instead of "675"), which the paper notes roughly doubles output length and inference cost and, per a directly cited prior study, produces results highly sensitive to random initialization (ranging from near-perfect on 100-digit addition to complete failure on 90-digit addition depending on the seed). Abacus Embeddings instead solve the alignment problem architecturally: each digit in a number receives a positional embedding based on its position relative to the start of that specific number (not the whole sequence), with training using a randomized starting offset drawn uniformly from a range hyperparameter k (default 100) so the model sees a wide variety of positional-embedding values even when actual training sequences are comparatively short, an idea adapted from prior randomized-embedding work but using consecutive rather than fully random indices specifically to support this kind of length extrapolation.

All experiments use decoder-only causal transformers trained entirely from scratch under a fixed, modest compute budget (a single Nvidia RTXA4000 GPU for 24 hours for addition, an 8-GPU version for multiplication), with numbers formatted least-significant-digit-first (a known helpful convention from prior work) and no digit padding, evaluated with strict exact-match accuracy (any single wrong digit counts the whole answer wrong) across three regimes: in-distribution (up to the training operand length), out-of-distribution (beyond training length, up to 100 digits), and extreme OOD (100-160 digits, same-length operands only, tested selectively due to inference cost). On addition alone, Abacus Embeddings substantially outperform both FIRE and NoPE (no positional embedding) baselines, and, combined with input injection (a skip connection feeding the raw embedded input to every decoder layer, not just the first), models trained on operands up to only 20 digits achieve up to 99% accuracy on 100-digit addition, a reported 6x length-generalization factor versus a previous best of 2.5x, which the paper states is, to its knowledge, the longest sequence length ever demonstrated for learned addition. Adding a recurrent, "looped" transformer architecture (a single decoder block whose weights are reused across multiple forward passes, functioning like an adjustable-depth network at inference time, trained with a "progressive loss" that mixes full-recurrence and randomly-reduced-recurrence forward passes) improves results further still: out-of-distribution accuracy rises from 92.9% (Abacus Embeddings with a standard architecture) to 99.1% (Abacus Embeddings plus input injection plus looping), an 87% reduction in error, despite the looped model having roughly 10x fewer parameters than the non-recurrent comparison model since only one layer's weights are actually stored. A further ablation systematically trades off the number of unique layers in the recurrent block against how many times that block is looped while holding total effective depth fixed (from a fully non-recurrent 16x1 configuration through to a maximally-recurrent 1x16 configuration), finding an intermediate configuration (8 layers looped twice) achieves the best out-of-distribution accuracy, halving the error rate of the purely non-recurrent input-injection model.

The paper then extends these techniques to more demanding tasks. A combined addition-and-subtraction dataset shows the same architecture (8-layer recurrent block, 2 recurrences) can simultaneously learn to extrapolate on both a symmetric operation (addition) and an anti-symmetric one (subtraction) using Abacus Embeddings, without a separate model per operation. Multiplication, a substantially harder problem since output length scales with the sum rather than the max of the two operand lengths (creating longer-range dependencies), is tackled with looped transformers only (the authors explicitly note the much greater compute and hyperparameter-search cost of multiplication limited them to this narrower architectural scope), removing input injection from inside the recurrent block and down-weighting gradients from higher-recurrence-count batches; the result is near-saturated in-distribution accuracy on 15-digit multiplication, exceeding a directly cited prior result without needing to zero-pad operands to equal length, with Abacus Embeddings specifically outperforming FIRE-only models on the hardest in-distribution multiplication problems. Array sorting (sorting up to 10 numbers of up to 10 digits each, tested with generalization to 30-number arrays of up to 30-digit numbers) is used as a more demanding testbed with two independent axes of extrapolation, longer individual numbers versus longer arrays. Here the results are genuinely mixed rather than uniformly favorable to Abacus Embeddings: Abacus alone is best when the individual numbers get longer (68.63% vs. FIRE's 55.32%), but FIRE alone is actually better when the array itself gets longer (21.35% vs. Abacus's 9.67%), and the combined Abacus+FIRE embedding is the most robust choice across both extrapolation axes simultaneously and in the "all OOD" (both axes stressed at once) condition, though a standard (non-recurrent) transformer architecture achieves the best overall exact-match accuracy for sorting while the looped transformer specifically excels at the narrower sub-task of identifying just the minimum array element, illustrating that different architectural variants suit different extrapolation demands even within the same broader task. Finally, the paper checks compatibility with the positional-embedding schemes actually used in deployed large language models: while Abacus Embeddings are, strictly speaking, a form of absolute positional embedding and therefore cannot generalize beyond the specific range of relative positions covered during training (mitigated in practice by randomizing the offset hyperparameter k), the paper shows Abacus Embeddings combine productively with both RoPE (the rotary embedding scheme used in most modern open-source LLMs, otherwise known to generalize length poorly) and FIRE, in each case unlocking meaningfully better out-of-distribution addition accuracy than either base scheme achieves alone, which the authors present as the key evidence that Abacus Embeddings could plausibly be incorporated into a general-purpose model without abandoning whatever positional scheme that model already relies on for ordinary language tasks.

## Learnings

The paper's central, highly transferable insight is a precise diagnosis of what specifically makes multi-digit arithmetic hard for a transformer, not the arithmetic operation itself, but the model's inability to represent a digit's significance (its position relative to the start of its own number) in a way that supports reliable cross-operand alignment, directly analogous to how a human aligns digit columns before performing long addition by hand. This is a specific, actionable template for approaching any task believed to be "hard for transformers": rather than assuming the surface-level task itself exceeds the architecture's capacity, it is worth asking whether some specific, nameable sub-piece of information the task requires (here, exact intra-number digit position) is simply not being made legible to the model in a form it can use, since fixing that legibility gap alone can resolve what looks like a much larger capability failure.

A second, more architecturally general learning is the demonstrated value of combining a positional fix with recurrent computation specifically for tasks with a genuinely iterative, multi-step structure: input injection and looped-transformer recurrence each independently improve on top of Abacus Embeddings, and their combination (an intermediate recurrent-block size, not the most extreme fully-looped configuration) gives the best result, at a fraction of the parameter count of an equivalently-deep non-recurrent model. This is a specific, reusable pattern (recurrent weight-sharing plus input injection, trained with a progressive/variable-recurrence loss) worth considering whenever a task has a natural notion of "more steps needed for a harder instance," since the architecture itself is then structurally suited to allocate more computation to harder problems without needing more parameters.

A third, more cautionary learning comes from the array-sorting results specifically: the paper's headline technique (Abacus Embeddings) is not uniformly best across every axis of extrapolation even within a single task, it wins decisively for longer individual numbers but loses to a competing embedding for longer arrays, and only the combined embedding is robust across both. This is a concrete, self-reported example of a general caution worth carrying elsewhere: a technique validated on one specific extrapolation axis (here, digit length) should not be assumed to generalize to a structurally different axis of the same task (here, array length) without separately testing it, since the underlying mechanism that helps with one may simply not address the other.

## Verification

The paper's verification practices are notably careful for a paper of this kind: every reported result is explicitly stated to be the mean of three independently trained models rather than a single run or a favorable best-of-many selection, which the authors contrast directly against a prior "best-of-ten" reporting convention they consider less representative of typical performance; accuracy is measured with a strict exact-match criterion (any single incorrect output digit counts as a full failure) rather than a softer per-digit metric that could mask partial failures; and the evaluation explicitly reports accuracy for every combination of operand lengths, including mismatched-length pairs most prior work omits, specifically to surface every instance of extrapolation rather than only the easiest same-length case. The paper also directly engages a documented reliability concern in this exact research area: it cites prior work's finding that an index-hint-based approach shows extreme seed-to-seed variance (from near-perfect to complete failure depending on random initialization) as a specific motivation for its own architectural choice, treating that instability as a real prior failure to design around rather than dismissing it. The recurrent-block-size ablation (sweeping from fully non-recurrent to maximally recurrent while holding effective depth fixed) is a genuinely controlled experiment isolating one specific architectural axis, and the paper is transparent that the non-recurrent comparison points in that sweep have substantially more parameters than the recurrent ones, reporting exact parameter counts in an appendix table rather than letting the parameter-count confound go unaddressed. One verification gap remains: although every headline number is described as an average over three runs, no run-to-run variance or standard deviation is reported anywhere for the paper's own results, which stands in some tension with the paper's own citation of severe seed-sensitivity as a known problem for the closely related prior index-hint method; a reader cannot currently tell from this paper alone how much of its own three-run averages might be masking meaningful spread of the same general kind it identifies as a weakness in that prior approach.

## Field context

This paper sits in the algorithmic-reasoning and length-generalization literature it surveys directly, building on and explicitly improving upon the index-hint approach (Zhou et al. 2023, 2024) as its primary point of comparison, on FIRE embeddings (Li et al. 2023) as the strongest prior positional-embedding baseline for this task, and on randomized embeddings (Ruoss et al. 2023) as the direct technical predecessor to its own randomized-offset training scheme; it also connects its recurrent-architecture choices to a separate line of work on weight-shared, adaptive-computation networks (Dehghani et al. 2018's Universal Transformer; Schwarzschild et al. 2021 and Bansal et al. 2022's recurrent convolutional networks for algorithmic tasks like mazes and prefix sums, the latter's progressive-loss technique adopted directly here). Within this corpus, the paper is a natural architectural sibling to [2311.01906] (Simplifying Transformer Blocks, reviewed earlier in this batch): both papers investigate which specific, precisely-targeted modifications to the standard transformer block meaningfully change its training and generalization behavior, but from opposite directions, Simplifying Transformer Blocks asks which standard components can be removed without losing performance, while this paper asks which single, small addition (a specialized positional embedding) can be made to unlock capability the standard architecture entirely lacks; read together they illustrate that the "minimal necessary transformer block" question runs in both directions depending on the task, sometimes toward removing complexity, sometimes toward adding one precisely-targeted piece. The paper's broader theme, that transformers reliably fail at precise, multi-step symbolic computation without specific architectural or representational help, also echoes a small but concrete data point already documented elsewhere in this corpus: [2211.09085] (Galactica, reviewed in an earlier batch) reports, in an appendix aside, extremely low accuracy (0.5-3%) on a checkmate-in-one chess task across all its model sizes despite otherwise strong scientific-knowledge performance, an independent, differently-sourced illustration of the same general pattern this paper investigates in depth for arithmetic: transformers can be highly capable at open-ended knowledge tasks while remaining strikingly poor at exact, multi-step symbolic reasoning unless something specific is done to address the representational gap.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central empirical claims are unusually well-supported for an architecture paper: results are consistently averaged over three independent training runs rather than single or cherry-picked runs, the evaluation grid deliberately includes mismatched-length operand pairs that most prior work omits, and the ablation isolating recurrent-block size from total effective depth is a genuinely controlled comparison rather than only an end-to-end result. The paper is also commendably candid about a result that complicates its own headline framing, the array-sorting extrapolation axis where Abacus Embeddings alone underperform FIRE alone, rather than selectively reporting only the sorting results that favor its proposed method.

The clearest limitation, one the paper's own Limitations section states directly rather than one this review is raising independently, is that no experiment in the paper touches natural language at all: every result is from models trained entirely from scratch on synthetic arithmetic, sorting, or mixed addition/subtraction data under a fixed, comparatively small compute budget, so while the paper demonstrates that Abacus Embeddings are at least mechanically compatible with FIRE and RoPE in this synthetic setting, whether the technique would actually help (or interact safely with) a general-purpose language model handling ordinary text alongside numerical reasoning remains untested by anything in this paper. A second, related point concerns compute-driven scope narrowing that the paper discloses but that meaningfully affects how the results should be weighted: the multiplication experiments use only looped transformers, explicitly because a full architectural sweep analogous to the addition experiments was judged too expensive, so the multiplication results, while a genuine positive demonstration, rest on a considerably narrower search than the addition results the paper's headline claims are drawn from. A third point concerns the paper's own reporting standard: despite explicitly citing severe seed-to-seed variance as a specific, named weakness of the closely competing index-hint method, and despite averaging its own results over three runs specifically to be more representative than a best-of-many report, the paper does not report the spread or standard deviation across those three runs for any of its own results, so a reader cannot use anything in the paper itself to check whether Abacus-Embedding-based training shows the same degree of seed sensitivity that the paper identifies as a problem elsewhere, or is meaningfully more stable, which the paper's own framing (three-run averaging is treated as sufficient to avoid unrepresentative reporting) implies but does not directly demonstrate with a reported variance figure.

What remains well supported: an explicit, digit-relative-to-number positional embedding substantially outperforms strong existing length-generalization embeddings (FIRE, NoPE) on multi-digit addition, achieving a considerably larger length-generalization factor than prior published methods; combining this embedding with input injection and a recurrent, weight-shared transformer architecture provides further, independently-verified accuracy gains at a fraction of the parameter count of an equivalent non-recurrent model; the underlying idea transfers, with task-specific architectural adjustments, to multiplication and array sorting, though the paper's own sorting results show this transfer is not uniformly the best choice on every extrapolation axis of that task; and the technique is mechanically compatible with the positional embedding schemes (RoPE, FIRE) actually used in deployed language models, a real and specifically-tested compatibility result rather than only an assumed one, even though its integration into an actual general-purpose model remains, by the paper's own account, unexplored.
