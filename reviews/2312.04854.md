Citation: Wang, H., Du, X., Yu, W., Chen, Q., Zhu, K., Chu, Z., Yan, L., Guan, Y. Learning to Break: Knowledge-Enhanced Reasoning in Multi-Agent Debate System. Neurocomputing, 2025 (arXiv:2312.04854, 2023).

## What this paper is about

When several instances of a language model debate a question to reach a more reliable answer, the approach only works as well as what each debater actually knows: if two agents have limited, mismatched, or simply wrong background knowledge on a topic, a debate can still converge, just on a confidently wrong answer that both sides agree on. This paper calls that failure mode "cognitive islands" and proposes MADKE, a multi-agent debate framework where debaters can adaptively pull relevant passages from a shared, retrieval-built knowledge pool (Wikipedia plus Google search results) before stating their view in each round, filtering the pool down to only what each agent judges personally useful rather than force-feeding everyone identical context. Across six question-answering and fact-checking datasets, this retrieval-augmented debate setup beats both single-agent and prior multi-agent-debate baselines, and, run on the open-source Qwen1.5-72B-Chat model, edges out GPT-4 on average.

## Extended summary

MADKE's debate proceeds in three stages, orchestrated by three LLM-instantiated roles (debater, judge, summarizer). A shared knowledge pool is built per question by combining the top-5 passages from a Dense Passage Retriever (DPR) run against a Wikipedia dump and the top-10 (title-plus-snippet) results from a Google search API. Before speaking in any round, each debater runs an "adaptive knowledge selection" prompt that lets it pick up to 3 of the most useful pool items for its own current reasoning, or explicitly flag that none apply, rather than being handed identical or forced context; the paper motivates this specifically to avoid two failure modes, injecting noise from irrelevant retrieved passages, and flattening the personalized reasoning that gives multi-agent debate its diversity benefit in the first place. The debate itself has a "simultaneous-talk" round (each debater answers independently, blind to the others) followed by "orderly-talk" rounds (debaters see each other's prior-round stances and selected knowledge, then may update or hold their position) for up to a fixed maximum number of rounds; after each round a separate judge-agent checks whether all debaters' stated answers are now consistent and, if so, ends the debate early rather than running to the round cap regardless. A summarizer agent, instructed to remain strictly impartial and not introduce its own opinions, produces the final answer from the debaters' outputs once the debate concludes.

Experiments span three single-hop QA datasets (TriviaQA, NQ), two multi-hop QA datasets (HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA), a fact-verification dataset (FEVER), and a constructed medical-domain benchmark (MMLU-Med, drawn from six MMLU medical subcategories), each subsampled to 500 (595 for MMLU-Med) examples for cost reasons. Four backbones are tested (GPT-4-Turbo as an upper-bound reference, GPT-3.5-Turbo, and Qwen1.5-32B-Chat and -72B-Chat), each compared against standard prompting, chain-of-thought, self-consistency, plain RAG (retrieval without debate), and the prior non-knowledge multi-agent-debate baseline (Du et al. 2023's MAD), using extract match for discriminative tasks and, for generative QA, both F1 and a GPT-4-based correctness judgment ("GPT4 Score," where GPT-4 is given the reference answer and asked to judge a candidate answer's correctness). MADKE outperforms every non-GPT-4 baseline across nearly every backbone-dataset combination, with the paper reporting a +4.6% average improvement over the strongest prior baseline when run on Qwen1.5-72B-Chat across the five open-domain datasets, and, notably, MADKE on Qwen1.5-72B-Chat exceeds GPT-4-Turbo's own standard-prompting GPT4 Score on four of five open-domain datasets (NQ, HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA, FEVER, by margins of roughly 5.6-7.8 points), averaging a reported +1.26% edge over GPT-4 across all six datasets including MMLU-Med (where MADKE still trails GPT-4 substantially, a gap the paper attributes to noisier, less relevant retrieval in the specialized medical domain).

To measure the "cognitive islands" construct directly rather than only overall accuracy, the paper defines two metrics: Cons (the fraction of examples where all debaters' final-round answers agree with each other, regardless of correctness) and Co2 (the fraction where they agree and are correct). Adding shared knowledge produces a small average decrease in Cons (about -1.6% for Qwen1.5-Chat backbones across five datasets), which the paper interprets as personalized knowledge legitimately broadening what different debaters argue rather than a defect, but a much larger average increase in Co2 (+7.8%), which the paper reads as the intervention's real payoff: debaters converge less often, but converge on the right answer distinctly more often. An ablation compares no-retrieval, Wikipedia-only, Google-only, and combined-source knowledge pools, finding Google search alone (+6.8% average GPT4 Score/EM improvement over no retrieval) outperforms Wikipedia alone (+2.7%), attributed to Google's built-in relevance ranking versus Wikipedia's fixed-length-segment chunking often missing the specific fact needed, with the combined pool performing best overall (+8.1%). A second ablation isolates the adaptive knowledge selection module itself (versus presumably handing debaters the whole retrieved pool unfiltered), finding it particularly impactful on the two multi-hop datasets, where retrieved passages are more likely to be tangentially related without directly answering the question, i.e., noisier by construction.

A parameter-scale sweep across the Qwen1.5-Chat family (1.8B to 72B) finds that adding retrieval knowledge produces little benefit at 1.8B-14B scale but a clearly growing benefit from 32B upward, which the paper interprets as smaller models lacking the "self-awareness" (the ability to accurately judge which retrieved passages are actually useful to their own reasoning) needed to exploit the adaptive-selection mechanism, with MADKE first matching or exceeding GPT-4's performance specifically once the backbone reaches 32B parameters. A separate sensitivity study varying the number of debaters (2-6) and the maximum debate-round cap on TriviaQA and HotpotQA finds that increasing the round cap increases both average rounds actually used and both consistency metrics (more rounds let harder, multi-hop problems get resolved more thoroughly), but that increasing the number of debaters beyond two does not meaningfully help and can hurt: with more debaters, individual agents in the sequential orderly-talk stage tend to converge toward whichever answer is stated most frequently by others rather than reasoning independently, reducing exactly the argumentative diversity the debate format is meant to leverage; the paper concludes a two-sided debate format is the more effective configuration for this framework. A token-consumption analysis finds MADKE costs more output tokens than single-agent baselines (an expected cost of running a multi-agent debate) but roughly 27.5% fewer tokens than the prior non-knowledge MAD baseline while still scoring 6.2 points higher, attributed specifically to the judge agent's ability to end a debate early once consensus is reached rather than always running to the fixed round limit as the earlier MAD method does. A qualitative case study on one HotpotQA example illustrates the intended mechanism directly: a debater initially answers incorrectly without invoking any retrieved knowledge, then in the next round selects knowledge relevant both to its own prior (wrong) stance and to the correct answer, and updates to the correct final answer after weighing that knowledge against the other debater's position.

## Learnings

The paper's central, reusable design idea is separating "give agents access to shared external grounding" from "force every agent to use identical context": the adaptive knowledge-selection step, where each debater independently decides which (if any) retrieved items are relevant to its own current reasoning before speaking, is presented and empirically supported as the mechanism that lets a shared knowledge pool improve correctness without collapsing the personalized reasoning diversity that a multi-agent debate is supposed to provide in the first place. This is a specific, transferable answer to a general tension in group deliberation systems (shared grounding versus preserved independence): rather than choosing one over the other, giving each participant selective, self-directed access to a common pool is offered as a way to get some of both.

A second, more surprising and directly actionable learning concerns model scale: the benefit of adding retrieved knowledge to a debate is reported to be negligible below roughly 32B parameters and to grow clearly above it, which the paper frames as a capability threshold in a model's ability to judge the relevance of retrieved evidence to its own reasoning rather than a smooth, continuous scaling effect. This is a concrete, falsifiable claim worth checking directly (rather than assuming any RAG-style intervention transfers uniformly across model sizes) whenever a retrieval-augmented multi-agent design is being deployed with a smaller backbone.

A third, more cautionary learning comes from the debater-count sensitivity study: adding more debating agents to a sequential, orderly-talk-style debate did not improve, and could actively harm, outcome quality, because participants in a sequential exchange increasingly gravitate toward whatever position has already been stated most often rather than reasoning independently, a specific, mechanistic account of diminishing (or negative) returns to scaling up the number of debate participants that is a useful check against assuming "more agents debating" is automatically better for multi-agent reasoning system design generally.

## Verification

The paper's verification of its central "cognitive islands" mechanism is a genuine strength: rather than relying only on aggregate task accuracy, it defines and reports two purpose-built metrics (Cons for raw agreement, Co2 for agreement-and-correctness) specifically designed to distinguish "debaters converged" from "debaters converged on the right answer," which is exactly the distinction the paper's own motivating example (Figure 2a, two agents converging on a wrong shared answer) requires to demonstrate the intervention addresses the intended failure mode rather than just improving accuracy through some unrelated channel. The retrieval-source and adaptive-selection ablations each isolate one specific design choice against the alternative of omitting it, rather than only comparing the full system against unrelated baselines, letting a reader attribute the reported gains to specific components. The qualitative case study functions as a direct, example-level trace confirming the mechanism the aggregate Co2/Cons statistics only describe in the aggregate: a debater is shown selecting specific numbered knowledge items and changing its answer as a documented consequence, rather than the mechanism being inferred only from summary statistics. Two verification gaps stand out. First, correctness for the generative open-domain datasets is judged in part by GPT-4 itself (the "GPT4 Score" metric), while GPT-4-Turbo's own standard-prompting output is simultaneously one of the systems being compared in the same table, including in the very same-family-judge configuration the field has flagged elsewhere as a source of self-preference bias in LLM-as-judge evaluation; the paper does not report any check (such as an alternative judge model or a human-agreement spot-check on the GPT4 Score itself) that would rule out such bias affecting the reported margins against GPT-4. Second, none of the main results tables report variance across repeated runs or random seeds, despite the paper explicitly setting a non-zero sampling temperature (0.5) specifically to preserve debate diversity, meaning the reported point-estimate margins, some of which are only a few percentage points on 500-example subsets, cannot currently be distinguished from run-to-run sampling noise using anything reported in the paper itself.

## Field context

This paper sits at the intersection of two lines of work already well represented in this corpus and the broader literature: multi-agent debate for LLM reasoning (Du et al. 2023's original multi-agent-debate framework, which this paper adopts directly as its strongest prior-work baseline and specifically improves on by adding shared retrieval and an early-stopping judge) and retrieval-augmented generation (citing Izacard et al. 2022's Atlas-style retrieval-augmented LMs and Yao et al. 2022's ReAct as prior approaches to injecting external knowledge, distinguishing its own contribution as specifically addressing personalized, adaptive knowledge selection within a multi-party debate rather than single-agent retrieval). Within this corpus, the paper's central diagnosis, that multiple instances of a debate system built on the same underlying model can converge confidently on a shared wrong answer precisely because their knowledge and viewpoints are not genuinely independent, is a direct, concretely-named instance of the same-model/same-backbone debate-diversity concern documented across several other reviewed multi-agent papers (the recurring pattern across 24selfcor, 25mcrit, 26madrag, and 26masense of debate participants sharing the same underlying model and thus lacking the kind of genuine independence needed for debate to correct rather than reinforce shared errors). MADKE's proposed fix, breaking that shared-knowledge bottleneck by giving each debater independent, adaptively-selected access to external retrieval rather than relying only on the base model's own internal knowledge for diversity, is a specific, testable answer to that broader concern, though the paper's own experimental design (each table row uses a single backbone for all debater, judge, and summarizer roles) does not itself test whether combining genuinely different underlying models as debaters, an orthogonal way to increase independence, would compound with or substitute for the retrieval-based fix it proposes.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claim, that adaptively-selected shared retrieval knowledge measurably breaks cognitive islands in multi-agent debate, is well-supported by its own purpose-built Co2/Cons metric pair and by a concrete, traced case study, a genuinely more rigorous verification of the specific claimed mechanism than reporting only aggregate accuracy would provide. The ablations (retrieval source, adaptive selection) are each cleanly isolated and the reported patterns (Google beating Wikipedia; adaptive selection mattering more on noisier multi-hop retrieval) are each given a specific, checkable mechanistic explanation rather than left as an unexplained correlation.

The most significant methodological concern is evaluation circularity: the paper's headline claim of exceeding GPT-4 rests substantially on the "GPT4 Score" metric, which uses GPT-4 itself as the judge of answer correctness across every system being compared, including GPT-4-Turbo's own standard-prompting baseline in the very same comparison table; without an independent judge model or a reported human-agreement check specifically on this metric, a reader cannot rule out that some portion of the reported gap over GPT-4 reflects the judge's own stylistic or self-referential preferences rather than purely factual correctness, a concern the field has documented elsewhere for same-family LLM-as-judge setups and one this paper does not address directly. A second, related gap is the complete absence of reported run-to-run variance: every main result is a single point estimate on a 500-example (or 595-example) subsample, despite the system's explicit use of temperature-based sampling for diversity, so several of the more marginal reported differences (for instance, MADKE's F1/EM scores on FEVER trailing plain RAG for the GPT-3.5-Turbo backbone specifically) cannot currently be distinguished from sampling noise using anything the paper itself reports. A third, narrower point: every experimental configuration uses a single backbone model for all of a run's debaters, judge, and summarizer roles, so while the paper's motivating problem (cognitive islands from limited, non-independent knowledge) is framed generally, its proposed solution is tested only as a fix for same-model debate rather than compared against, or combined with, the alternative and arguably more direct fix of using genuinely different backbone models as debaters, leaving open how much of the reported improvement is specific to the retrieval mechanism versus how it interacts with (or substitutes for) cross-model diversity the paper does not test.

What remains well supported: giving debate agents adaptive, self-selected access to a shared external knowledge pool measurably increases the fraction of examples where debaters converge on a correct rather than merely consistent answer, a specific and directly-verified claim about the intended cognitive-islands mechanism rather than only an aggregate accuracy improvement; the benefit of this mechanism is concentrated at larger model scales (32B parameters and above) rather than holding uniformly across scale; and a judge agent that can end debate early on consensus meaningfully reduces computational cost relative to a fixed-round-count debate baseline without sacrificing, and in this case improving, task performance.
