Citation: Zhou, W., Zhou, X., Han, S., Xu, H., Li, G., Li, Z., Xiong, F., Wu, F. Are We Ready For An Agent-Native Memory System? arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24775v1

## What this paper is about

Dozens of "agent memory" systems (Mem0, Zep, MemGPT/Letta, A-MEM, MemOS, and others) have appeared to give LLM agents persistent state across sessions, but they are usually compared only by end-to-end task-success scores (F1, EM) that treat each system as a black box. This paper, from a database-systems perspective, decomposes agent memory into four architectural modules (representation/storage, extraction, retrieval/routing, maintenance), builds a unified taxonomy of how twelve representative systems instantiate each module, and then runs both end-to-end and fine-grained controlled experiments to work out which specific design choices actually drive effectiveness, robustness, and operational cost.

## Extended summary

The paper first formalizes an agent memory system as a tuple M_sys = <R, S, Q, U>: representation-and-storage R (logical format, from flat text/vector tokens to knowledge graphs, trees, and heterogeneous composites; physical backend, from transient in-context registers to specialized single-engine or multi-engine stores), extraction S (how raw interaction streams become memory primitives, from raw concatenation through schema-free semantic extraction to schema-constrained structured extraction), retrieval-and-routing Q (native attention, dense KNN, topological subgraph traversal, autonomous agentic routing via function calls or query rewriting, and multi-stage hybrid execution), and maintenance U (conflict resolution/versioning, capacity-driven eviction, LLM-driven semantic consolidation, and continuous parametric optimization). It explicitly distinguishes agent memory from RAG (a stateless, read-only retrieval primitive) and from context engineering (curating what enters a single inference turn), framing memory instead as persistent, updatable infrastructure governing the full lifecycle across turns and sessions. Table 1 classifies twelve systems (MemoChat, Mem0, MEM1, MemAgent, MemTree, Zep, Mem0^g, Cognee, LightMem, SimpleMem, MemOS, MemoryOS, A-MEM, Letta) along all four axes.

The end-to-end evaluation (Section 4) runs five research questions across five workloads and eleven datasets. RQ1 (overall effectiveness, on LoCoMo, LongMemEval/MemoryAgentBench, and DB-Bench/LifelongAgentBench) finds no single architecture dominates: structure-aware systems (Zep, Cognee) lead LongMemEval, hybrid-filtering systems (MemOS) lead LoCoMo exactness, and trace-preserving approaches (plain Long Context, MemoChat) lead DB-Bench's procedural-execution workload; across full workload coverage, MemoryOS and MemOS stay closest to an overall frontier. RQ2 (retrieval fidelity, on LoCoMo with gold evidence spans) finds retrieval quality is less about ranking one relevant memory first than about evidence completeness at larger budgets: SimpleMem wins Recall@1 (39.0) but A-MEM and MemTree pull ahead at Recall@5/@10 (69.5/85.9 and 59.7/80.5) and degrade far less as the temporal gap between query and supporting evidence widens, while flat Embedding RAG drops sharply once evidence is not recent. RQ3 (update robustness, on LongMemEval's Knowledge-Update and Temporal-Reasoning slices plus LoCoMo's Temporal slice, and a four-backbone ablation using Qwen3-8B/DeepSeek-Chat/GPT-5.4-mini/GPT-5.4) finds graph- or relation-organized systems (Zep, Cognee) handle direct fact revision and temporally dispersed evidence best, while systems lacking explicit lifecycle management return stale facts ("hallucinations of the past"); backbone strength changes absolute answer quality more than it changes which memory pipeline wins, with MemOS remaining the strongest configuration across all four tested backbones. RQ4 (long-horizon stability, on LongBench, LongMemEval, and LoCoMo binned by context length/session count/evidence distance) finds relation-aware or hierarchical-consolidation systems (Cognee, MemOS, MemoryOS, SimpleMem) degrade far less than flat long-context prompting or dense-only retrieval as the effective horizon grows (e.g., Embedding RAG falls from 37.1 to 7.4 Answer F1 on LoCoMo as the evidence gap widens). RQ5 (operational cost, on 8 systems using a unified time-overhead runner) finds efficiency tracks maintenance scope rather than structure per se: LightMem and MemTree sit on the strongest cost-utility frontier (LightMem: 48.3 utility at 3.67s/query; MemTree: 63.5 at 15.9s), while systems doing broad graph-wide consolidation or multi-store synchronization (Cognee, Zep, MemoryOS) reach higher utility only at 30-155+ seconds per query.

Section 5's fine-grained, single-module ablations (holding a system's other three modules fixed) isolate specific causal effects. M1 (representation): LightMem's raw, verbatim user-turn storage clearly beats both an LLM-generated abstractive summary and a lightly-compressed variant on both benchmarks, and a deeper MemTree hierarchy gives only modest gains over a flat one, so preserving recoverable evidence matters more than adding abstraction or structure per se. M2 (extraction): coarser, less selective extraction (MemoChat's heuristic topic segmentation over LLM-driven segmentation; MemOS's "Fast Memorize" over "Fine Memorize," which swings LoCoMo EM from 2.5 to 25.5; LightMem extracting both user and assistant turns rather than user-only) consistently preserves more downstream answerability than aggressive, selective filtering. M3 (retrieval): A-MEM's balanced dense-sparse fusion beats a sparse-leaning variant, and SimpleMem's explicit query-planning step improves over no planning, but adding a further reflection stage on top of planning does not help and may hurt. M4 (maintenance): a stricter, more conservative consolidation threshold in MemoryOS modestly outperforms both delayed flushing (which leaves more evidence unresolved at query time) and MemoChat's forced single-topic summarization (which over-compresses), while plain, unconsolidated Long Context still wins on strict substring exact match by simply preserving raw phrasing.

The paper closes by synthesizing nine numbered findings into a set of practical, workload-conditioned design guidelines (matching representation to workload bottleneck, favoring coverage-preserving extraction, preferring moderate hybrid fusion and lightweight planning over deeper reflection in retrieval, and preferring conservative/localized consolidation over both aggressive compaction and delayed batch reorganization in maintenance) and states the intent to release the evaluation testbed and taxonomy repository publicly.

## Learnings

The specific finding that Recall@1 and Recall@10 identify different "winning" systems (SimpleMem tops early-hit precision; A-MEM and MemTree pull ahead at larger budgets and stay far more stable as the query-evidence temporal gap widens) is a transferable methodological point for anyone benchmarking any retrieval system, not just agent memory specifically: top-1 ranking accuracy and evidence-completeness under a realistic retrieval budget are genuinely different properties that can favor different architectures, and a single Recall@1 or nDCG-style number can hide which of these properties a system actually has.

The extraction ablations (M2) converge on a specific, somewhat counterintuitive lesson worth generalizing beyond memory systems: more aggressive, "cleaner" write-time filtering (fine-grained LLM-based fact extraction, single-topic-forced summarization, user-only-turn extraction) can look appealing as a design choice but measurably destroys the raw context needed for later multi-hop or compositional reasoning, sometimes catastrophically (MemOS's Fast-vs-Fine-Memorize swing from 25.5 to 2.5 EM on the same benchmark); the general lesson, that early, irreversible compression trades away information a downstream reasoning step cannot recover, generalizes to any pipeline stage that summarizes or filters before knowing what future queries will need.

The operational-cost finding that efficiency tracks the scope of what each write touches, rather than whether a system uses "richer" structure at all (LightMem and MemTree's path-local, bounded updates stay cheap; Cognee's, Zep's, and MemoryOS's whole-graph or multi-store consolidation gets expensive as memory grows, sometimes without proportionally better accuracy) is a specific, actionable systems-design principle: the cost driver in a stateful, continuously-updated system is less "how structured is the representation" than "how much of the existing structure does a single new write have to touch or recompute."

## Verification

The paper's core methodological strength is genuinely decomposing what most agent-memory papers report only as a single end-to-end score into four separately-ablatable modules, and following through with controlled, single-module-at-a-time variant experiments (Section 5) rather than only reporting whole-system comparisons; this lets several specific causal claims (e.g., that MemOS's extraction mode alone swings LoCoMo EM by 23 points, or that raw-text retention alone matters more than added hierarchy) be attributed to a specific mechanism rather than to an unspecified combination of a system's many design choices. The unified time-overhead runner used for the cost analysis (RQ5) is a genuine attempt at fair cross-system latency comparison, which most task-accuracy-only agent-memory benchmarks omit entirely, and the four-backbone robustness ablation (RQ3, Figure 9) is a real generalization check beyond a single LLM.

Two gaps are worth naming. First, the paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple-seed results for any of its many reported numbers (Tables 2-5, Figures 7-12 all present single point estimates), so a reader cannot judge which of the paper's many close margins (e.g., MemOS's 8.9 vs. the next-best system's score on LoCoMo EM in Table 2, or MemoryOS's 23.2-to-23.5 "Conservative-Merge" gain in Figure 12) reflect a stable, reproducible effect versus noise in a single evaluation run; this matters more here than in a single-headline-number paper precisely because the paper's central contribution is fine-grained comparison across many close-scoring systems. Second, two of the eight authors are also original co-authors of MemOS, one of the twelve evaluated systems, and no blinding, independent-evaluator, or pre-registration protocol is described to mitigate the resulting conflict of interest; this does not by itself invalidate the reported MemOS results, which are checked against real benchmark ground truth (LoCoMo, LongMemEval, etc.) rather than against a self-graded metric, but the paper does not disclose any specific safeguard (e.g., a blinded run, or exclusion of MemOS from headline framing) that would let a reader rule out the possibility that implementation effort or configuration tuning was not applied evenly across all twelve systems.

## Field context

The paper's own related-work framing positions it as filling a specific gap relative to prior agent-memory benchmarks (LoCoMo, LongMemEval, MemBench) and a recent database-community effort it cites as narrower in scope (limited to LoCoMo and LongMemEval alone): by adding a four-module decomposition, fine-grained single-module ablations, and explicit operational-cost profiling across a much broader set of twelve systems and eleven datasets. Within this corpus, the closest connection is [reviews/2606.06036.md](2606.06036.md) (MRAgent, "Memory is Reconstructed, Not Retrieved"), reviewed earlier: MRAgent's own critique of A-Mem and Zep as "passive" retrieval systems that perform a fixed-hop graph expansion rather than adaptively steering exploration based on what has already been found, and its finding that active, multi-step reasoning is specifically most valuable for multi-hop and temporal queries, together provide a mechanistic explanation that is directly consistent with, and helps explain, this paper's own RQ2 finding that retrieval fidelity depends on evidence-completeness at larger budgets rather than on top-1 ranking, and its RQ1 finding that structure-aware and hybrid-filtered systems retain an edge specifically on temporally scattered or multi-hop evidence. A second, more specific connection is [reviews/2606.13681.md](2606.13681.md) (EvoArena), also reviewed earlier: EvoArena's EvoMem design, which keeps a git-like versioned history of memory updates rather than only the latest state so an agent can inspect why something changed, is a concrete instantiation of exactly the "Timestamp-Based Multi-Versioning" maintenance category this paper's own taxonomy identifies (via Zep, Mem0^g, and MemOS) as the most reliable mechanism for handling dynamic knowledge updates in its RQ3 findings; reading the two together, EvoArena provides an independently-motivated benchmark-side validation that this specific maintenance strategy matters, evaluated on a different set of evolving-task benchmarks than this paper's own LongMemEval/LoCoMo update slices.

## Critical discussion

The paper's controlled, single-module ablation methodology (Section 5) is a genuine methodological advance over black-box, whole-system agent-memory comparisons, and several of its specific causal claims (raw-text retention beating summarization for LightMem; coarse extraction beating fine-grained extraction for MemOS; conservative consolidation beating both delayed flushing and over-aggressive summarization for MemoryOS) are supported by isolating exactly one changed module while holding the rest of the pipeline fixed, which is a meaningfully stronger form of evidence than comparing different whole systems that differ in many respects simultaneously.

The most significant limitation is the undisclosed conflict of interest around MemOS: two of the paper's eight authors are original co-authors of the MemOS paper, one of the twelve systems under evaluation, and MemOS is repeatedly highlighted among the strongest or "closest to frontier" systems across RQ1, RQ3, and the backbone-robustness ablation. This does not mean the reported MemOS results are wrong, since they are measured against the same real benchmark ground truth as every other system, and MemOS's strong showing is not universal (it is not the leader on RQ2 retrieval fidelity or RQ5 cost-efficiency, where LightMem and MemTree lead instead); but the paper discloses no specific safeguard, such as blinded configuration tuning, an independent third-party implementation check, or pre-registered evaluation protocol, that would let a reader rule out uneven implementation effort or hyperparameter tuning across the twelve systems, which matters more here than in a typical single-system paper precisely because several of the paper's headline comparisons involve close margins between systems. Separately, the complete absence of confidence intervals, variance, or multi-seed reporting anywhere in the paper (Tables 2-5, Figures 7-12) means several of the specific numeric contrasts the paper's findings are built on (e.g., 23.2 vs. 23.5 for MemoryOS's Conservative-Merge ablation) cannot be distinguished from noise using the evidence presented, even though the paper's broader qualitative patterns (structure helping at longer temporal distances; coarse extraction beating fine-grained extraction; localized maintenance being cheaper than global reorganization) are generally large enough in magnitude to be more robust to this gap than any single close comparison would be.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the paper's central, repeated qualitative pattern, that no single memory architecture dominates across workloads, and that effectiveness depends on matching representation and retrieval strategy to the specific bottleneck a workload exercises (evidence completeness, temporal reasoning, procedural state-tracking), is supported by a genuinely broad evaluation spanning five distinct workload types rather than a single benchmark; the fine-grained ablation findings, being isolated single-module comparisons rather than whole-system contrasts, are more directly attributable to the specific mechanism each identifies; and the operational-cost analysis (LightMem/MemTree's cost-efficiency frontier versus the much higher latency of globally-consolidating systems) is a distinct, practically important axis that most prior agent-memory evaluations omit entirely.
