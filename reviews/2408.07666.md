Citation: Yang, E., Shen, L., Guo, G., Wang, X., Cao, X., Zhang, J., Tao, D. Model Merging in LLMs, MLLMs, and Beyond: Methods, Theories, Applications and Opportunities. arXiv:2408.07666, 2024 (v5: 2025-12-31).

## What this paper is about

Model merging is the practice of directly combining the parameters of several independently trained models into a single model, no retraining, no access to the original training data, and often no extra inference cost, to inherit some combination of the source models' individual capabilities. This survey provides a comprehensive map of that rapidly growing area: a taxonomy of merging methods split into techniques that prepare models to be merged well and techniques that actually perform the merge, a review of what theoretical explanations exist for why merging works at all, the survey's own original benchmark comparison across five model backbones, and a wide-ranging catalogue of where model merging has been applied, from aligning and detoxifying large language models to continual learning, federated learning, and even as both an attack vector and a defense mechanism.

## Extended summary

The survey opens by placing model merging among four related paradigms (single-task learning, multi-task learning, federated learning, ensemble learning), arguing model merging uniquely combines low storage cost (a single resulting model, not one per task or one per ensemble member), no need for original training data, and no retraining, at the cost of the interference and conflict problems that arise from directly combining parameters trained for different purposes. Its taxonomy (Section 2) splits methods into "pre-merging" methods, which prepare models to merge well before the merge happens, and "during-merging" methods, which perform the actual combination. Pre-merging methods include merger-friendly fine-tuning (training in ways specifically designed to reduce later merge conflicts, such as linearized fine-tuning to achieve "weight disentanglement," where different weight-space directions correspond to functionally disjoint regions of input space, or sharpness-aware and subspace fine-tuning to produce flatter, less conflict-prone task-specific optima), architecture transformation (converting heterogeneous model architectures into a shared one, typically via knowledge distillation, so they can be merged parameter-wise at all), and weight re-basin or alignment (exploiting the linear mode connectivity property of neural networks, that independently-trained models starting from the same pretrained checkpoint often sit in loss-landscape basins connected by a near-flat path, by permuting one model's neurons to align with another's before merging, since aligned models interfere with each other much less than unaligned ones).

During-merging methods are organized into six families. Basic methods (simple parameter averaging, and Task Arithmetic's influential "task vector" framing, where a fine-tuned model's parameters minus the pretrained model's parameters is treated as a directional, addable/subtractable/combinable unit of capability, enabling multi-task learning by addition, targeted forgetting by subtraction, and even task analogies by vector arithmetic) are the simplest but, the survey notes, often unsatisfactory once tasks meaningfully interfere with each other. Weighted-based methods design rules (evolutionary search, gradient-based optimization, Fisher-information-weighted averaging, closed-form solutions for linear layers) to find better-than-uniform combination coefficients, at coarser (whole-model) or finer (per-layer, per-parameter) granularity, generally trading extra data or compute for better retained performance. Subspace-based methods project models into sparse (keeping only the highest-magnitude parameters, trimming sign conflicts) or low-rank (SVD-based) subspaces before merging, specifically to discard the components most likely to cause cross-task interference. Optimization-based methods construct data-free objectives from properties of the task vectors themselves (for instance, explicitly optimizing for greater mutual orthogonality between task vectors, which several cited theoretical results connect directly to reduced interference). Routing-based methods depart from all of the above by merging dynamically, at inference time, conditioned on the specific input or task, trading some additional latency and memory for a merged model that can better track per-sample differences than a single static merge ever could. A final, smaller category of post-calibration methods corrects a merged model after the fact, specifically to address "representation bias" (a documented gap between what individual expert models and the naively-merged model internally represent), via a technique called "representation surgery" and several direct follow-ups.

Section 3 reviews theoretical explanations for why merging works, organized by what kind of relationship holds between the models being merged: single-trajectory averaging (stochastic weight averaging or exponential moving averages within one training run, explained via bias-variance decomposition and stability bounds), same-task merging (multiple fine-tunes of the same task/dataset, explained via linear mode connectivity and flat-minima arguments), and cross-task merging (models fine-tuned on genuinely different tasks, explained via neural-tangent-kernel-based "weight disentanglement" theory linking task-vector near-orthogonality to low interference, and via a direct connection between task vectors and accumulated task gradients). The survey's own assessment of this theoretical landscape is notably candid: it states plainly that a unified theory has not yet emerged, that most theoretical guarantees assume shared initialization or shared tasks (limiting their relevance to the heterogeneous, cross-task, cross-modality settings the paper's application sections describe), and that the flatness-generalization connection, while plausible, still lacks a rigorous, direct link specifically to mergeability.

Section 3.2 presents the survey's own original empirical contribution: a benchmark comparison of representative merging methods across five backbones (ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14 on 8 visual classification tasks, RoBERTa-Large on 8 GLUE tasks, Llama-3.1-8B on 5 text-generation tasks following MergeBench, and InternVL-2.5 on 5 multimodal tasks following OptMerge). Across all five settings, the consistent pattern is that pretrained (unmerged) models sit at the low end, independently fine-tuned per-task models sit at the high end as an upper bound, and every merging method falls somewhere in between; naive weight averaging performs noticeably worse than more sophisticated approaches; and the optimization-based WUDI-Merging method consistently ranks among the best performers across every backbone tested, including outperforming more complex subspace and dynamic-routing methods in several settings. For the large-language-model backbone specifically, the survey reports a notable and somewhat counter-intuitive finding: more sophisticated merging strategies (subspace-based or elaborately weighted methods) sometimes underperform the much simpler Task Arithmetic baseline, which the authors attribute to two plausible causes, discarding genuinely important (not just conflicting) parameters when pruning to a sparse or low-rank subspace, and unreliable parameter-importance estimation from the limited calibration samples typically available for LLM merging.

Sections 4 and 5 catalogue applications across foundation models and more than ten machine-learning subfields. In large language models, merging is used for human-preference alignment (adding a small-data-aligned model's task vector to an unaligned model, reported to approximate performance of full-data alignment; merging reward models with domain-specific fine-tunes to build domain-private reward models; interpolating gradients during RLHF specifically to prevent forgetting general knowledge while still optimizing for reward), for detoxification (negating a task vector derived from fine-tuning on toxic data, shown by the original Task Arithmetic paper to reduce toxic-output rates with minimal fluency loss on a control task), for knowledge unlearning (subtracting a task vector fine-tuned on the specific content to be forgotten, presented as a cheaper and less catastrophically-forgetful alternative to gradient-ascent-based unlearning, directly motivated by data-privacy regulation and the prohibitive cost of retraining a model like Llama-2-70B from scratch), for faster training (merging intermediate training checkpoints, reported to save meaningful compute, e.g., 30 GPU-hours on a RoBERTa-Base/WikiText-103 run, and to serve as an alternative to learning-rate decay schedules during pretraining), for faster mixture-of-experts inference (merging multiple experts' parameters before computing outputs rather than aggregating all experts' outputs, to capture more-experts' benefit without proportionally more compute), for faster reasoning specifically (merging "slow-thinking," long-chain-of-thought reasoning models with faster conventional models, reported to cut inference token consumption by roughly 50% on DeepSeek-R1-7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B while preserving comparable accuracy, directly motivated by documented cases of extreme token overuse, e.g., a cited example of one reasoning model using 901 tokens to answer "2+3=?" versus 5-7 tokens for conventional models), and for combining multiple domain-expert LLMs into one composite model. In multimodal large language models, merging is used to fuse modality-specific models into a single modality-agnostic model (avoiding the cost of collecting paired multimodal training data) and for cross-modal knowledge transfer from high-resource to low-resource modalities (one cited result reports an 18.42% classification-error reduction on an audio task via attention-layer merging from a text/image-trained model). In visual generative models, merging enables style-mixing (combining independently-trained style-specific diffusion or GAN experts, with one cited result finding merged diffusion models also show a measurable anti-memorization effect, generating outputs less likely to replicate specific training examples) and reduced training cost via merging intermediate diffusion-model training checkpoints (one cited method reports 23x and 7x training speedups for two specific training objectives). Across continual learning, multi-task/multi-domain/multi-objective/auxiliary learning, out-of-distribution and domain generalization, federated learning, zero-/few-shot learning, and adversarial learning, the recurring theme is that merging offers a way to combine or transfer knowledge across models without the data-collection, storage, or retraining costs of the traditional approach to each of these problems, while the adversarial-learning subsection specifically documents merging's dual-use nature: it can propagate a "poisoned" LoRA module's backdoor into an otherwise-clean model with reported success rates exceeding 90% for one dedicated attack framework, but a specifically-trained defensive module merged into an already-poisoned model can also measurably reduce that same backdoor's effect, and merging complicates intellectual-property enforcement since the survey reports one specific finding that a tested watermarking scheme becomes undetectable after merging while a tested fingerprinting scheme remains detectable.

The survey closes with seven explicitly-named open challenges: the persistent, often large performance gap between merged and independently-trained models (quantified directly from the survey's own Section 3.2 results, e.g., an 8-task ViT-B/32 gap of 90.3 for independent models versus 66.5 for naive averaging); the shortage of theoretical grounding, particularly for merging models trained on genuinely different tasks or from different initializations; the need for "trustworthy" merging that can both protect model owners' intellectual property and defend combiners against malicious merged-in content; the high memory and compute cost of many current high-performing methods (one cited example: merging 72 fine-tuned ViT-B/32 models requires over 200GB of memory); the difficulty of merging architecturally heterogeneous models without expensive distillation-based conversion; the comparative neglect of designing fine-tuning procedures specifically to produce more mergeable models, rather than only designing better merging algorithms after the fact; and the open question of transferring merging techniques developed in one subfield to others not yet explored.

## Learnings

The survey's most broadly transferable conceptual contribution is the "task vector" framing itself (a fine-tuned model's parameters minus its pretrained starting point, treated as a directional, arithmetically-combinable unit of capability): this reframes a wide range of otherwise-separate problems, multi-task learning, targeted unlearning, detoxification, style mixing, cross-lingual transfer, as variations on the same simple operation (add, subtract, or interpolate vectors in weight space), which is a genuinely reusable mental model for approaching a new problem, ask whether the desired behavior change can be expressed as adding or removing a specific, isolatable task vector, before reaching for a more expensive retraining-based solution.

A second, more cautionary learning, directly evidenced by the survey's own original benchmark rather than only asserted, is that architectural sophistication in a merging method does not reliably track with better results, especially for large language models specifically: subspace-based and elaborately-weighted merging methods sometimes underperform the much simpler Task Arithmetic baseline on the Llama-3.1 benchmark tested, with the survey offering two specific, plausible mechanistic explanations (discarding genuinely important, not just conflicting, parameters when pruning to a subspace, and unreliable importance estimation from the small calibration sample typically available). This is a concrete, benchmark-grounded caution against assuming a newer or more mathematically elaborate merging technique will automatically outperform a simpler established baseline in a new setting, worth checking empirically rather than assumed from a method's sophistication alone.

A third, more strategic learning concerns where research effort in this area has and has not concentrated: the survey's explicit list of open challenges names "merger-friendly fine-tuning" (designing the fine-tuning stage itself, not just the merge algorithm, to produce more easily-combined models) as comparatively under-explored relative to the much larger literature on merge-time algorithms, and separately names the almost complete absence of theoretical treatment for merging models with different initializations or trained from scratch on different tasks (as opposed to fine-tunes of a shared checkpoint) as a genuine gap rather than a solved-but-unpublicized problem. Both are specific, actionable directions for anyone deciding where a marginal unit of research effort in this space would fill a real gap rather than adding another entry to an already crowded category.

## Verification

As a survey with an embedded original empirical contribution, this paper's verification standard operates on two levels. For the literature review portions (Sections 2, 4, 5), verification consists of accurately representing what each cited paper claims and, where the survey itself synthesizes across papers (as in the taxonomy tables and the "Summary and Discussion" subsections closing Sections 2 and 3), stating clearly which conclusions are the survey's own synthesis versus a specific cited result; the survey is reasonably disciplined about this, generally attributing specific numerical claims (e.g., the GSM8K score jump from 2.2 to 66.3 after merging WizardLM with WizardMath, or the 18.42% audio-classification error reduction from cross-modal attention merging) to the specific paper reporting them rather than presenting them as generally-established facts. For its own original contribution (Section 3.2's five-backbone benchmark comparison), the survey follows established third-party evaluation protocols where available (FusionBench for vision models, MergeBench for LLMs, OptMerge for MLLMs) rather than inventing a new evaluation setup, which is a reasonable choice for comparability with the broader literature the survey is reviewing. However, the reported results in Figure 6 appear to be single-run point estimates for each method-backbone combination, with no reported variance, confidence interval, or repeated-run comparison, which matters because several of the closer method-to-method comparisons the survey highlights (for instance, the LLM-backbone finding that some sophisticated methods underperform basic Task Arithmetic) rest on point-estimate differences whose robustness to run-to-run variation cannot be assessed from anything reported in the survey itself. The survey's own theoretical section (3.1) models good verification practice for a synthesis piece by explicitly naming the scope limits of the theory it reviews (shared-initialization or shared-task assumptions, wide-network/linearization assumptions for the NTK-based results) rather than presenting the cited theoretical results as settled explanations that generalize to the heterogeneous, cross-task, cross-modality applications catalogued later in the same paper.

## Field context

This survey positions itself explicitly against a small number of existing model-merging resources it argues are each narrower in scope, MergeKit and FusionBench (each discussed as benchmark-focused works covering only seven or eight representative methods), and a prior "learning from models" survey that the paper states covers model merging only in a single page as a subsection of a broader topic, framing its own contribution as the first genuinely comprehensive treatment spanning methods, theory, and applications across foundation models and more than ten machine-learning subfields simultaneously. Within its own literature, it builds its taxonomy directly on Task Arithmetic's foundational task-vector framing and the linear-mode-connectivity theoretical tradition (Git Re-basin and related weight-alignment work), treating these as the two central organizing ideas the rest of the field's methods elaborate on or depart from. Within this corpus, the survey's "Faster Reasoning of LLMs" application (merging long-chain-of-thought reasoning models with faster conventional models specifically to reduce token consumption while preserving accuracy) is a direct, mechanistically distinct answer to the same underlying problem diagnosed empirically in [2310.03716] (reviewed earlier in this batch), which showed that RLHF-trained models often produce excessively long outputs substantially because reward models latch onto length as an easy, shallow signal rather than genuine quality; where that paper's proposed fixes operate on the reward-modeling and PPO-training pipeline itself, this survey's cited long-to-short merging approach offers a training-free, post-hoc alternative (directly combine an already-verbose reasoning model's parameters with a concise model's parameters) to the same practical symptom, output length divorced from necessity, via an entirely different mechanism. The survey's "Human Preference Alignment for LLMs" subsection, discussing merging reward-aligned models with SFT models (ExPO, an Online Merging Optimizer that interpolates gradients during RLHF specifically to prevent catastrophic forgetting of general knowledge) as an efficient alternative or complement to full RLHF training, is also directly relevant background for [2204.05862] (Anthropic's foundational RLHF paper, reviewed in a previous batch), whose own central concern, that RLHF training can degrade general capabilities in smaller models (the "alignment tax"), is exactly the kind of forgetting-during-alignment problem several of the merging-based approaches surveyed here are specifically designed to mitigate.

## Critical discussion

As a survey, this paper's core value proposition is coverage and organization rather than a single falsifiable claim, and on that standard it performs well: the two-phase (pre-merging/during-merging) taxonomy is a genuinely useful organizing structure that most of the dozens of individual methods discussed map cleanly onto, the theoretical section is honest about the field's current lack of a unified framework rather than overselling the maturity of existing theory, and the survey's decision to conduct and report its own original five-backbone benchmark comparison (Section 3.2), rather than relying entirely on other papers' potentially incommensurable reported numbers, is a real methodological strength that most literature surveys do not attempt.

The clearest specific limitation is that the original benchmark results underlying several of the survey's more pointed comparative claims (particularly the LLM-backbone finding that sophisticated methods can underperform basic Task Arithmetic) are reported as apparent single-run point estimates without variance, so a reader cannot currently distinguish a robust method-ranking difference from noise using anything in the survey itself, a gap worth flagging especially because the survey draws a specific mechanistic conclusion (parameter pruning discarding important information, or unreliable importance estimation from small calibration samples) from what may be a single comparison per method. A second, more structural observation concerns the "Post-calibration-based Merging Methods" category (Section 2.3.6): unlike the other five during-merging categories, each of which synthesizes a genuinely broad, independently-authored literature, this category is built substantially around one specific technique, "representation surgery," and its direct sequels, work whose lead-author surname matches this survey's own first author; if this is in fact a self-citation (which this review cannot independently confirm from the text alone), it would be worth noting as a place where the taxonomy's structure may reflect the authors' own research emphasis somewhat more than the surrounding literature's actual relative maturity, though the technique itself is presented accurately and the broader taxonomy elsewhere draws on a genuinely wide, non-self-referential set of sources. A third, more general point, one inherent to the survey's own chosen format rather than a flaw in execution, is that this is an actively and repeatedly revised "living" document (five versions over sixteen months, with the most recent revision made only months before this review), meaning any snapshot reading of it, including this one, will necessarily miss whatever the field produces after that snapshot's date, a limitation the survey's own existence as a continuously-updated companion to a maintained GitHub reading list is a reasonable, if partial, response to.

What remains well supported: model merging is a genuinely distinct and increasingly consequential paradigm compared to multi-task learning, federated learning, and ensembling, with real, demonstrated tradeoffs (low storage and no retraining cost, versus a persistent performance gap relative to independently-trained experts) rather than a strictly dominant technique; the survey's own conducted benchmark confirms, across five different model backbones and modalities, that more sophisticated merging methods (particularly optimization-based approaches) generally, though not universally, outperform naive parameter averaging, while every tested method still falls short of independently fine-tuned models; and the field's application breadth, spanning alignment, detoxification, unlearning, efficiency, continual learning, and both attack and defense scenarios, is real and substantiated by the specific, individually-cited results the survey compiles, even where the underlying theoretical explanation for why merging works in each of these settings remains, by the survey's own explicit account, incomplete.
