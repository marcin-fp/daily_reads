Citation: Mikhail Burtsev. Recursive Criticality of AI Self-Improvement. arXiv preprint arXiv:2609.00137 (v1), 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00137

## What this paper is about

AI systems now do part of the work of building the next AI systems. This paper asks when that feedback turns from ordinary help into a self-amplifying loop. It writes a small delay-differential equation for "AI capability" in which progress depends on a baseline research rate, a delayed boost from current capability, the fraction of that boost that survives the development pipeline, and a research frontier that gets harder as it is approached. Linearizing around a trajectory gives a single dimensionless number, the recursive reproduction number R_AI (realized recursive gain divided by the rate at which the frontier hardens). Small improvements amplify across development cycles when R_AI > 1 and die out when R_AI < 1, much like the basic reproduction number of an epidemic.

The author draws several qualitative conclusions from the model. More compute or effort speeds progress without changing which side of the threshold the system is on. The development-cycle delay caps how fast amplification can run. A finite research frontier eventually pushes any supercritical episode back below threshold. And a network of research actors can be supercritical as a whole when every actor is subcritical on its own. Illustrative simulations show how these regimes change the time between AGI and ASI thresholds on an arbitrary capability scale. The author states explicitly that these are conditional demonstrations, not forecasts.

## Extended summary

**Framing and variables.** Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is treated as a property of an AI and R&D system (models, human researchers, labs, compute, evaluation, deployment), not of an isolated autonomous agent. Capability x(t) is defined (Eq. 1) as a weighted average, over a fixed panel of tasks, of the log reduction in minimum resource cost to complete each task at a tolerated loss, at fixed resource prices, relative to time t0. The author calls it a local coordinate for research capability, not a universal measure of intelligence. The model separates four ingredients:

- r(t), an exogenous baseline research-productivity scale;
- a(x,t), the recursive gain from capability to future research productivity under unconstrained use, returning after an end-to-end delay tau;
- the operational closure chi(x,t) in [0,1], the fraction of potential gain that survives evaluation, integration, training, and deployment into a validated successor, so the realized gain is g = chi a;
- f(x), the productivity of direct improvement, with frontier hardening sigma(x) = -d ln f / dx.

Closure is described as a property of the process, not of autonomy. A system with heavy human participation can have high closure if AI contributions are evaluated and integrated efficiently. A highly autonomous workflow can have low closure if its outputs fail evaluation, cannot be run safely, or never propagate. Human review, experimental capacity, and secure execution can bind as throughput rises.

**Model and threshold.** The dynamics are dx/dt = r(t) f[x(t)] exp(Phi(x[t - tau], t)), with dPhi/dx = g (Eq. 3 and 4). Linearizing around a reference trajectory with frozen coefficients (speed v, gain g, hardness sigma, delay tau) gives d xi/dt = -v sigma xi(t) + v g xi(t - tau), with characteristic equation lambda + v sigma = v g exp(-lambda tau). Defining R_AI = g / sigma = chi a / sigma (Eq. 8), Proposition 1 states that all roots have negative real part for R_AI < 1, lambda = 0 is a root at R_AI = 1, and a positive real root exists for R_AI > 1. The author presents this as a scalar case of stability theory for positive delay systems and notes that Cunningham et al. independently derived a related unit threshold from economic elasticities. Because v multiplies both the damping and feedback terms, the threshold does not depend on baseline throughput in the minimal model. Near threshold the dominant root is approximately v sigma (R_AI - 1) / (1 + v sigma R_AI tau). It passes smoothly through zero, so a newly supercritical system can look like ordinary acceleration. As v sigma tau grows without bound the root tends to ln(R_AI) / tau, so the successor-development cycle caps the amplification rate. The author concludes that rapid capability growth is neither necessary nor sufficient evidence of recursive criticality, and that diagnosis needs estimates of recursive gain and frontier hardening across cycles rather than a benchmark slope. R_AI > 1 is stated to be local and not to imply indefinite growth.

**Finite frontier.** With f(x) = (1 - x/X)^beta, sigma = beta / (X - x). Proposition 2: if x approaches a finite X, sigma diverges, and g stays bounded, then R_AI tends to 0, so the system is subcritical near the frontier. Supercriticality can therefore be transient within a research paradigm. Architectural or scientific advances can move X, change beta, or raise g, and the author says this should not be read as a claim about a final ceiling.

**Coupled actors.** For n actors, d xi_i/dt = -v_i sigma_i xi_i + v_i sum_j g_ij xi_j(t - tau_ij) (Eq. 11). Row-normalizing by the receiving actor's hardness gives the reproduction matrix K_ij = g_ij / sigma_i, with network number R_net = rho(K), the spectral radius. Proposition 3, stated for the zero-delay system, gives stability for rho(K) < 1 and instability for rho(K) > 1, so K_ii < 1 for every actor does not guarantee network stability when cross-actor transfer is strong.

**Reference scenarios.** Current capability is normalized to x0 = 0 and the paradigm's frontier to X = 1. The AGI and ASI thresholds are placed by assumption at x = 0.50 and 0.80, and beta = 2 is chosen as a structural assumption. Without recursion, x(t) = r t / (1 + r t), so T_AGI = 1/r and T_ASI = 4/r. The rate r_ref = 1/24 per year places the no-RSI AGI crossing at 2050, with t = 0 taken as 2026. This is motivated by the 2023 Expert Survey on Progress in AI (2,778 authors, aggregate 50% date of 2047 for high-level machine intelligence) and the Longitudinal Expert AI Panel (205 experts, conditional median 2050, quartiles 2039 and 2065). The surveys are used "only to set an illustrative timescale" because their definitions differ and their uncertainty is large. The recursive part fixes tau = 0.5 year, and closure is a logistic chi(x) = 1 / (1 + exp(-k x)) with k = 10, so chi(0) = 0.5. This gives R_AI(x) = a chi(x)(1 - x) / beta, and a peak value R_peak = max over x of R_AI. Only a varies across four scenarios: smooth scaling (a = 0.5, subcritical throughout), weak supercritical (a = 3, only modestly above the peak boundary), transient takeoff (a = 6), and rapid transition (a = 15). Fig. 2 maps R_peak over (beta, a) at k = 10 and over (beta, k) at a = 3. The maps could not be assessed from the text.

Table 1 (years from 2026):

| Scenario | a | T_AGI | T_ASI | AGI to ASI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No-RSI baseline | 0 | 24.00 | 96.00 | 72.00 |
| Smooth scaling | 0.5 | 21.35 | 74.13 | 52.79 |
| Weak supercriticality | 3 | 12.90 | 24.73 | 11.83 |
| Transient takeoff | 6 | 8.40 | 11.08 | 2.69 |
| Rapid AGI-to-ASI | 15 | 4.50 | 4.95 | 0.45 |

The author reads these as follows. Subcritical feedback still advances AGI by about 2.7 years and ASI by about 22. In the weak supercritical case, R_AI rises modestly above 1 and falls back well before AGI, yet "the temporary period of amplification leaves the system on a persistently advanced capability trajectory". In the rapid case both thresholds fall inside one amplification episode, and R_AI then collapses as the fixed frontier nears. Effects grow for thresholds farther from the start because feedback compounds over the capability interval. An outward-moving or jumping frontier (X to X + delta X) raises R_AI immediately and can produce several separate recursive episodes. Caveats as stated: r_ref and tau are only plausible choices, beta, a, and k "are not presently constrained by direct empirical estimates" and were picked to span regimes, and all reported periods are "conditional outputs of the stated parameterization".

**Strategic scenarios.** Starting from the weak supercritical trajectory, three illustrative three-actor networks each set an effort multiplier m_r, a delay tau, and a matrix K:

- Closed labs: within-actor values 1.00, 0.90, 0.70, cross-transfer 0.02 on all links, rho(K) = 1.01, m_r = 1.0, tau = 0.30 year.
- Open ecosystem: within-actor values 0.65, 0.55, 0.45, cross-transfer 0.50 on all links, rho(K) = 1.55, m_r = 0.8, tau = 0.50 year.
- Global competition: within-bloc values 0.90, 0.85, 0.62, asymmetric transfers between about 0.03 and 0.40, rho(K) about 1.15, m_r = 1.2, tau = 1.0 year.

The off-diagonal values come from figure text. For the global case, the assignment of transfer values to specific links cannot be recovered. Table 2 gives first-crossing times among actors:

| Configuration | T_AGI | T_ASI | AGI to ASI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Closed labs | 10.40 | 16.53 | 6.13 |
| Open ecosystem | 9.75 | 12.58 | 2.82 |
| Global competition | 8.97 | 13.42 | 4.45 |

The author concludes that network coupling gives the open ecosystem the shortest AGI-to-ASI interval despite lower effort. Global competition reaches AGI first, but its interval stays longer than the open ecosystem's despite greater effort. Research-network structure is part of the dynamical state, and "aggregate investment provides information about the speed of progress, but it does not determine proximity to recursive criticality". The configurations are said to separate mechanisms, not to describe real institutions.

**Hardware and power.** Deployability requires C_req(x) <= C_phys(t), with C_req(x) = C_req(x_AGI) exp(kappa (x - x_AGI)). Physical headroom is F = C_phys(T_AGI unconstrained) / C_req(x_AGI), and Q = C_req(x_ASI) / C_req(x_AGI). With the assumptions Q = 100 and physical compute growing at g_phys = 0.25 per year: F = 8 keeps infrastructure ahead; F = 2 binds between AGI and ASI and lengthens the transition; F = 0.75 binds before the unconstrained AGI crossing and delays both. The author treats physical compute here only as a capacity limit that does not directly change R_AI, noting that compute can also raise closure, raise gain, or shorten cycles, which is not modeled.

**Conclusion and measurement agenda.** The quantities to measure are named: the causal effect of AI research capability on later R&D productivity, the fraction of gains that reach successor systems, cycle duration, resource-normalized research productivity, frontier hardening, and cross-actor transfer. The more informative signal is "whether each increment of AI research capability is becoming increasingly effective at producing the next one". No empirical estimation is attempted.

## Learnings

If the baseline research rate multiplies both the damping from a hardening frontier and the recursive boost, then the threshold for self-amplification depends only on their ratio, and more money or compute changes speed but not regime. This separation between pace and regime is a clean way to think about any feedback loop. It is a consequence of the separable, multiplicative form, however, and disappears as soon as compute also raises closure or gain, which the paper itself concedes.

With a fixed feedback delay, the amplification rate of a supercritical loop saturates at ln(R_AI) / tau however much throughput is added. Once throughput is high, shortening the development cycle matters more than adding capacity.

The network result is the epidemiological next-generation matrix applied to research actors: the spectral radius of the within-actor plus cross-actor transfer matrix, not the largest individual loop, decides stability. Openness can push an ecosystem of individually subcritical actors over threshold.

In the finite-frontier model, whether a simulated trajectory is "supercritical" says surprisingly little about outcomes. By our reconstruction, crossing times change smoothly with recursive gain straight through the R_AI = 1 boundary, and a loop just below threshold ends up almost as far ahead as one just above it (see Critical discussion). How strong the feedback is matters more than which side of 1 the local number sits on.

## Verification

The paper has no verification method of its own. Its propositions are analytic and its scenarios are deterministic simulations, with code released for reproduction. Verification enters as a satellite idea through operational closure: only the part of AI-generated research that passes evaluation, can be executed safely, and propagates into a validated successor counts toward recursive gain. The paper names human review, experimental capacity, and secure execution as bottlenecks that can bind as throughput rises. In this framing, evaluation capacity is a direct multiplier on the reproduction number, not a side cost. The paper does not model how closure is measured, what evaluator issues the verdict, or what happens when evaluation errs, for example when a flawed contribution passes and propagates.

## Field context

By the author's own framing, the paper sits between two literatures. One is the conceptual intelligence-explosion line (Good, Hutter, Schmidhuber's Gödel machines, Everitt et al.). The other is the economic growth models that put AI inside the production of ideas, with automation, bottlenecks, and diminishing returns (Aghion, Jones and Jones; Davidson's compute-centric takeoff framework; Besiroglu et al.; Jones; Davidson et al. on feedback loops in innovation networks). It also cites resource-constrained singularity models (Jafari et al.) and the survey by Chen et al. separating bounded self-refinement from open-ended research loops. The closest cited result is Cunningham et al., who derive a related unit threshold from recursive elasticities. The author presents his contribution as the dynamics missing from both lines: delay, frontier exhaustion, and multi-actor coupling. He cites MLE-bench, RE-Bench, PaperBench, human-calibrated task-horizon measurements (Kwa et al.), and measures of AI R&D automation as partial measurements of the loop that do not yet identify its causal gain.

Our own inference: the formal tools are standard. The reproduction number and its matrix generalization are borrowed from epidemic modeling, and the frontier-hardening term plays the role that "ideas get harder to find" plays in the cited growth models. What is new is the combination and the vocabulary (operational closure, recursive runway), not a new mathematical result.

Connections to reviewed work:

- reviews/2607.07663.md (Chen et al., cited here) argues qualitatively that every self-improvement loop is only as good as its evaluator and that RSI is limited by grounding, collapse, and compute. This paper turns the evaluator limit into a closure factor chi, research hardening into sigma, and compute into a deployability constraint. It does not model collapse or evaluator drift, since a is exogenous.
- reviews/2606.12683.md (From AGI to ASI, cited here) maps parallel pathways and bottlenecks, including "research gets harder" and compute limits, and does not rule out a fast intelligence explosion. This paper offers a minimal quantitative model consistent with that map. Within a fixed paradigm, amplification is self-limiting and paradigm shifts can restart it. That review also records effective compute growing roughly tenfold per year, which corresponds to a growing r(t) in this model, the ingredient the scenarios hold fixed.
- reviews/2401.02843.md is the 2023 expert survey used to set r_ref. That review notes a 15% response rate and the survey authors' own warning that expert forecasts are unreliable. This supports Burtsev's choice to use the survey only as a timescale.
- reviews/2609.15818.md and reviews/2607.27191.md report empirical evidence on AI inside AI R&D. Atria Dawn reports that AI was used in almost all development tasks while humans made most final method decisions. The CRUX shadow evaluations, in two case studies, found agents that could not yet complete open-ended AI research to a publishable standard. In our reading these bear on closure and on where the evaluation bottleneck sits, but neither estimates the causal effect on successor productivity that R_AI requires.
- reviews/2601.05280.md argues that self-training without an external signal has no built-in direction of improvement. Burtsev instead assumes a positive recursive gain a and does not ask what would make it positive.

## Critical discussion

The mathematics is sound for what it claims. The characteristic equation follows from Eq. 3. If R_AI < 1, any root with non-negative real part would need |lambda + v sigma| >= v sigma > v g, which rules it out, so Proposition 1 holds. The near-critical root and the ln(R_AI) / tau limit follow from a first-order expansion and a large-throughput limit. Proposition 3 is the standard Metzler-matrix stability condition. It is stated only for zero delay while the network scenarios use delays of 0.3 to 1 year. Standard results for positive delay systems, the class the paper cites, suggest the threshold does not depend on the delays, but the paper does not state or prove this. We also reimplemented the single-actor scenarios from the text. We used forward Euler, Phi(x) = a times the integral of chi from 0 to x (so Phi(0) = 0), and x = 0 before t = 0. This reproduces every entry of Table 1 to within 0.01 year. The text states neither the history function nor the normalization of Phi, but the match suggests these are the code's choices. The closed and open matrices from Fig. 4a give rho(K) of about 1.006 and 1.554, consistent with the stated 1.01 and 1.55. For the global case, the recoverable transfer values give rho(K) between about 1.03 and 1.24 depending on how they are assigned to links, so the stated 1.15 cannot be checked from the text.

The weaker part is the interpretation of R_AI in the paper's own scenarios. R_AI is a frozen-coefficient local criterion, and in the finite-frontier model the coefficients change on the same timescale as the trajectory. In our reconstruction, the weak supercritical case (a = 3) sits above threshold only between x of about 0.11 and 0.30, roughly years 2.8 to 7.7. There the dominant root is about 0.006 per year, so small perturbations grow by only about 2% over the whole supercritical window. Sweeping a across the critical value (about 2.84 for beta = 2 and k = 10) gives smooth crossing times. At a = 2.8, which is subcritical everywhere, T_AGI is 13.36 and T_ASI is 26.62 years. At a = 3 the values are 12.90 and 24.73. The paper says the weak supercritical episode "leaves the system on a persistently advanced capability trajectory". The model's own outputs do not support tying that lead to the supercritical episode: nearly all of it comes from the cumulative recursive term exp(Phi), which acts just as much below threshold. That is also why the subcritical a = 0.5 scenario advances ASI by 22 years. Section 3.3 and the abstract's emphasis on the "onset" criterion therefore overstate how much the threshold matters for timing. Amplification becomes large only well above 1: by the same estimate, perturbations grow about threefold at a = 6 and about seventeenfold at a = 15. The threshold is a correct stability boundary, not a phase transition in outcomes.

A related point narrows the diagnostic message. With constant r and Phi depending only on x, Eq. 3 gives d ln(dx/dt)/dt = -sigma dx/dt(t) + g dx/dt(t - tau), which is approximately v sigma (R_AI - 1). So within the minimal model, R_AI > 1 coincides with the capability trajectory curving upward in the paper's resource-adjusted coordinate. In our simulations the sign of the acceleration matched R_AI > 1 at 97 to 99% of time steps; the mismatches come from the delay. The paper rightly says a benchmark slope cannot reveal the regime, but curvature can, provided inputs are constant. The real identification problem is therefore separating recursive gain from growth in r(t), such as rising investment and compute. The scenarios never face this problem because r is constant and m_r is a fixed multiplier. The paper also gives no identification strategy for a, chi, sigma, or tau, and Eq. 1's fixed task panel at fixed prices is hard to keep stable across the paradigm shifts the paper invokes.

The separation of pace from regime is built into the model, not found by it. Throughput leaves the threshold unchanged because r multiplies f and exp(Phi) alike. The text usually says "in the minimal model", but the contributions list states flatly that more compute, expenditure, or effort "can accelerate capability growth without changing recursive criticality". Section 4.2 then concedes that compute can raise closure, gain, or cycle speed, which would change the regime.

Scenario inputs carry no empirical weight, and the author says so. The AGI and ASI positions (0.5 and 0.8), beta, a, and k are chosen, not estimated. The logistic centering sets chi(0) = 0.5, so R_AI(0) = a/4. The transient-takeoff and rapid scenarios are therefore already supercritical today (R_AI(0) of 1.5 and 3.75), which the text does not point out. Calibrating the no-RSI baseline to expert AGI medians may double count, since respondents presumably already expected AI-assisted research. Statements such as "advances ASI by about 91 years" are conditional arithmetic, and the paper labels them that way.

In Section 4.1 each configuration changes K, m_r, and tau at once. There is no run that varies one while holding the other two fixed. The comparisons "despite the lower baseline research intensity" and "longer than in the open ecosystem despite its greater research effort" are therefore confounded. For example, global competition has both a lower rho(K) than the open ecosystem (1.15 versus 1.55) and a longer delay (1.0 versus 0.5 year), so its longer interval cannot be assigned to weaker coupling alone. Several conclusions, such as "competition can increase the overall rate of research without producing a comparable increase in recursive coupling", are true by construction of the chosen parameters.

Reporting is also incomplete. The text never specifies the nonlinear multi-actor model behind Fig. 4b, including how K evolves with capability to give a time-dependent R_net(t). It also never says how the compute constraint enters the dynamics in Section 4.2, for example whether a capped deployable capability still feeds the recursive loop. Both are left to the released code.

What remains supported: a clear, correct linear-stability formalization of recursive AI R&D feedback. It shows that throughput and regime separate in a multiplicative model, that cycle delay caps the amplification rate, that a finite frontier ends supercritical episodes, and that network coupling can make an ecosystem supercritical when no actor is. Its list of measurable quantities is useful. It does not show where any real system sits relative to R_AI = 1. Within its own finite-frontier scenarios, the size of the recursive gain matters much more for timing than which side of the threshold the system is on.

## Relevance to us

Mostly this is not our problem. It is a macro model of AI R&D for forecasting and governance debates. One point transfers, as an analogy rather than evidence. In the model, the share of generated work that passes evaluation and integration (closure) and the cycle time multiply the recursive gain, while raw generation throughput does not change the regime. For an AI-scientist loop, that is a formal version of our working view that verification cost, not generation capability, may set which designs scale. It argues for spending on cheap, fast, reliable checks (B12) over more parallel generation. The paper assumes this structure rather than testing it, and it says nothing about physics or about how closure would be measured.
