Citation: Du, Z., Wang, R., Bai, H., Cao, Z., Zhu, X., Cheng, Y., Zheng, B., Chen, W., and Ying, H. Enabling Agents to Communicate Entirely in Latent Space. Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), 2026.

## What this paper is about

When two LLM-based agents collaborate, they normally talk to each other in natural language: one agent's rich internal state gets compressed down into a sequence of discrete tokens before the other agent can read it, discarding whatever didn't survive that compression. This paper asks what happens if agents skip the token bottleneck entirely and instead pass each other's raw internal representations (the model's last-layer hidden states, one per generated position) directly. Their method, Interlat, trains a small "communication adapter" that lets a receiving agent interpret another agent's hidden-state sequence as if it were an input, and separately trains a way to compress that hidden-state message down to as few as 8 positions without losing much of its usefulness. Across an embodied planning benchmark and a math benchmark, the authors report that agents communicating this way outperform agents communicating via trained natural-language plans, while also making communication itself dramatically faster.

## Extended summary

The architecture designates one agent as a "reasoning" sender, whose last-layer hidden states for its generated plan (a sequence H of vectors, one per token position) are collected and marked with special boundary tokens, and a second "actor" agent as receiver, which is trained to consume H directly as a sequence of input embeddings via a trainable communication adapter (a lightweight self-attention layer plus a projection) rather than decoding H into text first. The actor is trained with a composite objective: a standard task cross-entropy loss; a "conditional thought separation" term that minimizes overlap only in the sense of maximizing a weighted Jensen-Shannon divergence between the actor's output distribution when conditioned on the correct ("matched") latent message versus a latent message sampled from a different task ("mismatched"), which is meant to force the actor to actually depend on the content of H rather than ignore it; and a "plan-aligned regularization" term that anchors the actor's H-conditioned predictions to what it would predict from the corresponding language-space plan, specifically to prevent the separation objective from being gamed by shifting probability mass toward idiosyncratic tokens that increase divergence without helping task performance. Because learning to interpret a completely new, non-linguistic input channel from scratch is unstable, training uses a curriculum that starts by mixing in the corresponding token embeddings for a stochastically-chosen fraction of communication positions and gradually reduces that fraction to zero.

A separate second stage addresses the fact that full-length latent messages (potentially hundreds of positions) are expensive to transmit and score. Here, a new "reasoning" model M_φ is trained, with the previously-trained actor and its communication adapter now frozen, to autoregressively generate a much shorter latent message (K positions, K≪L) entirely in latent space, feeding its own last hidden state back as its next input without ever decoding to tokens. This compression model is trained with three losses: a task cross-entropy term evaluated through the frozen actor (so the compressed message must still drive correct behavior); an "uncertainty-weighted agreement" term that distills the frozen actor's behavior under the full-length message into its behavior under the compressed message, but only at token positions where the full-length message actually reduced the actor's predictive entropy relative to having no message at all (avoiding wasted training signal at positions where the message wasn't informative anyway); and a latent geometry alignment term that penalizes the compressed message's actor-side representation for drifting away from the full-length message's average direction, meant to prevent representational collapse under aggressive compression.

Experiments use a two-agent sender-receiver setup (explicitly chosen as "the building block of various multi-agent systems," with tool use, retrieval, and multi-round debate deliberately excluded to reduce confounds) on ALFWorld (embodied, interactive planning, capped at 20 steps per episode) and MATH (non-interactive, symbolic). Base (not instruction-tuned) models are used as actors specifically to isolate the effect of communication from instruction-tuning priors, with instruction-tuned counterparts of the same models used to generate the CoT plans and latents used in training. Baselines are CoT (full): full-parameter supervised fine-tuning on complete chain-of-thought plans; and No-CoT: predicting answers directly with no plan. Variants of the method itself include Text (same pipeline, but with the CoT plan substituted for the latent message), No-Comm (no inter-agent communication of any kind), CrossTask (matched latents replaced by a different task's latents), several statistics-preserving noise perturbations (CovGauss, RandomRot, white/covariance noise), and a cross-family condition (Qwen-derived latents fed to a LLaMA actor).

On ALFWorld (Table 1), Interlat obtains the highest success rate among all compared methods and variants across all three tested backbones (Qwen2.5-7B-Base, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Base, LLaMA3.1-8B-Base) and both seen and unseen task splits, generally with somewhat longer but more successful trajectories, which the authors interpret as latent communication enabling more thorough, "informed" exploration rather than degraded efficiency. On MATH (Table 2), Interlat beats the Text and No-Comm variants and the No-CoT baseline, but underperforms the CoT (full) baseline on the Overall score and on Levels 3 and 4, edging it out only on the hardest tier (Level 5: 15.80 vs. 15.05). The authors interpret this as evidence that natural language's strict linearity is a helpful regularizer on easier problems but forces a "premature collapse" of the search space on the hardest ones, where latent superposition of multiple hypotheses is advantageous instead. A series of structured perturbation experiments (CrossTask, statistics-preserving noise variants, cross-family transfer) shows performance degrading progressively as latent structure is more thoroughly destroyed, and — notably — the cross-family condition (Qwen latents training a LLaMA actor) performs at least as well as same-family Interlat, which the authors take as evidence the effect is not merely architectural compatibility between same-family hidden states. A training-dynamics plot shows the separation loss staying flat (near its theoretical maximum, ln 2) for roughly the first 2,000 steps before sharply dropping, described as an "aha moment" when the actor begins genuinely exploiting the latent channel.

The compression experiments (Table 3) compare naive truncation of an untrained model's full-length latents against a purpose-trained compression model, across message lengths from 8 to 128 positions (1.8% to 28.8% of the full length): naive truncation is competitive only at moderate compression (around 50%) and degrades sharply beyond that, while the trained compression model is more stable and consistently higher-performing across the whole range, while also cutting end-to-end communication latency from 9.19s to as low as 0.20s (roughly 24x with an additional lightweight bridge module). A follow-up analysis attributes this to the trained compression model preserving a broader, less peaked probability distribution over plausible next tokens (measured via the cumulative probability mass concentrated in the top-10 tokens) compared to untrained truncation, which collapses more quickly toward a single most-likely token. Ablations (Table 4) show removing the communication adapter is by far the most damaging change (collapsing ALFWorld success to near zero even though the model still produces fluent text), removing the curriculum destabilizes training, and removing the separation loss causes the model to fall back to ignoring the latent channel; for the compression model, the geometry-alignment loss is the single most load-bearing component, ahead of the agreement/distillation loss, while removing the task cross-entropy loss slightly hurts in-distribution performance but slightly helps generalization to unseen tasks (a trade-off the authors note but do not further investigate). A final experiment extends the two-agent setup to 3-agent sequential-chain and parallel-tree topologies on the smallest backbone, finding both configurations beat matching natural-language topologies and both beat the base two-agent Interlat setup, with the parallel-tree topology performing best.

The paper's Limitations section is explicit about scope: only two benchmarks (one interactive, one symbolic) and a controlled two-agent (or small 3-agent) setting are tested, not larger or more heterogeneous multi-agent ecosystems; the method requires white-box access to a model's hidden states, so it cannot apply to closed or API-only models; and latent messages are not human-readable, complicating debugging and monitoring, for which the paper offers only a preliminary PCA-based visualization (Figure 4) as a partial mitigation. A separate Ethical Considerations section explicitly names the possibility that a fully latent communication channel could in principle be used to circumvent language-based safety monitoring, and states the authors avoided training or evaluating on any harmful instructions as a mitigation.

## Learnings

A trainable "communication adapter" bridging one agent's raw hidden-state sequence into another agent's input-embedding space is not a minor implementation detail but the single most load-bearing component of the whole method in this paper's own ablation: removing it collapses task success to near zero even though the receiving model still produces fluent, coherent text, showing that two independently-trained models' latent spaces are not usable together without an explicit, trained translation layer between them, even when both are transformer-based language models.

A "conditional distribution separation" objective — penalizing a model for producing similar outputs whether it is given the correct auxiliary input or a mismatched one — is a concrete, reusable way to force a model to actually depend on an auxiliary conditioning channel instead of learning to ignore it; this paper's own ablation shows that removing exactly this objective causes the receiver to fall back to a shortcut of ignoring the latent channel and regressing toward single-agent performance, which generalizes as a technique well beyond latent communication specifically, to any setting where a model has an available but not strictly necessary auxiliary input.

When compressing a rich intermediate representation for a fixed, frozen downstream consumer, aligning the compressed representation's global geometric direction to the uncompressed original's turned out to be the most critical training signal in this paper's own ablation, ahead of both the direct task-utility loss and a behavioral-agreement/distillation loss — a useful reminder that when a representation's "meaning" is defined only implicitly by how a frozen downstream model uses it, task loss alone may under-constrain what a compressed version of it should preserve.

## Verification

The paper's most substantive verification effort targets a specific, load-bearing claim: that the actor model genuinely uses task-relevant structure in the latent communication rather than reacting to some cheap statistical proxy of it. This is tested with a deliberately staged sequence of perturbations — swapping in a different task's latents entirely (CrossTask), then perturbations engineered to preserve only first- and second-order statistics (mean, covariance) of the latent vectors while destroying all higher-order structure (CovGauss, RandomRot), then unstructured noise — and showing performance degrades progressively as each successive perturbation removes more of the genuine structure while preserving superficial statistics. This is a well-designed, falsifiable verification strategy for ruling out the specific alternative explanation that the model is exploiting only coarse statistics of the latent vectors, and is a reusable template for any claim that a model "uses" a rich signal rather than a shallow summary of it. Where verification is weaker is on statistical reliability of the headline results: the paper states results are "averaged over three independent runs," and does report per-condition standard deviations for the ablation table (Table 4) and the compression sweep (Table 3), but the main results table (Table 1) — which carries the paper's most central and most likely to be cited comparisons across three backbones — reports only point estimates with no variance at all, an inconsistency between how rigorously different parts of the same paper are verified.

## Field context

The paper positions itself at the intersection of two active lines of work it treats as previously separate. From single-model latent reasoning (pause and filler tokens that scaffold intermediate computation; a latent coprocessor modifying the transformer's KV cache; approaches that feed a model's last hidden state back as its own next input to enable latent-space "thinking"; modular encode-reason-decode frameworks), the paper explicitly frames its contribution as "shifting focus from single-model latent reasoning to inter-agent communication entirely in latent space." From prior hidden-state-based inter-agent communication specifically, it distinguishes itself from one-shot activation grafting/blending between agents (Ramesh and Li, 2025), per-token latent deltas tied to a language trajectory (Tang et al., 2025), probability-weighted tokenizer-embedding transmission (Pham et al., 2023), and autoencoder-inferred "thought" injection (Zheng et al., 2025), with its stated differentiator being direct transmission of full, temporally-aligned last-hidden-state sequences plus a separate learned compression stage, while explicitly avoiding parameter sharing, memory coupling, or KV-cache synchronization between agents, each of which it treats as a distinct alternative design choice rather than dismissing outright. It connects more loosely to classical emergent-communication research (referential games producing non-linguistic protocols from scratch), which it distinguishes as learning a new channel disconnected from a model's internal computation, unlike its own reuse of existing hidden states. None of these specific precedents are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first paper in the corpus specifically about inter-agent (rather than single-agent) latent communication.

## Critical discussion

The natural-language communication baseline this paper compares against is not uniformly a strong one, which complicates the interpretation of the paper's central claim. In Table 1, the "Text" variant (agents communicating via a CoT plan under the identical fine-tuning pipeline used for Interlat) sometimes performs worse than "No-Comm" (no communication at all) — for example, LLaMA3.1-8B on seen tasks scores 62.86 for Text versus 63.57 for No-Comm, and the pattern is not isolated to one cell. If a trained natural-language communication channel can be actively harmful relative to no communication in some settings, then part of Interlat's reported margin over "Text" may reflect a comparatively weak or under-optimized implementation of language-based communication in this specific supervised fine-tuning setup, rather than solely reflecting an inherent advantage of latent over token-based communication in general; a more decisive test of the paper's central claim would need a natural-language communication baseline that is reliably and substantially better than no communication across all tested conditions.

The MATH result is framed in the main text as an "intriguing inversion" in which Interlat "surpasses the CoT baseline on the most challenging Level-5 tasks," which is numerically accurate (15.80 vs. 15.05) but is a narrow, roughly half-a-point win on one difficulty tier set against the CoT baseline's considerably larger lead on Overall (38.35 vs. 36.88), Level-3 (45.65 vs. 40.08), and Level-4 (31.19 vs. 27.45). The proposed explanation — natural language's "forced discretization" causing "premature collapse" on hard problems, versus latent superposition permitting broader search — is a plausible account of the one favorable comparison cell, but the aggregate picture in the very same table is a clear net loss to the CoT baseline on this benchmark, which the "surpasses" framing does not foreground.

Statistical uncertainty is reported unevenly across the paper: Table 1, which carries the central cross-backbone comparison the abstract's headline claims rest on, reports no standard deviations despite the stated three-run averaging practice used elsewhere, while Tables 3 and 4 do report per-condition standard deviations (often in the range of roughly 1-3 points). Some of Table 1's own reported gaps between conditions (for instance, the cross-family Qwen2LLaMA variant edging out same-family Interlat by well under a point) are comparable in size to the variability reported for structurally similar comparisons elsewhere in the paper, so it is not possible from Table 1 alone to judge which specific comparisons would survive a formal significance check.

On a different axis, the paper's own Ethical Considerations section is commendably direct about naming a genuine, non-hypothetical concern: a fully latent, non-human-readable communication channel could in principle be used to circumvent language-based safety monitoring of what agents are telling each other. Its stated mitigation — not training or evaluating on harmful instructions, plus a preliminary PCA visualization of latent structure for transparency — is a reasonable first step but a fairly limited response to a channel whose entire design goal is efficiency gained specifically by not being decoded into inspectable text; the authors' own Limitations section acknowledges this gap rather than claiming it is resolved, which is an appropriately modest framing given how far the current interpretability mitigation actually goes.

## Relevance to us

This paper is relevant on two fronts, one methodological and one cautionary. Methodologically, both the "conditional thought separation" training objective (penalizing similar outputs under matched versus mismatched auxiliary inputs, to force genuine reliance on a signal) and the staged-perturbation verification strategy (progressively destroying candidate structure — task identity, then higher-order statistics, then all structure — to isolate exactly what a model is sensitive to) are directly reusable techniques whenever we want to check that a model genuinely uses an auxiliary input (a retrieved document, a tool's output, another agent's intermediate state) rather than ignoring it or reacting only to a shallow proxy of it, independent of whether the auxiliary input is a latent vector or something else entirely. On the cautionary side, the paper's own Ethical Considerations section names, without fully resolving, a concrete interpretability cost of latent (non-token) communication between agents: it is not human-readable and could in principle bypass language-based safety oversight, which speaks directly to our interest in keeping an agentic system's intermediate reasoning inspectable. If we ever evaluate or adopt a latent-communication or latent-reasoning technique for its efficiency benefits (here, up to a 24x reduction in communication latency), this paper is a concrete, self-acknowledged reminder that the efficiency gain is being traded directly against the inspectability of what agents are actually telling each other, not merely against some abstract accuracy or cost metric, and that trade-off should be made deliberately rather than incidentally when the stakes of an undetected coordination failure are high.
