Citation: Zhang, Y., Li, H., Wang, C., Chen, L., Zhang, Q., Ye, P., Feng, S., Wang, D., Wang, Z., Wang, X., Xu, J., Bai, L., Ouyang, W., and Hu, S. The Avengers: A Simple Recipe for Uniting Smaller Language Models to Challenge Proprietary Giants. AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI), 2026.

## What this paper is about

Instead of training ever-larger proprietary models, this paper asks whether a team of many small (~7B parameter), openly available models can be combined to match a frontier proprietary model's overall performance across a broad range of tasks. The proposed system, the Avengers, does this with no additional training at all: it embeds and clusters queries from a small labeled validation set, records which of the candidate models does best on each query cluster, and then at inference time routes each new query to whichever model was strongest on similar queries, using repeated sampling and majority voting to produce the final answer. Combining 10 openly available ~7B models this way, the paper reports the resulting system beats GPT-4o, GPT-4.5-Preview, and GPT-4.1 on average across 15 benchmarks spanning math, code, logic, knowledge, and affective/emotion tasks, and generalizes better than several trained neural routers to five held-out benchmarks the system was never calibrated on.

## Extended summary

The method has two stages. Offline calibration: each query in a validation set is embedded with a text-embedding model, the embeddings are clustered (K-means with K=64 by default) into semantically coherent query types, and each candidate model's accuracy is measured within every cluster, producing a per-model "capability profile" vector over clusters. Online inference: a new query is embedded, assigned to its nearest cluster, and the top-scoring model(s) for that cluster are selected to answer it via repeated sampling (10 samples) and majority voting — Self-Consistency when one model is selected, or the "Model-Switch" multi-model variant when more than one is. A separate automatic model-selection step scores each candidate model in a larger pool by summing an inverse-rank score across clusters, and picks the top-|M| models to deploy, explicitly rewarding models that are either broadly strong or narrowly excellent in specific clusters, which the authors argue yields a complementary rather than merely individually-strong model set.

The main experiment starts from a pool of 22 open ~7B models, automatically selects 10, and evaluates them (plus baselines) on 15 datasets across five categories (math, code, logic, knowledge, affective), each dataset run over 5 random seeds. The headline result is an average score of 70.54 for the Avengers versus 69.20 for GPT-4.1, 68.18 for GPT-4.5-Preview, and 60.22 for GPT-4o, with the Avengers beating GPT-4.1 on 10 of 15 individual datasets, most heavily on math (AIME, +37% relative) and code (MBPP, +32% relative), while GPT-4.1 keeps a clear edge on some knowledge-heavy benchmarks (GPQA, MedQA) attributed to its larger scale and broader knowledge. A second comparison isolates the routing mechanism itself against three trained neural routers (RouterDC, EmbedLLM, MODEL-SAT) and two mixture-based methods (Mixture-of-Agents and Symbolic-MoE), all given the same 10-model pool and the same Self-Consistency decoding the Avengers uses; the Avengers still comes out ahead on average, and its advantage widens substantially (a further 8 to 10 percentage points over the best trained router) on five additional out-of-distribution benchmarks the system was never calibrated on, which none of the models or the clustering saw during offline calibration.

A series of ablations probe robustness of the method's own design choices: swapping among five embedding models changes the average score by only about half a point (70.28 to 70.70); swapping among five clustering algorithms (K-means, hierarchical, GMM, spectral, BIRCH) changes it by under a point (70.04 to 70.71); performance rises sharply as the number of selected models grows from 1 to about 10 and then plateaus; and performance is stable across a roughly 10x range of the cluster count K (14 to 140), while both too few clusters (under-fitting the query-type structure) and too many (each query becoming close to its own cluster, at which point the validation-set score approaches an oracle upper bound but test-set performance collapses) hurt performance — the authors use this observed inverted-U pattern to argue, speculatively, that the neural router baselines they compare against likely overfit their validation objective in an analogous way, since those methods report peak-test-set numbers rather than validation-selected checkpoints. A separate experiment shows the calibration-to-test data split can be as lean as 30% calibration data while still beating GPT-4.1. A model-usage breakdown shows the system does route meaningfully to niche models: a financial-domain fine-tune (Fin-R1) is selected for roughly half of ARC-Challenge queries, and a medical fine-tune (Llama-3.1-8B-UltraMedical) for roughly 40% of MedQA queries, despite neither model being a broadly dominant general performer.

The authors are explicit that reported baseline numbers are enhanced beyond each baseline's original published setup for fairness: all baselines use the same automatically-selected 10-model pool, all router/SLM results incorporate the same 10-sample Self-Consistency decoding the Avengers uses, the neural routers are reported at their peak test-set performance rather than a fixed training budget, and the Mixture-of-Agents baseline is additionally given an oracle variant that manually picks the top-3 models per task (addressing a context-length failure mode of the original method with 10 models).

## Learnings

A training-free routing scheme built from off-the-shelf embeddings, ordinary clustering, and a simple per-cluster performance lookup can match or beat several purpose-trained neural routers (RouterDC, EmbedLLM, MODEL-SAT) on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution benchmarks in this paper's tests, and its out-of-distribution advantage was the larger of the two — a useful data point that a query-routing system's generalization can come from having fewer learnable parameters to overfit with, not from a more sophisticated routing mechanism.

The paper's own diagnostic curve (test-set and validation-set performance as cluster count K varies from 1 to 10,000) shows a clean under-clustering / stable / over-clustering pattern: validation performance keeps climbing toward an oracle ceiling as K grows, while test performance rises, plateaus, then collapses — a concrete, reusable illustration of a specificity-vs-generalization trade-off in any clustering-based lookup system, independent of whether the clustering is used for LLM routing specifically.

Automatically selecting a complementary model set by an inverse-rank score across query clusters (rather than by each model's single overall accuracy) surfaced niche, low-visibility fine-tuned models (a financial-domain and a medical-domain 7B model) as heavily used, high-value components of the final system — a demonstration that "best on average" and "valuable to include in an ensemble" are different properties worth optimizing separately when assembling a model pool.

## Verification

The paper's checks are standard automated benchmark scoring (accuracy against reference answers) across all 15 (plus 5 out-of-distribution) datasets, with no LLM-judge-based scoring described; code tasks (MBPP, HumanEval) presumably use execution-based correctness, though the exact scoring harness for each dataset is not detailed. Each headline number is averaged over 5 random seeds, which is a genuine and appreciated form of noise control, but the paper reports only point-estimate averages in its main tables and does not report seed-to-seed standard deviations or any significance test, so a reader cannot tell how much of the paper's per-dataset improvements or regressions relative to GPT-4.1 (several of which are single-digit percentage points, e.g. +1.66% on MMLUPro, +0.67% on FinQA) would survive a formal significance test against 5-seed noise. The clearest verification-relevant design choice is methodological rather than statistical: the authors explicitly separate the "routing mechanism" evaluation (Table 2/3, all methods normalized to the same model pool and decoding strategy) from the raw headline comparison against proprietary models, which is a useful move for isolating what the Avengers' actual algorithmic contribution buys versus what comes from picking a good model pool or decoding strategy.

## Field context

The paper positions itself within a router-based paradigm for multi-model LLM systems, distinguished from mixture-based approaches (Mixture-of-Agents and its variants, which pool multiple models' outputs per query) and from model-merging approaches (which combine model weights rather than outputs), with the latter two paradigms deferred to the appendix's related-work discussion. Among router-based methods, it contrasts itself with a line of trained neural routers — RouterDC (dual contrastive learning), EmbedLLM (learned compact model embeddings), and MODEL-SAT (capability-instruction-tuned routing) — all of which require training a neural component and re-training when new tasks or models are introduced, versus its own training-free, incrementally-updatable clustering approach. It also distinguishes itself from prior training-free routing work (k-NN or k-means-based routers in cost-performance tradeoff settings), which the authors state has previously been applied only in settings with large model-size disparities and has not previously been shown to bring smaller open models to parity with a flagship proprietary model's overall performance. The specific benchmark suite (AIME, MATH500, MBPP, KORBench, MMLUPro, GPQA, and others) and the repeated-sampling/voting inference strategy (Self-Consistency; Model-Switch) are both drawn from existing, separately cited prior work rather than introduced here. None of these specific precedents (RouterDC, EmbedLLM, MODEL-SAT, Mixture-of-Agents, Symbolic-MoE) are yet reviewed in this corpus.

## Critical discussion

The most important caveat, which the paper itself discloses but somewhat undersells relative to its headline framing, is that all baseline methods were substantially modified from their original published form to be given to this comparison: every baseline (including the mixture-based and trained-router methods) is run on the same automatically-selected 10-model pool the Avengers itself picked, all SLM-based methods are given the same 10-sample Self-Consistency decoding the Avengers uses, and the trained neural routers are reported at their peak test-set score rather than at a fixed training budget or a validation-selected checkpoint. The authors' stated rationale — isolating the routing mechanism itself — is reasonable and their transparency about this choice is a genuine strength, but "peak test-set score" for the neural-router baselines is a distinctly generous standard that a deployed system could not actually achieve without access to test-set labels, and comparing the Avengers' own (presumably not similarly cherry-picked) result against baselines evaluated at their test-set peak understates how large the reported OOD advantage over those routers might be under a fairer, validation-based stopping rule for all methods, or overstates it if the Avengers' own reported numbers are not selected under an equivalent procedure — the paper does not clarify whether the Avengers' own numbers are similarly a "peak" selection across any of its own design choices (e.g., the K=64 cluster count, chosen ahead of time from an ablation the authors ran) versus a single pre-registered configuration.

The proposed explanation for why trained neural routers underperform in this setting (Appendix B.4: they "overfit" toward an over-clustering-like regime because of their extra learnable parameters) is explicitly labeled "speculative" by the authors themselves, which is appropriately hedged, but it is also not independently tested against an alternative, simpler explanation: the trained routers in this comparison were also given the same fixed 10-model pool the Avengers itself selected (rather than a pool independently optimized for each router), and it is not established whether their relative weakness reflects their algorithm or reflects being evaluated on a model pool assembled by a criterion (inverse-rank cluster score) that may specifically favor a clustering-and-lookup style of exploitation.

On the positive side, the ablations covering embedding model, clustering algorithm, and cluster count are genuinely comprehensive for a paper whose central claim is about robustness/simplicity, and the explicit separation between the raw "beats GPT-4.1" headline and the "beats other routers under a matched setup" comparison is a methodologically sound way to decompose the claim. The paper's own limitations section is also honest about scope: it does not test chat, multilingual, or cross-cultural conversational tasks (citing the difficulty of objective scoring there), and it only tests ~7B-scale models, not smaller ones, so the "smaller models" framing in the title should be read as "one particular small-model scale that happens to be well-supported by the open-source community," not as a general claim across all sub-frontier model sizes.

## Relevance to us

This is the clearest example in the corpus so far of Tension 1 (frontier backbone vs. small specialized models): rather than choosing between a frontier API and a single small specialized model, this paper reports a concrete, tested middle path — a training-free ensemble of several small, individually mediocre-to-strong open models, automatically selected and routed by simple clustering, matching or beating a frontier proprietary model's overall performance across a broad task mix. This is a genuine mechanism worth remembering the next time we weigh a single-frontier-backbone approach against a smaller, on-prem or lower-cost alternative: the relevant comparison point may not be "one small model vs. one frontier model" but "an orchestrated small-model pool vs. one frontier model," which changes the shape of the cost/capability trade-off substantially, especially since the reported routing overhead (embedding, clustering, one profile lookup per query) is far cheaper than training or fine-tuning a specialized model. The model-usage finding — that niche, low-visibility, community-contributed fine-tunes (a financial-domain and a medical-domain model) become disproportionately valuable once given a mechanism to be selected only where they are actually the best option — is also a concrete counter-example to a "just use the best general-purpose small model" heuristic, worth keeping in mind if we ever build or evaluate a similar tool-pool or model-pool selection mechanism ourselves (echoing this corpus's separate concern, in TAXONOMY.md's tool-pool tension, about needing complementary rather than uniformly-strong components in a pool).
