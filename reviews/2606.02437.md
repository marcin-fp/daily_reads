## What this paper is about

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT, especially LoRA) is usually framed as a cheap substitute for full fine-tuning. This paper argues for a different framing: a small adapter can be a compact, persistent unit of "local state" sitting on top of a strong shared foundation model — carrying part of a specific instance's preferences, skills, tool habits, or memory, while the base model supplies general competence. Using a biological analogy (humans share over 99% of their genome; the small fraction of variation produces all human individuality), the paper organizes evidence around three coupled requirements that must all hold for this vision to work: the shared base model must be strong enough to make small updates worthwhile ("Scale Up"), the local adaptive state must be small and stable enough to train and store cheaply at scale ("Scale Down"), and many such adapted instances must be able to coexist and interact usefully ("Scale Out"). The paper reports a large collection of experiments and infrastructure measurements supporting each axis, while explicitly cautioning that the overall thesis is a research direction, not a deployed, validated system.

## Extended summary

**Scale Up.** The paper argues RL is "prior-limited" — it can only reinforce trajectories the current policy already samples with non-negligible probability — so a stronger base model expands what RL can discover and stabilize, making small local updates on top of it disproportionately valuable. A motivating comparison (Table 2) shows LoRA-adapted larger models (e.g., DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-32B with rank-8 LoRA, 0.07B trainable parameters) achieving larger headroom-normalized AIME2025/GPQA-Diamond gains than a much smaller model (1.5B) under full-parameter RL — a comparison the authors themselves flag as confounding model size and training method simultaneously, so its role is illustrative rather than a clean causal isolation. The paper's stronger evidence is engineering feasibility: a Kimi K2 case applies GRPO-style on-policy LoRA RL to a 1.04T-parameter, 32.6B-active-parameter MoE model, using LoRA on selected dense and expert layers plus hybrid tensor/pipeline/expert/sequence parallelism, reducing compute/communication footprint to roughly 10% of full-parameter RL while showing stable reward curves. This surfaces a specific, technically detailed failure mode: training-inference mismatch (TIM), where numerical differences between a serving-oriented rollout engine and a training-oriented backend can flip discrete MoE expert-routing decisions, meaning the training engine ends up optimizing a different effective computation than the one that generated the sampled rollout — more than a precision issue, since it changes the object of the policy update. The fix, Router Replay (R3), records routing decisions during rollout and replays them during training; reported metrics show R3 reduces rollout/training probability mismatch, keeps PPO KL divergence near zero (0.000026 at step 46 vs. substantial increases for baselines), and improves downstream validation accuracy on a math task where baselines degrade. A further case (GLM-5/5.1, combining MoE, Multi-Head Latent Attention, DeepSeek Sparse Attention, and Multi-Token Prediction) illustrates that at frontier scale, training/serving infrastructure becomes part of the model's semantic definition, not just a substrate: a generic LoRA wrapper correct for ordinary linear layers can be semantically wrong for specialized attention/routing modules, so an adapter can load successfully while the served computation silently differs from the trained one.

**Scale Down.** A 216-run Qwen3-8B PPO sweep (nine LoRA ranks x four batch sizes x six seeds, fixed 500-step schedule on math with verifiable rewards) finds three regimes rather than a monotonic capacity curve: ranks 16-32 are the strongest practical operating point (highest mean gain, low downside risk); ranks above 64 add parameter/checkpoint/memory footprint without raising the observed best-run ceiling; and ranks 1-4 are the most consequential finding — their best-seed runs are competitive with ranks 16-32, but their across-seed mean degrades and variance widens, indicating an optimization/initialization problem rather than a hard capacity limit. This motivates OLoRA-tail, an RL-native initialization that anchors the adapter's few available directions in the minor (rather than principal) singular subspace of the pretrained weight matrix, without the extra singular-value scaling used by prior methods (PiSSA, MiLoRA). The paper shows PiSSA-style principal-subspace initialization causes training collapse under on-policy RL (reward drops to -1.0, KL explodes to ~8) because perturbing the model's dominant pretrained directions breaks the small-trust-region assumption that makes RL policy-gradient surrogates valid, whereas the minor subspace is comparatively inert and safer to update; OLoRA-tail matches or beats standard LoRA on six math benchmarks (58.3% vs. 56.3% average) at rank 16, and at the extreme of rank 1 delivers a consistent ~+20% gain over the base model across batch sizes on Qwen3-8B (vs. LoRA degrading from +15% to -18% as batch size grows) and a 35.5% vs. 24.0% average pass rate on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct (6 seeds, reported with standard deviation). A separate hyperparameter-transfer study (AG News/DistilBERT and a harder Qwen3-4B MATH setting) tests three LoRA alpha-scaling conventions and finds that scaling alpha with the square root of rank (the rsLoRA rule, here validated empirically in an RL setting rather than proposed) best preserves a reusable learning-rate band across ranks, particularly in the harder reasoning setting where the viable learning-rate range is narrow. The paper then extends "Scale Down" beyond static LoRA to δ-mem, a stateful adapter that reads a compact recurrent memory state to produce history-dependent low-rank corrections and writes back via a delta-rule update; on Qwen3-4B-Instruct, the best δ-mem variant raises an aggregate score across IFEval/HotpotQA/GPQA-D/MemoryAgentBench/LoCoMo from 46.79% to 51.66% while using only 0.12-0.48% of backbone parameters, outperforming textual-memory (BM25 RAG, MemoryBank) and other parametric-memory baselines (Context2LoRA, MemGen) on most tasks.

**Scale Out.** The paper introduces DishNameBenchmark, a controlled slot-writing/slot-querying benchmark, to measure LoRA's usable memory capacity; across 263 runs it finds a sharp capacity transition (near-perfect accuracy below ~1e-3 memory tokens per trainable parameter, collapse above ~1e-2) and that training MLP-only LoRA is the most parameter-efficient module choice for memory storage (MLP > Attention ~ All >> Unembedding). A skill-memory experiment (Qwen3-235B base, rank-32 LoRA, ALFWorld) raises average task performance from 0.646 to 0.845, supporting the narrower claim that LoRA can store reusable procedural/skill-like state rather than arbitrary facts. Context Learning / Context Distillation is proposed as an explicit "write policy": a query-only rollout is scored by a privileged query-plus-context system, and the resulting reward signal updates the query-only policy via an RL-style update — so gains obtained with retrieved context, tools, or demonstrations can be partially internalized into parameters without requiring that context at inference time. In a social-simulation setting (OASIS, populations of 128-512 agents from one community), replacing shared-base-model agents with per-user rank-4 LoRA adapters (each trained on 80 historical tweets) preserves substantially more identity heterogeneity (2-2.5x higher within-side stance dispersion) and produces richer, more structured interaction topology (more effective communities, higher co-engagement modularity) than the shared-base condition — while the authors explicitly caveat this is evidence from one community, one recommender, one seed per cell, not a general social-simulation law. Finally, a controlled model-count experiment trains many LoRA variants from the same Qwen3-30B base (differing only by data ordering/masking under an identical RL recipe) and aggregates their AIME24 answers by majority vote: accuracy rises from 0.3644 at k=1 to 0.4867 at best (k=198), following an approximately log(k) curve (R^2=0.888) that continues improving well past the point where repeated sampling from one model (self-consistency) saturates (best 0.4378 at k=24) — evidence, the authors argue, that distinct LoRA trajectories learn complementary rather than merely noisy-redundant policies, though they explicitly frame the log-law as "an empirical law in one controlled regime, not a universal theorem."

**Infrastructure (MinT).** The paper's final chapter argues all three axes require a systems layer, exemplified by MinT (described in a companion paper), which separates policy identity (a durable record of an adapted behavior) from serving residency (a placement fact: shared storage, CPU cache, or GPU batch slot). Concrete measurements: adapter-only handoff keeps moved training-to-serving artifacts far smaller than merged checkpoints (a Qwen3-30B rank-16 adapter is 1.692 GB vs. 61.084 GB merged); a two-phase readiness protocol (register/prewarm before exposing a new adapter to traffic) eliminates cold-load disruption to existing warm users (old-traffic TTFT p95 stays at 9.63s with zero >20s stalls, vs. 24.03s and 10 stalls without any admission control); and packing a fragmented MoE LoRA adapter from 37,248 to 672 tensor objects yields an 8.5-8.7x live-loading speedup with nearly unchanged file size. The paper explicitly frames its own Limitations: "the evidence in this paper points to a direction, not a deployed system," most experiments run at controlled-benchmark or simulation scale, and large-scale validation on real personal-model deployments remains open, attributed to compute constraints rather than missing methodology. No funding is disclosed.

## Learnings

Training-inference mismatch (TIM) in sparse MoE reinforcement learning, and its fix via Router Replay (recording routing decisions during rollout and replaying them during training), is a specific, mechanistically clear, and broadly transferable finding for anyone running on-policy RL on any MoE model: a numerically negligible perturbation in a dense model can flip a discrete routing decision in a sparse model, silently changing which computation is actually being optimized, and this is categorically different from (and not fixed by) standard importance-sampling correction, which assumes the two policies remain comparable distributions over the same computation graph.

OLoRA-tail's core insight — that in on-policy RL, the safest initialization for an extremely low-rank adapter's few available directions is the pretrained weight matrix's minor (least-perturbing) singular subspace without additional value scaling, rather than the principal subspace that works well for supervised fine-tuning — is a transferable, mechanistically grounded design rule: it correctly predicts and explains why prior SVD-based initializations (PiSSA, MiLoRA) that help in SFT can destabilize RL, tying the failure directly to the KL-trust-region argument that governs when RL policy-gradient surrogates remain valid. This is a genuinely generalizable lesson (initialization strategy should differ by objective type — dense supervised loss vs. sparse RL reward — not be treated as a universal default) independent of this paper's specific personal-models framing.

The LoRA memory capacity law (usable capacity bounded to roughly 1e-3 to 1e-2 memory tokens per trainable parameter, with a sharp transition rather than a graceful degradation, and MLP layers being the most parameter-efficient place to write memory) is a concrete, reusable quantitative reference point for anyone considering using adapter weights as a memory substrate rather than retrieval or context — the finding that capacity is a hard, measurable ceiling (not an unlimited store) is the more important takeaway than the specific numbers, since it directly motivates treating LoRA as one tier in a memory hierarchy (alongside context, retrieval, and tool state) rather than a general-purpose fact store.

The diversity-via-independently-trained-LoRA-variants result — that majority voting across models differing only in data ordering and masking continues improving well past the point where repeated sampling from a single model saturates, following an approximately logarithmic curve in model count — is a transferable, cheaply-testable claim about ensemble diversity: it suggests that even the modest diversity introduced by different RL training trajectories from a shared base (not different architectures or pretraining corpora) is a real, non-redundant source of collective accuracy, which is a useful, low-cost lever (train many cheap LoRA variants rather than one large model) for anyone building majority-vote or routing-based multi-model systems.

## Verification

Verification quality varies substantially across this paper's many sub-studies, tracking roughly with how public and reproducible the underlying system is. The Scale Down experiments are the best-verified: the 216-run rank/batch/seed sweep and the OLoRA-tail rank-1 comparison (6 random seeds, reported with standard deviations) directly separate "best-case signal exists" from "the configuration is reliable," which is the paper's own stated methodological point and is demonstrated rather than merely asserted. The δ-mem and LoRA-memory-capacity results are benchmarked against multiple named baselines (BM25 RAG, MemoryBank, Context2LoRA, MemGen) on public or clearly-described benchmarks (HotpotQA, LoCoMo, MemoryAgentBench), giving external reference points for the reported gains. By contrast, the Scale Up trillion-scale evidence (Kimi K2 LoRA RL, GLM-5/5.1 support) is presented via smoothed training curves on proprietary internal systems with no variance, no seed repetition, and no external reproducibility path — the paper is candid that these are "proof of existence" demonstrations rather than statistically characterized results, but readers cannot independently verify the underlying claims. The OASIS social-simulation result and the model-count collaboration experiment are each run at one seed per configuration (respectively per population size, and 30 samples per k for the voting curve), which the authors explicitly flag as a scope limitation for OASIS but do not equally flag for the collaboration log-law fit, despite both resting on comparably thin per-point replication.

## Field context

This paper sits at the center of the active PEFT/LoRA scaling literature it cites extensively (LoRA itself, PiSSA, MiLoRA, rsLoRA, LoRA-forgetting and representation-movement studies), and extends it in an unusual direction: rather than asking whether LoRA matches full fine-tuning on a fixed task, it asks whether LoRA can serve as a first-class unit of persistent, population-scale individual state. This framing creates a direct, load-bearing tension with a claim made elsewhere in this reviewed corpus: [reviews/2605.22502.md](2605.22502.md) ("Compiling Agentic Workflows into LLM Weights") explicitly cites a companion study finding that "low-rank methods fail to approach full fine-tuning on procedural tasks," and for that reason uses full-parameter fine-tuning rather than LoRA to compile a multi-step conversational procedure into a small model's weights. This paper's own Scale Out section makes essentially the opposite bet for a related class of state — that LoRA can carry "skills, tool habits, workflows" as persistent behavioral memory — supported here by a single ALFWorld skill-transfer result (Qwen3-235B, rank-32 LoRA, 0.646 to 0.845) rather than the multi-domain procedural comparison the other paper reports. Neither paper engages with the other, but a downstream synthesis comparing them directly would need to reconcile "LoRA is insufficient for multi-step procedures" against "LoRA is sufficient for skill/workflow memory," and the difference may hinge on task complexity, rank, or training method (RL here vs. the other paper's full supervised fine-tuning) rather than being a simple contradiction. This paper is also thematically adjacent to [reviews/2604.18936.md](2604.18936.md) (fine-tuning small reasoning models for QFT via RL and SFT), which reaches a related but narrower conclusion — that RL and SFT both preferentially fix factual/recall errors before reasoning errors on one 7B model — a data point this paper's own Scale Up motivating comparison (Table 2, LoRA-on-larger-model vs. full-RL-on-smaller-model) would need to be reconciled with in any broader account of where trainable-parameter budget is best spent.

## Critical discussion

The paper's strongest evidence is where it is most narrowly scoped and most carefully controlled: the rank/batch/seed sweep, the OLoRA-tail ablation with reported variance across seeds, and the concrete, measured MinT infrastructure numbers (artifact sizes, load-time speedups, tail-latency percentiles under different admission policies) are all specific, falsifiable, and would be straightforward for another group to attempt to replicate given the described setup. The paper is also unusually self-aware about the limits of its own evidence throughout the text — repeatedly noting when a comparison is confounded (Table 2), when a result is from "one controlled regime, not a universal theorem" (the model-count log-law), or when a finding is scoped to "one community, one recommender mechanism, and one seed per cell" (OASIS) — which is good practice rarely this consistent across a paper this broad in scope.

The main limitation is that this self-awareness is not applied uniformly, and the overall document structure (a sweeping title and an extended biological analogy spanning the introduction and section headers) sits in tension with an evidence base that is, item by item, a collection of narrow, often single-seed or proprietary-system demonstrations rather than a unified experimental program. The Kimi K2 and GLM-5/5.1 evidence for "Scale Up requires computation provenance" — arguably the paper's most striking engineering claim (making a 1T-parameter MoE model trainable via LoRA RL at ~10% of full-RL compute) — rests entirely on internal, non-reproducible systems with smoothed curves and no reported variance, which is a reasonable disclosure for an industry lab's own infrastructure but means the specific magnitude of the claimed 10% compute saving cannot be independently checked. The model-count collaboration experiment's log(k) fit (R^2=0.888) is presented with more apparent statistical confidence than its underlying replication (30 samples per k, one base model, one task) can fully support, and unlike the OASIS experiment the paper does not explicitly flag this as a single-setting result before generalizing the "diversity is a computational resource" thesis from it. What remains solidly supported across the paper's more rigorously tested claims: within the specific settings tested, extremely low-rank LoRA adapters are underoptimized rather than under-capacity and can be rescued by RL-native (minor-subspace) initialization; LoRA memory capacity is a real, measurable, bounded quantity rather than an unlimited store; and per-user LoRA adapters produce measurably more stable behavioral heterogeneity than shared-model persona prompting in at least one controlled social-simulation setting. The broader "millions of personal models" architecture these findings are assembled to support remains, as the paper's own Limitations section states, a direction rather than a demonstrated system.
