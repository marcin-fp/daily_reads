Citation: Zhao, H., Si, S., Wang, Z., Wang, Z., Chen, L., Li, X., Liang, Z., Sun, M., Zhang, M. Crafter: A Multi-Agent Harness for Editable Scientific Figure Generation from Diverse Inputs. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30611v1

## What this paper is about

Making a publication-quality scientific figure — a method diagram, a poster, an infographic — is one of the most tedious parts of writing a paper, and existing AI tools only cover a narrow slice of that job: they mostly generate one figure type from text alone, and their raster output can't be locally edited afterward (swap an icon, fix a label, without regenerating everything). This paper argues the fix isn't a better image-generation model but a "harness": an orchestration layer of cooperating agents that plans multiple candidate layouts, tracks corrections in a structured record instead of an ever-growing pile of free-text instructions, and iteratively verifies and refines the result. It introduces two such harnesses (Crafter for generation, CraftEditor for converting the raster result into an editable SVG afterward) plus CraftBench, a new benchmark spanning three figure types and four kinds of input (not just text) to actually test whether a system generalizes rather than excelling at one narrow setting.

## Extended summary

The harness is formalized as a four-role loop over a shared, evolving specification S: a Designer proposes a plan, an Executor renders it (pluggable — an image generator for Crafter, a code/asset-injection step for CraftEditor), a Verifier emits a directive diagnostic (per-dimension scores, named defects, and suggested fixes — not a single scalar score), and a Reviser applies typed, structured edits to S (e.g., "ban artifact category X," "resize element Y") rather than appending free-text corrections to the prompt, which the authors argue is what causes iterative repair to silently degrade via contradictory accumulated instructions. Crafter instantiates this with five cooperating agents (intent reasoner, plan generator, image-generation backend, critic, specification refiner) plus a convergence judge, built on three mechanisms: diversity-driven plan exploration (K parallel candidate visual framings, so a structurally bad starting layout can be abandoned rather than repaired), the structured corrective layer (typed edits, as above), and a verify-then-refine loop with an early-exit gate and best-so-far checkpointing (since LLM-driven iterative editing is empirically non-monotonic). CraftEditor reuses the same four-role loop for raster-to-SVG conversion in three phases: extraction (an instruction-driven keep/delete cleanup replacing brittle off-the-shelf segmentation on cluttered layouts), processing (captioning and vector/raster classification of each element), and composition (generating and iteratively refining an SVG skeleton against a hybrid critic combining a VLM's layout judgment with programmatic structural checks — text overflow, arrow-endpoint accuracy, element overlap — that the authors note VLM judgment alone tends to miss).

CraftBench (279 samples) is built via a seven-stage quality pipeline (keyword filtering, a 15-type VLM content classifier, complexity rescoring requiring 8+ named components and 10+ minutes of estimated manual recreation time, alignment verification, two rounds of VLM quality assurance, and full manual review) from arXiv preprints across 18 subject areas, award-tier CVPR/ICLR posters, and a research blog, spanning text-to-image (179 samples) and three reference-conditioned tasks (mask-completion, key-element composition, sketch-conditioned generation; 30-40 samples each) that require preserving a given input rather than generating from text alone. Every reference-conditioned sample passed unanimous review by three graduate annotators. Evaluation uses a per-image VLM-judge protocol (Gemini 3.5 Flash for CraftBench, the official Gemini 3.1 Pro judge for PaperBanana-Bench) scoring the candidate and a human-drawn ground truth independently (avoiding pairwise position bias) on task-specific weighted aspects, yielding a lenient {Model, Tie, Human} win-rate; a blind human pairwise study (60 comparisons, three graduate annotators) found the automatic judge agrees with the majority human verdict 72% of the time (Cohen's kappa = 0.58).

Under a controlled comparison holding the image-generation backbone (Nano Banana 2) and vision-language agent (Gemini 3.1 Pro) fixed across all agentic methods, Crafter beats the strongest agentic baseline (PaperBanana) by 16.61 points on PaperBanana-Bench and 22.20 points on CraftBench, and is the only method to improve over its own backbone generator on every quality dimension and every task on both benchmarks; the closest baseline (PaperBanana) shows its own gain over its backbone shrink sharply from 22.60 points on the narrow benchmark it was designed for to 8.10 points on the broader CraftBench, and actually falls below its backbone on the sketch task, illustrating the generalization failure the paper's introduction predicts for systems built around a single figure type. Swapping the image-generation backbone from Nano Banana 2 to Nano Banana Pro shifts Crafter's overall score by only 0.34-2.10 points, supporting the claim that the harness contribution is largely executor-agnostic. Ablations (removing one mechanism at a time from the full pipeline) show every component matters: removing plan exploration (K=1) costs 8.56 points, removing the structured corrective layer (reverting to free-text revision) costs 8.90 points, removing the refinement loop costs 5.48 points, and removing the directive critic (reverting toward less actionable feedback) costs 5.04 points. A separate ablation on CraftEditor (evaluated on 80 held-out Crafter outputs, scored by a three-VLM ensemble spanning Gemini, GPT-5.4, and Doubao) finds CraftEditor beats two raster-to-editable baselines on every one of seven axes (overall 8.04 vs. 6.91 and 3.69), with iterative composition contributing the larger effect (-2.15 points overall when removed) and agentic cleaning a smaller but consistent one (-0.33 points). Per-figure inference cost is reported directly: $0.25 (Crafter with Nano Banana 2) to $0.32 (with Nano Banana Pro), roughly 2-3x PaperBanana's $0.11, plus $0.85 per CraftEditor conversion; the authors note the full 279-sample CraftBench run cost under $90 total. Authors' stated limitations: headline results depend entirely on closed-source, proprietary image-generation backbones and VLM judges (making the harness's absolute numbers conditional on vendor access and judge biases); per-sample latency and API cost are non-trivial at scale (up to four parallel image generations plus three refinement rounds per Crafter call); and CraftBench, while quality-controlled sample-by-sample, remains modest in size (279 samples) and skews toward academic/poster figures, with infographics the smallest and least-represented category. Three named failure modes are documented with examples (Appendix L): dropped panels when the intent reasoner collapses a multi-panel caption into one panel before the specification is built; mismatched infill at mask boundaries in mask-completion; and "literal skeleton" outputs that follow a sketch's layout faithfully but omit the concrete illustrative content (e.g., a worked example) the ground truth includes. No funding is disclosed.

## Learnings

The core transferable idea — replace free-text accumulation of correction instructions with typed, structured edits written to a shared specification object that every round rebuilds its prompt from — is a directly reusable pattern for any iterative LLM-agent refinement loop, not just figure generation: the paper's own diagnosis of why free-text correction degrades (successive natural-language addenda introduce contradictions that the generator "absorbs silently," with no single round looking anomalous) is a specific, mechanistic account of a failure mode that likely recurs in any multi-round agentic editing task (code repair, document revision, structured-data cleanup), and the fix generalizes directly.

The "diversity-driven plan exploration" mechanism — generating several structurally distinct candidate framings up front and letting a judge pick the best starting point, rather than committing to one framing and repairing it — is a reusable design principle wherever a generation task has multiple qualitatively different valid solution shapes and a bad initial shape cannot be locally patched into a good one; the ablation evidence here (the single largest individual-mechanism contribution, 8.56 points) is a useful data point that plan-level diversity can matter more than additional refinement rounds within a single plan.

The directive-vs-scalar critic distinction (per-dimension diagnostics with named defects and suggested fixes, rather than a single quality score) is a specific, testable claim about what makes an LLM-as-judge component useful as a training/refinement signal rather than merely an evaluation signal — a scalar score tells you how well you did but not what to change next, and the ablation (5.04-point drop when reverted) offers direct evidence that actionable diagnostic structure, not just judge presence, drives the gain.

## Verification

The paper's verification practices are uneven across its two claimed contributions. For CraftEditor's editable-output claims, verification is genuinely strong: a three-VLM ensemble (Gemini, GPT-5.4, Doubao) spanning multiple vendors scores every output, with a retry rule for low-confidence judgments, and the composition-phase critic is explicitly hybrid, combining VLM judgment with programmatic structural checks (arrow-endpoint accuracy, element overlap) specifically because the paper notes VLM evaluation alone tends to miss these — a concrete acknowledgment that a single judge type has blind spots, addressed architecturally rather than merely disclosed. CraftBench's dataset construction is also unusually rigorous for a benchmark paper: a seven-stage automated-plus-manual quality pipeline, and every reference-conditioned sample requires unanimous agreement from three independent graduate annotators, with disagreement triggering revision rather than exclusion or majority-vote acceptance. The main Crafter generation results, by contrast, rest on a single VLM judge per benchmark (Gemini 3.5 Flash for CraftBench, Gemini 3.1 Pro for PaperBanana-Bench) with no cross-vendor judge validated the way CraftEditor's results are, and the blind human-agreement check for this judge (72% agreement, Cohen's kappa = 0.58 on 60 comparisons) is disclosed transparently but represents only moderate, not strong, agreement by standard interpretation of the kappa statistic, a nuance the paper's framing ("confirms that the automatic score is a reliable proxy") somewhat overstates relative to what a kappa in the 0.41-0.60 range conventionally indicates. No repeated-trial variance is reported anywhere in the main results: each benchmark is explicitly run "end-to-end once per method," so none of the reported win-rate gaps (including the ablation deltas) carry a confidence interval or indication of run-to-run stability.

## Field context

Crafter positions itself against two families it reviews directly: code-generation methods (TikZ-based systems like AutomaTikZ and TikZilla) that produce editable but visually plain diagrams, and agentic raster-generation pipelines (PaperBanana, AutoFigure, Paper2SysArch, and poster/education-focused systems) that produce visually rich but uneditable output and, per this paper's own controlled comparison, generalize poorly beyond the single figure type and text-only input condition each was designed around. Its central architectural bet — an executor-agnostic orchestration layer with all task-specific behavior living in agent prompts rather than pipeline code — echoes, in a completely different application domain, the general "harness matters as much as the underlying model" argument this paper's own related-work section attributes to Anthropic's engineering writing on agent harnesses; within this corpus, the same underlying question of "where should structure and correction logic live in a multi-agent pipeline" is examined from different angles in [reviews/2605.22502.md](2605.22502.md) (which argues for compiling a fixed procedure directly into model weights rather than any runtime orchestrator at all) and [reviews/2512.04388.md](2512.04388.md) (which trains a small model to generate the orchestration plan itself); Crafter's answer — keep a lightweight, prompt-configurable orchestration harness and treat the underlying generator as a pluggable black box — is a third distinct point in that same design space, optimized here for a setting (rich, structured visual composition with locally-correctable errors) where neither weight-compilation nor a single learned orchestrator plan is the obvious fit. No prior work specifically on scientific figure generation, diagram synthesis, or VLM-as-judge evaluation for visual tasks appears elsewhere in this reviewed corpus, so this paper introduces a genuinely new sub-topic to the collection rather than extending one already covered.

## Critical discussion

The paper's empirical design has real strengths: a controlled comparison holding the image-generation backbone and vision-language model fixed across all agentic baselines isolates the orchestration design's contribution from raw generator capability, a clean per-mechanism ablation attributes the aggregate gain to specific, individually-justified components rather than an opaque end-to-end number, and the executor-swap experiment (Nano Banana 2 vs. Pro) directly tests the claimed backbone-agnosticism rather than merely asserting it. The documented failure-case analysis (Appendix L) is also a good-faith addition rarely seen in benchmark papers of this kind: it names specific mechanisms responsible for specific failure modes (the intent reasoner collapsing multi-panel captions; a critic that checks structure but not mask-boundary content continuity) rather than presenting only success cases.

The main limitation is evaluation dependence on a single, same-vendor judge for the primary generation results, compounded by a headline generator lineup (Nano Banana 2/Pro) that is itself from the same vendor (Google/Gemini) as both the CraftBench judge (Gemini 3.5 Flash) and the vision-dependent agents inside Crafter's own pipeline (Gemini 3.1 Pro) — unlike the CraftEditor evaluation, which deliberately uses a three-vendor judge ensemble specifically to guard against exactly this kind of concern, no analogous cross-vendor check exists for the main Table 2 results that make up the paper's central "Crafter beats every baseline on every dimension" claim. The human-validation check that does exist for this judge (kappa = 0.58, 72% agreement, N=60) is a moderate, not strong, level of agreement by conventional standards, and given that every headline percentage in the paper flows through this single judge with no run-to-run variance reported anywhere, the precision implied by point differences in Table 2 (e.g., Crafter's 50.34 vs. PaperBanana's 35.96 overall on CraftBench, a comfortable 14-point gap, versus the narrower 3.73-point gap between CraftEditor's ablation variants in Table 4) should be read with some caution about how much of the reported gap reflects a stable underlying difference versus single-run judge noise. A second, smaller concern: the paper's flagship claim that Crafter "generalizes across figure types and input conditions" is demonstrated relative to the specific baselines and benchmark tasks tested here, but CraftBench itself — despite genuinely more diverse coverage than prior single-type benchmarks — still skews toward academic and poster figures with infographics as the smallest, least-represented category (30 of 279 samples), a scope limitation the paper's own Limitations section names directly rather than glossing over. What remains solidly supported: within this specific benchmark suite and this specific set of baselines under a controlled backbone comparison, the four-role harness abstraction with typed structured edits, plan-level diversity, and a directive (not scalar) critic each independently and measurably improve figure-generation quality over both a standalone generator and a narrower agentic pipeline, and the raster-to-editable-SVG conversion component is validated by a properly multi-vendor-judged comparison against the two most directly comparable prior systems.
