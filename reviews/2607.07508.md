Citation: Hou, Z., Li, Y., Tang, J., Dong, Y. Single-Rollout Asynchronous Optimization for Agentic Reinforcement Learning. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07508v1

## What this paper is about

Reinforcement learning for LLM agents is moving from synchronous training (generate a whole batch of rollouts, then update) toward asynchronous training (start updating as soon as any rollout finishes), because agentic tasks have highly variable-length trajectories and synchronous batches waste GPU time waiting for the slowest one. But the most popular RL algorithm for LLMs, GRPO, estimates how good a response was by comparing it against a group of other responses to the same prompt, which structurally requires waiting for that whole group to finish, defeating much of asynchrony's benefit and getting worse as the group's slowest member drifts further from the current policy. This paper's proposal, Single-rollout Asynchronous Optimization (SAO), replaces group-comparison with a single rollout per prompt plus a trained value model to estimate the baseline instead, paired with a simplified, more aggressive token-level clipping scheme to control the resulting off-policy drift, and shows it trains stably for about a thousand steps where a naive asynchronous GRPO setup collapses.

## Extended summary

The paper identifies two specific problems with running GRPO-style RL asynchronously. First, "policy lag": because the rollout-generating model can be updated multiple times while a single long trajectory is still being produced, tracking an exact old-policy probability for importance sampling would require keeping an infeasible history of every intermediate checkpoint. SAO's fix, Direct Importance Sampling (DIS), sidesteps this by using the rollout engine's own logged probabilities directly as the importance-sampling denominator (dropping the usual separate "old policy" term entirely) and applying a stricter double-sided token-level clipping window, masking out of the gradient any token whose importance ratio falls outside a fixed trust region rather than only clipping one side as standard PPO/GRPO does. Second, "group-wise sampling mismatch": GRPO's advantage estimate depends on averaging over several rollouts per prompt, which both forces training to wait for the group's slowest member and is structurally incompatible with settings (online learning, many real agentic environments) that only ever provide one trajectory of feedback per prompt. SAO's fix is to drop group comparison entirely and use a single rollout per prompt with a trained value-model baseline (effectively a PPO/GAE-style setup rather than GRPO's group-relative one), which trades GRPO's zero-variance-by-construction baseline for one that depends on having an accurate, fast-adapting value model.

To make single-rollout training practical, the paper introduces three specific value-model design choices. "Faster value update" runs K=2 gradient updates on the critic for every one update on the policy, on the reasoning that an inaccurate, lagging value estimate is the primary source of noisy advantages and destructive updates in this regime. "Frozen-attention" value training freezes the value model's attention layers and only updates its mixture-of-experts projection layers, motivated by an empirical observation that the critic's gradient norms are dramatically larger and less stable specifically in the attention layers, on the hypothesis that pretrained attention weights already know how to attend to the relevant tokens. A "skip-observation" token-level GAE modifies the standard Bellman-error recursion to bridge directly from the last token of one agent action to the first token of the next action, explicitly skipping over environment-observation tokens the model itself did not generate, to avoid injecting noise from asking the value function to predict environment-state values it has no way to have learned.

Experiments fine-tune Qwen3-30B-A3B (initialized via SFT on GPT-OSS-120B-generated tool-integrated-reasoning data for the math-with-Python setting) and evaluate on four math-reasoning benchmarks (AIME2025, BeyondAIME, HMMT Nov 2025, IMOAnswerBench) plus SWE-Bench Verified for agentic coding. SAO outperforms both a vanilla GRPO baseline (which the paper reports collapses at roughly 160 training steps, with reported scores being its best pre-collapse performance) and an improved "GRPO (w/ DIS)" baseline that trains stably but plateaus lower, reaching 97.3% on AIME2025 (vs. 93.5% for GRPO+DIS), 74.8% on BeyondAIME, 88.3% on HMMT, 74.0% on IMOAnswerBench, and 29.8% on SWE-Bench Verified (vs. 27.0% for GRPO+DIS and a 23.0% untrained baseline). A performance-over-training-steps plot shows SAO and GRPO+DIS tracking similarly for roughly the first 400 steps before diverging, which the paper attributes to the single-rollout-plus-value-model design's advantages compounding over a longer training horizon rather than showing up immediately.

Ablations (Tables 3-5) isolate each design choice's individual contribution: removing the faster value update (single critic update per policy step) drops AIME2025 from 97.3% to 95.0%; removing frozen-attention (full-parameter value training) drops it further, to 90.6%, and Figure 4(b) shows this variant's critic gradient norms are visibly larger and noisier throughout training; a vanilla VAPO baseline (a prior value-based RL method, without DIS) collapses similarly to vanilla GRPO; and a simple running-mean-of-recent-rewards baseline in place of a trained value model reaches only 79.8% on AIME2025, well below SAO, offered as evidence that a genuinely trained value model, not merely any single-rollout baseline-subtraction scheme, is doing real work. A further appendix ablation compares token-level value/advantage estimation against two step-level alternatives (averaging or using only the last token's value per conversation turn), finding token-level estimation outperforms both by a moderate margin (89.8% vs. 85.8-87.3% on AIME2025 at a matched 400 training steps), attributed to token-level supervision providing a finer-grained learning signal for tracking logical transitions within long reasoning trajectories.

A separate demonstration targets the paper's structural claim that single-rollout methods are specifically suited to non-stationary, online settings where group-based methods cannot operate at all: a simulated online creative-writing task where an LLM-judge-assigned reward criterion (matching one of several requested writing styles) is switched partway through training to favor a different style, three times in sequence. SAO's value-based critic tracks each shift and recovers quickly, while a running-mean-reward baseline (chosen specifically because, unlike GRPO, it is at least compatible with single-rollout online feedback) shows a pronounced adaptation lag attributable to its reward-history window still being biased toward the just-abandoned style. The paper explicitly frames this scenario, rather than the main math/coding benchmarks, as the setting where single-rollout training's structural advantage over group-based methods is most direct, since group-wise baselines are not just less efficient here but are not applicable at all.

The paper's own stated limitations note that all experiments use a single backbone (Qwen3-30B-A3B) on large-scale agentic reasoning, coding, and simulated-writing tasks, so conclusions may not transfer to smaller models, non-agentic RLHF with dense rewards, or short-rollout settings; that SAO's dependence on a trained value model and on rollout log-probabilities requires infrastructure able to reliably preserve token-level behavior probabilities during asynchronous generation; and that the online-learning demonstration uses a controlled simulated preference shift, with real user-facing deployment needing stronger safeguards and privacy review. The abstract additionally states that SAO was "successfully deployed in the agentic RL pipeline for training the open GLM-5.2 model (750B-A40B)," a claim not accompanied by any GLM-5.2-specific results, metrics, or comparison anywhere in the body of the paper.

## Learnings

The specific empirical observation that a value model's attention-layer gradients are dramatically larger and noisier than its MoE-layer gradients during RL training, and that simply freezing the attention parameters while training only the MoE projections meaningfully stabilizes critic optimization (a 6.7-point AIME2025 gap in the ablation), is a concrete, transferable diagnostic and fix for anyone training a value/critic model on top of a pretrained MoE LLM backbone: it suggests the instability in this setting is architecturally localized rather than diffuse, and that a pretrained model's attention patterns may already be adequate for a downstream value-estimation task without further tuning.

The "skip-observation" GAE modification, explicitly bridging the Bellman-error recursion across environment-generated tokens the policy did not produce rather than computing a value difference across that boundary as standard token-level GAE would, is a specific, reusable design pattern for any agentic RL setup with interleaved action/observation trajectories: it names a concrete failure mode (asking a value function to predict the value of an external environment state it has no basis for estimating) and offers a direct, mechanical fix rather than a general architectural workaround.

The paper's demonstration that a single-rollout, value-based training scheme is not merely a less-efficient substitute for group-based methods but is structurally necessary in settings that only ever provide one trajectory of feedback per input (illustrated concretely via the online, shifting-reward-preference writing task where GRPO-style baselines cannot be applied at all) is a useful reframing for evaluating advantage-estimation methods generally: the relevant comparison in such settings is not "single-rollout vs. group-based" on equal footing, but "does a specific single-rollout baseline (a trained value model vs. a running-mean reward) track a genuinely non-stationary reward signal well," which the paper shows are meaningfully different in practice.

## Verification

The paper backs its central design choices with targeted ablations rather than only a headline comparison: each of the three value-model interventions (faster value update, frozen-attention training, skip-observation GAE) has a corresponding controlled variant that removes just that one piece, and the resulting performance drops (2.3, 6.7, and a separate token-vs-step-level GAE comparison of roughly 2-4 points) are each individually reported rather than presented only as a combined "with vs. without everything" contrast. The frozen-attention claim is further supported by a direct mechanistic measurement (critic gradient norms over training, Figure 4b) rather than resting on the downstream accuracy numbers alone, and the DIS clipping claim is similarly supported by a training-dynamics plot of the token-level clip ratio (Figure 4c) showing the baseline VAPO's near-zero clip ratio failing to gate divergent updates before its reported collapse at roughly 90 steps.

Several verification gaps limit how confidently the specific numeric margins should be read. All reported benchmark comparisons appear to come from a single training run per configuration; the paper reports averaging across multiple evaluation rollouts at test time (16 runs for AIME2025/HMMT/IMOAnswerBench, 4 for BeyondAIME) to reduce evaluation-time variance, but this does not address training-run-to-training-run variance, which is a well-documented source of noise in RL training generally and is not addressed here via multiple training seeds or repeated runs for either SAO or its baselines. All experiments use a single 30B-parameter backbone (Qwen3-30B-A3B), so the reported margins and the specific failure/collapse points for the baselines (approximately 160 steps for vanilla GRPO, approximately 90 steps for vanilla VAPO) are established for one model family and scale rather than shown to generalize. Most notably, the abstract's claim that SAO was "successfully deployed" in training the substantially larger, differently-architected GLM-5.2 model (750B-A40B) is not accompanied anywhere in the paper by GLM-5.2-specific metrics, an ablation, or even a qualitative description of what "successful" meant in that deployment, so this specific claim currently rests entirely on the authors' assertion rather than on evidence presented in this paper.

## Field context

The paper positions SAO against two adjacent lines of work it discusses directly: algorithmic refinements to GRPO/PPO-style objectives for synchronous RL (sequence-level importance weighting, adaptive clipping, VAPO's smoother alternatives to hard clipping), which it argues have not addressed the asynchronous, policy-lag setting specifically; and systems-level asynchronous RL infrastructure for LLMs (AReaL's fully decoupled rollout-training pipeline with staleness-aware PPO-style updates, ROLL Flash's fine-grained rollout-train decoupling, and MobileRL's difficulty-adaptive GRPO variant for mobile GUI agents), which it argues have prioritized throughput and system efficiency over training stability and effectiveness. Its stated point of differentiation is algorithmic rather than systems-level: showing that group-based advantage estimation is structurally, not just practically, mismatched to asynchronous and single-feedback-per-prompt settings, and that a properly-stabilized single-rollout, value-based alternative can outperform an already-stabilized group-based baseline (GRPO with the same DIS clipping mechanism applied) rather than merely matching it more cheaply.

## Critical discussion

The paper's ablation structure is a genuine strength: each of its three specific value-model interventions is tested in isolation with a corresponding mechanistic measurement (gradient norms, clip ratios, explained variance) rather than only a downstream accuracy delta, which makes the individual causal claims (frozen attention stabilizes the critic; faster value updates reduce advantage noise; skipping observation tokens in the GAE recursion avoids a specific class of noise) more credible than a single combined ablation would allow. The online-learning demonstration is a well-targeted, structurally clean illustration of exactly the setting (single-trajectory-per-prompt feedback) the paper argues motivates the whole approach, rather than a generic diversity showcase.

Three points temper how far the specific numbers should be read. First, the abstract's framing prominently features SAO's deployment in training GLM-5.2 (750B-A40B) as validating evidence, but the paper presents no metrics, comparisons, or even qualitative detail about that deployment anywhere in its body; as written, this specific claim is an unsubstantiated assertion rather than a demonstrated result, and a reader should not treat it as evidence beyond what the Qwen3-30B-A3B experiments themselves establish. Second, every reported comparison rests on a single training run per configuration on a single 30B-parameter backbone, so the specific numeric margins (e.g., SAO's 3.8-point AIME2025 edge over GRPO+DIS) and the specific reported collapse points for baselines are not yet shown to be stable across seeds or to generalize across model scales or families. Third, the ablation isolating "SAO (w/ DIS only)" against "GRPO (+DIS)" shows a comparatively modest gap (94.2% vs. 93.5% on AIME2025), suggesting that a meaningful share of SAO's full 3.8-point headline advantage over GRPO+DIS specifically depends on the additional value-model machinery (faster updates, frozen attention, skip-observation GAE) stacked on top of the core single-rollout replacement, rather than from replacing group-wise sampling with single-rollout sampling alone; the paper's own ablations make this decomposition visible, which is a strength, but the headline framing (single-rollout as the central idea) somewhat understates how much of the measured gain depends on the accompanying value-model engineering.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the specific, mechanistically-measured claims behind each individual design choice (attention-layer gradient instability in the value model, and its mitigation by freezing those parameters; a near-zero clip ratio failing to prevent training collapse under the VAPO baseline; a running-mean reward baseline lagging behind a trained value model specifically under a genuinely non-stationary reward signal) are each backed by a direct, isolated measurement rather than resting on the aggregate benchmark numbers alone; and the qualitative case that group-wise advantage estimation is structurally, not merely practically, incompatible with single-trajectory-per-prompt feedback settings is well-illustrated by the online learning demonstration, independent of how the specific math/coding benchmark margins would hold up under further replication.
