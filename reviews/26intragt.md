Citation: Ma, F., Rao, Z., Li, X., Chen, Z., Sun, H., Zhao, Y., Chen, X., and Xiang, Z. IntrAgent: An LLM Agent for Content-Grounded Information Retrieval through Literature Review. Proceedings of the 64th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL 2026).

## What this paper is about

Pulling one specific fact out of a scientific paper, such as "what laser wavelength did they use," is a task standard retrieval-augmented generation handles surprisingly badly, because chunking a paper by semantic similarity to the query ignores the paper's own structural organization and can retrieve the wrong passage or bury the right one in noise. This paper carefully defines this specific task (IntraView: retrieval strictly and faithfully grounded in one supplied paper, with an explicit requirement to say "not found" rather than guess), builds an agent, IntrAgent, that mimics how a person actually skims a paper for a fact, first guessing which section probably has the answer using the paper's own heading structure, then reading sections in that priority order while explicitly checking after each one whether enough has been gathered, and introduces a new 315-question, five-domain benchmark, IntraBench, to measure this. Across seven different backbone language models and a wide range of RAG and literature-agent baselines, IntrAgent wins by a substantial and consistent margin.

## Extended summary

IntraView is framed as a content question-answering (CQA) problem but with two features that distinguish it from standard CQA and from other literature-related tasks: the model receives the full literature rather than a pre-selected or processed chunk, with the relevant information potentially anywhere in the document or absent entirely; and answers must be faithfully grounded in the provided content alone, explicitly excluding the kind of author-intent or external-domain-knowledge reasoning that a related task like PeerQA allows. It is also explicitly distinguished from literature-search agents such as PaperQA2 and SciMaster, which are designed to answer general scientific questions by searching across external sources rather than extracting facts strictly from one supplied paper; the paper argues, and later demonstrates, that these agents degrade into RAG-like static retrieval pipelines once their external search capability is disabled and they are given only the target paper.

IntrAgent implements a two-stage pipeline modeled on human reading behavior. In the Section Ranking stage, the input literature (typically a PDF) is converted to Markdown with structural section markers using minerU, a layout-detection model; an LLM then infers the hierarchical parent-child relationships among the extracted headings to build a section tree (a "hierarchy preservation" step), pruning redundant nodes; finally, an LLM reasons over this heading hierarchy together with the research question to produce a ranked ordering of sections by estimated relevance. In the Iterative Reading stage, the agent repeatedly chooses among three actions: accessing the next section in ranked order, extracting query-relevant details (terminology, numbers, experimental results, comparative or causal statements) from the current section into a short-term memory anchored to source sentences, and checking whether the accumulated details are sufficient to answer the question. The sufficiency check is explicitly designed to prevent both premature stopping (since relevant evidence can be distributed across multiple non-adjacent sections) and hallucination (by requiring the agent to continue reading rather than guess when evidence is incomplete). A confidence-level parameter (conservative, balanced, or aggressive) lets a user trade off thoroughness against computational overhead. Once the loop terminates, a final answer is synthesized from all accumulated extracted details.

IntraBench consists of 315 test instances across five STEM domains (physics, earth science, public health, engineering, material science), built by domain-expert team members who each curate a pool of familiar peer-reviewed papers, randomly sample from that pool to reduce selection bias, and write natural, expert-style questions in four categories (study subject and experimental setup; data characteristics and collection; technical approach and details; conclusions and results). Each question is paired with six manually constructed multiple-choice options, including standardized "All of the above" and "None of the above" options that may themselves be correct, with distractors drawn from paper-adjacent values or field conventions. To evaluate free-form short answers (which methods must generate without ever seeing the multiple-choice options) against this format without relying on surface-level string-matching metrics unsuited to domain-specific terminology, a separate LLM (GPT-4.1 by default) maps each generated answer to the closest multiple-choice option, falling back to "None of the above" when no confident match exists; this mapping step is validated against independent human-expert annotation on a 65-instance physics subset, with reported correctness agreement of 63-65 out of 65 across three candidate mapping models.

IntrAgent is compared against a broad and credentialed set of baselines: several vanilla RAG variants across three different embedding backbones, an Anthropic-style contextual RAG variant, and three RAG extensions targeting multi-hop reasoning, re-ranking, or long-context retrieval (DRAGIN, R²AG, LongRAG), alongside four literature-focused agents (LUMOS, PaperQA2, Agentic-Hybrid-RAG, and SciMaster, the last of which the paper notes reports a strong 32.1 score on Humanity's Last Exam, making it a genuinely credentialed rather than strawman baseline). Across seven backbone LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, DeepSeek-R1, o3, o4-mini, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Llama-3.1-70B), IntrAgent's cross-domain macro-average accuracy ranges from 68.8% to 75.9%, beating the strongest baseline for each backbone by 7.4 to 21.0 percentage points (13.2 points on average, per the abstract). The paper attributes this gain specifically to targeted evidence selection rather than simply providing more context, and argues that the literature-search agent baselines underperform mechanistically because they are not designed to work from a single supplied document. A worked example illustrates this concretely: given a question about the excitation laser wavelength used in a surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) experiment, vanilla RAG retrieves the wrong chunk and answers incorrectly, while IntrAgent's section-ranking reasoning correctly identifies the "Experiment Setup" section as most likely to contain the answer, and its sufficiency check confirms the extracted detail (785 nm) directly addresses the question before terminating.

Ablations isolate each component's contribution. Removing hierarchy preservation (ranking raw, unstructured headings instead of the inferred hierarchy) causes a clear accuracy drop across all three tested backbones. Comparing confidence levels shows more aggressive reading uses fewer iterations and tokens but lower accuracy (58.9% conservative, 68.3% balanced, 62.7% aggressive), with the notable and somewhat counter-intuitive finding that the most thorough ("conservative") mode, despite reading the most sections, performs worst, which the paper attributes to the same performance-degrades-with-long-context phenomenon documented elsewhere in the RAG literature. Removing the sufficiency check entirely (accessing only the top-ranked section) causes a dramatic accuracy collapse on the physics subset, from 75.4% to 32.2%, with manual failure analysis identifying two distinct failure modes: incomplete retrieval, where evidence scattered across multiple sections cannot be found in the single top-ranked section, correctly triggering an honest "None of the above" in many cases; and outright hallucination, where the agent answers confidently despite insufficient support.

Robustness checks address two further questions. Comparing GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, and DeepSeek-R1 as the answer-mapping model against human ground truth on the physics subset finds GPT-4.1 achieves the best agreement (65/65) and is adopted as the default; a broader combinatorial check (three backbones times three mapping models) confirms IntrAgent consistently and substantially outperforms vanilla RAG regardless of which mapping model is used to grade answers. A separate stress test rewrites one physics paper's section headings into three deliberately degraded styles (a beginner-level rewrite, a highly noisy version, and a stylistically bizarre "Shakespearean" rendition); IntrAgent's accuracy drops only modestly, from 89.2% on the original headings to an average of 84.6% across the three degraded variants, suggesting the section-ranking mechanism reasons about a section's likely role rather than pattern-matching on exact conventional heading text.

The paper's stated limitations are that IntrAgent and IntraBench currently address text-based retrieval only, excluding non-textual modalities (plots, figures, tables) that the authors acknowledge often encode essential quantitative or structural information, and that the current benchmark does not yet include all article types, notably excluding review papers.

## Learnings

When an answer can be scattered across non-adjacent sections of a structured document, an explicit, separately reasoned "have I gathered enough to answer" stopping decision is not a minor refinement but can be the single most load-bearing design component in a retrieval pipeline: removing it collapsed accuracy from 75.4% to 32.2% in one tested domain, a far larger effect than removing structural heading information or changing the reading-thoroughness setting. Any retrieval system operating over multi-section documents should treat when to stop reading as a first-class, explicitly checked decision rather than an implicit byproduct of a fixed retrieval budget.

More thorough reading is not a safe default improvement: a "conservative" mode that read the most sections and consumed the most tokens scored worse than a "balanced" mode that read less, echoing a documented pattern in the broader RAG literature where performance degrades in very long contexts. This is worth treating as a specific, checkable caution distinct from the usual context-length/attention-degradation framing, since here the extra material comes from the source document itself, already known to be topically relevant, rather than from noisy retrieved chunks elsewhere in a corpus.

A structure-aware ranking mechanism that reasons about a document's hierarchical organization, rather than matching on exact conventional heading text, can remain robust even when a document's headings are deliberately degraded into non-standard styles, with only a modest accuracy drop across several intentionally corrupted heading rewrites. This is a specific, transferable robustness test (deliberately degrading a real document's structure and checking whether a structure-aware method still finds the right content) worth replicating for any tool making a similar structure-awareness claim, particularly on document types with less standardized formatting than the STEM papers tested here.

## Verification

Verification appears in two distinct places in this paper. First, the agent itself has a built-in, ablation-validated anti-hallucination mechanism: the sufficiency check is explicitly designed to prevent premature, underdetermined answers, and its removal is shown to cause a specific, large, and mechanistically explained failure (a disaggregated breakdown into incomplete-retrieval failures, which correctly and honestly resolve to "None of the above," versus outright hallucination failures, which do not). Second, the benchmark's own evaluation metric, an LLM mapping free-form generated answers onto predefined multiple-choice options, is validated directly against independent human-expert annotation rather than assumed reliable by construction, with reported agreement of 63-65 out of 65 on a physics subset across three candidate mapping models. This human validation, however, covers only one of the benchmark's five domains; the mapping's reliability for earth science, public health, engineering, and material science questions is not independently confirmed against human judgment in this paper, leaving an open question about how far the physics-domain validation generalizes to the rest of the benchmark that the headline accuracy numbers depend on.

## Field context

IntraView is explicitly and carefully distinguished from several adjacent task families: standard content question-answering, which typically operates over a pre-selected or generic knowledge chunk rather than a full, structurally complex scientific document; RAG-based scientific QA, criticized here for relying on surface-level semantic-similarity chunking that ignores document structure and often misaligns domain-specific query terminology with the relevant passage; literature-search agents such as PaperQA2 and SciMaster, designed for open-ended scientific question answering via external search rather than retrieval strictly grounded in one supplied document, and shown here to degrade into static RAG-like pipelines once forced into the single-document setting; and PeerQA, a related content-grounded QA task that, unlike IntraView, explicitly permits reasoning about author intent or external domain knowledge beyond the paper itself. The comparison against SciMaster is a meaningfully strong baseline choice specifically because SciMaster is independently credentialed via a reported score on Humanity's Last Exam, so its underperformance on IntraView relative to its general scientific-QA strength supports the paper's core argument that the two task families genuinely require different capabilities rather than one simply subsuming the other. Within this corpus, this paper is a directly relevant, working instance of Arc 3 (reasoning over a literature, not top-k RAG; being in context is not being used), applied here to single-document extraction rather than whole-corpus reasoning.

## Critical discussion

The baseline sweep is unusually broad and well-chosen for this genre of paper: multiple RAG variants across three embedding backbones, several recent RAG extensions explicitly built for multi-hop reasoning or long-context retrieval rather than weak strawmen, and multiple literature-search agents including one (SciMaster) independently validated as strong via an external, well-known benchmark, all evaluated under an identical protocol across seven backbone LLMs. This is a genuinely fair and thorough comparison, directly addressing the common concern about crippled or missing frontier-plus-tools baselines; here the baselines are neither crippled nor a strawman set.

The evaluation metric depends on an LLM-based answer-to-option mapping validated against human judgment on only 65 of the benchmark's 315 instances, all from a single domain (physics); the reported high agreement there is reassuring for that domain specifically, but no equivalent human-validation check is reported for the other four domains, so the reliability of the headline accuracy numbers outside physics rests on an unverified generalization of a single-domain validation (narrow-evaluation, specific to the metric-validation step). Relatedly, the default mapping model (GPT-4.1) belongs to the same model family as one of the seven evaluated backbones, and that specific configuration (GPT-4.1-backed IntrAgent) achieves the single highest accuracy in the entire main results table; the mapping step is validated against independent human judgment rather than trusted blindly, and Table 6's cross-mapping-model check does show IntrAgent's advantage over RAG holds regardless of which model performs the mapping, but the paper does not report whether the mapping model shows any measurable leniency specifically toward same-family-generated answers relative to different-family ones, a natural and directly checkable extension of the robustness analysis already presented (same-family-judge). The dramatic sufficiency-check ablation result is reported for only one domain and one specific backbone/mapping-model combination, so while the qualitative conclusion, that the sufficiency check matters a great deal, is well supported by the case shown and its accompanying failure-mode analysis, the precise magnitude of its contribution elsewhere in the benchmark is not directly demonstrated.

What remains well supported: the core comparative claim, that IntrAgent outperforms a wide and genuinely credentialed set of RAG and literature-agent baselines across all five domains and seven backbone models, rests on a large, diverse, and fairly constructed comparison under an identical evaluation protocol, and is the paper's best-evidenced result. The mechanistic explanation for the gain, structure-aware section prioritization combined with an explicit, ablation-validated stopping criterion rather than simply more context, is directly supported by the ablation studies and the worked example, and the heading-degradation stress test is a specific, well-designed check that directly addresses a natural skeptical question about the method's reliance on conventional formatting, rather than leaving it untested.

## Relevance to us

This paper is directly relevant to Arc 3 and B08 (whole-literature reasoning, not top-k RAG; being in context is not being used), offering a concrete, working demonstration of the design pattern that bet is oriented toward. IntrAgent shows, with a specific and legible worked example, that chunking a paper by semantic similarity to a query can fail on exactly the kind of fact a domain expert would locate immediately and correctly by reasoning about document structure (laser wavelength belongs in an experimental setup section), and its two-stage separation of "have I found the right place" (structure-aware section ranking) from "have I found enough" (an explicitly checked, separately ablated sufficiency decision) is a directly adaptable template for any physics-paper-grounded extraction task we might build, such as pulling a stated approximation regime, a derivation assumption, or a reported experimental parameter out of a specific paper. The ablations tell us in advance which components are load-bearing: the sufficiency check dominates (its removal caused by far the largest measured collapse), with hierarchy preservation a smaller but still real contributor, which should inform where we invest engineering effort if we build a similar pipeline for physics literature. The heading-degradation robustness test is a specific, transferable validation idea worth replicating directly on physics papers, which often have even less standardized structure than the STEM domains tested here (a key assumption or approximation might appear in a footnote, an appendix, or inline within a derivation rather than under a clearly labeled section), to check whether an analogous structure-aware ranking mechanism still reasons about role rather than exact heading text. The counter-intuitive finding that the most thorough reading mode performed worst is a specific caution worth carrying into our own retrieval-heavy physics tools: pulling in more of a source document is not a safe default even when the material is topically on-target, and an explicit, separately tuned stopping decision is a component worth evaluating on its own rather than assuming a larger reading budget is strictly better.
