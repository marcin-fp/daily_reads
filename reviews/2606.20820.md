Citation: Zhou, Z., Ye, Z., Chen, Z., Li, B., Liu, F. CELEUS: Certifiable and Efficient LLM Evaluation via E-Processes. ICML 2026 Workshop on Hypothesis Testing, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20820v2

## What this paper is about

When you evaluate an LLM by sequentially testing samples and stopping once your confidence interval gets tight enough, the standard statistical guarantee ("95% confidence") can quietly break: repeatedly checking and updating a confidence interval, then using that same monitoring to decide when to stop, is exactly the kind of data-dependent peeking that invalidates a one-shot confidence interval's coverage promise. CELEUS fixes this by building the confidence interval out of "e-processes" — a sequential-testing tool whose validity guarantee holds no matter when or how often you check it — while also making the evaluation efficient: it prioritizes evaluating the most informative, hardest-to-predict samples first, and fills in cheap "surrogate" estimates (produced by a much smaller model) for the samples it hasn't gotten to yet, cutting the number of expensive evaluations needed by roughly half.

## Extended summary

The paper targets certifiable evaluation: given a data pool, sequentially evaluate samples and maintain a confidence interval (CI) for the model's true population risk, stopping once the CI reaches a target precision. It identifies a specific gap in the closest prior method, Cer-Eval (Wang et al.): Cer-Eval's certification relies on adaptive concentration inequalities proven for a fixed partition of the data, but the practical algorithm repeatedly learns and updates that partition from the same evaluated data — a form of double dipping that breaks the assumption underlying the proof, so the claimed coverage can fail once the CI is monitored and used to decide when to stop, rather than checked only at one pre-specified endpoint.

CELEUS instead builds its CIs from e-processes, whose anytime-valid guarantee (the probability of ever crossing a rejection threshold, at any stopping time, stays bounded by the target error rate) follows from a martingale-style construction and requires only that evaluation scores be bounded (satisfiable by normalizing or clipping). Making this efficient, rather than merely valid, is the paper's core technical contribution: a naive risk signal computed under non-uniform sample selection (prioritizing informative samples) would be biased, so CELEUS constructs a per-round inferential signal with two parts — a surrogate-completed estimate of the pool-wide risk (using observed risks for evaluated samples and a smaller surrogate model's approximate risk scores for the rest), plus an inverse-probability-weighted correction that turns the newly-revealed sample's surrogate-prediction residual into an unbiased estimate of the total remaining residual. The paper proves (Theorem 3) that this combined signal is conditionally unbiased for the finite-pool risk regardless of the (non-degenerate) sampling rule used, which is the property that lets it be plugged into a standard hedged-capital e-process construction to yield valid, shrinking CIs (Theorem 5), and further proves (Theorem 4) the variance-optimal oracle sampling rule that motivates the paper's practical "uncertainty-guided" sampling heuristic (assign higher selection probability to samples whose observed-vs-surrogate discrepancy is likely to be large). A separate Hoeffding-based correction (Theorem 6) lifts the finite-pool-risk CI to a population-risk CI when the pool is drawn i.i.d. from the target distribution, with the correction shrinking toward negligible as the pool size grows; the paper notes in an appendix that for non-i.i.d. pools, this specific correction can be swapped for other concentration or generalization bounds suited to the pool's dependence structure, while the finite-pool e-process guarantee itself is unaffected.

Experiments evaluate three benchmarks (SST-2 sentiment classification, AG News topic classification, MMLU multiple-choice) across six surrogate-target model pairs spanning model-family and generation axes (e.g., Llama-3-8B as surrogate for Llama-3-70B, DeepSeek-67B, Mixtral-8x7B, and Qwen2.5-72B; Llama-2-7B as surrogate for Llama-3-70B and DeepSeek-67B), with all surrogates being fixed 7-8B open-source models used with no task-specific training or fine-tuning. Three research questions are tested. (RQ1, coverage) A targeted diagnostic Monte Carlo experiment (50 repeats x 1000 trials each, in a constructed synthetic setting designed to expose Cer-Eval's double-dipping failure mode) finds Cer-Eval's empirical miscoverage rate is 0.161, more than three times its nominal 0.05 target, while every e-process-based method tested (including CELEUS) stays within the nominal level (0.021-0.030). (RQ2, efficiency) Across SST-2, MMLU, and AG News, CELEUS reaches the target CI precision (epsilon=0.05) using 54%, 60%, and 62% fewer evaluated samples than EVALUE (a uniform-sampling, no-surrogate e-process baseline), evaluating only 4.6%, 2.8%, and 4.6% of each full pool; relative to a no-surrogate ablation of CELEUS itself (uncertainty-guided sampling only, no surrogate scores), adding surrogate scores further reduces the required samples by 10-23%. At the compute budget where an infeasible "oracle" sampling rule (using the true, otherwise-unknown per-sample residuals) first reaches the target precision, CELEUS closes 90%/93%/84% of the gap between the no-surrogate baseline and the oracle on the three benchmarks, versus only 53%/60%/60% for CELEUS's own no-surrogate ablation. (RQ3, mechanism) Three separate ablations attribute the efficiency gain to each of CELEUS's specific design components: the paper's weighted final performance estimator stays centered near zero bias throughout evaluation, while a naive unweighted version carries a persistent ~1-2% bias under non-uniform sampling; the proposed per-round signal achieves 1.5-2x lower mean-squared error than the naive single-step observed risk as an estimator of the pool risk; and CELEUS's uncertainty-guided sampling reduces the per-round conditional variance by 27%/20%/5% relative to uniform sampling on the three benchmarks, closing roughly 67%/53%/18% of the uniform-to-oracle variance gap.

Supplementary analyses include: results under cross-entropy loss (in addition to the main text's 0-1 loss), which show the same qualitative ordering; a comparison of three label-free sample-selection proxy strategies (differing in which quantity plays the role of the target-loss proxy versus the surrogate-correction term), finding one strategy consistently produces the tightest CIs though the difference narrows as more samples are evaluated; a hyperparameter-sensitivity sweep (the error-budget split between the e-process and the population correction, the hedge weight, a scaling stabilizer, and the sampling-probability floor) showing the method's efficiency changes smoothly and the chosen defaults are near-optimal without being tuned on evaluation outcomes; a computational-cost breakdown showing CELEUS's per-round wall-clock overhead (up to ~0.51 seconds on the slowest tested configuration, dominated by running the small surrogate model on the remaining unevaluated pool) is disclosed as real but small relative to the time saved by needing far fewer expensive target-model evaluations; and full per-(dataset, surrogate, target)-configuration breakdowns showing the ordering CELEUS <= CELEUS(no surrogate) <= EVALUE holds on every one of the 18 tested configurations, alongside an explicitly-flagged "corner case" where CELEUS's median stopping time is very slightly better than the infeasible oracle's on one specific configuration, which the paper attributes to finite-sample noise rather than to CELEUS somehow exceeding a proven optimality bound. The authors' own stated limitations (Appendix A.5) note that CELEUS's label efficiency does not imply lower total computational cost in every setting, since surrogate scoring and the per-round e-process bookkeeping add overhead beyond simple uniform-sampling baselines, though this overhead is measured to be small relative to the labeling cost saved when using small, fixed open-source surrogates. No funding beyond the disclosed academic grants and university compute resources is stated.

## Learnings

The core construction — combining a surrogate-completed pool-risk estimate with an inverse-probability-weighted correction for the newly-revealed sample's surrogate residual — yields a signal that is provably conditionally unbiased for the finite-pool risk under any valid (non-degenerate) sampling rule, decoupling "how to pick informative samples to evaluate next" from "how to keep the resulting risk estimate statistically valid regardless of that choice." This is a specific, transferable pattern for building efficient-yet-valid sequential estimators in any active-testing or prediction-powered-inference setting, not specific to LLM evaluation.

The paper's diagnosis of prior work's specific failure mode — a method's validity proof assumes a fixed decision (here, a fixed data partition) that the practical algorithm in fact re-derives adaptively from the same data being tested, a form of double dipping — is a specific, transferable checklist item for auditing any "adaptively updated confidence interval used to decide when to stop" procedure: verify whether the procedure's proof holds a quantity fixed that the deployed algorithm actually updates using the evaluation data itself.

Deriving a theoretically variance-optimal but practically infeasible oracle sampling rule first, and then designing and empirically validating a label-free heuristic against how much of the oracle-to-baseline gap it closes (here, 90%/93%/84% depending on the benchmark), is a specific, transferable methodology for justifying a practical acquisition or sampling heuristic in active learning or active testing: rather than validating a heuristic purely by its downstream end-to-end performance, benchmark it against a known theoretical ceiling to quantify how much headroom remains.

## Verification

This paper's empirical validation is unusually thorough for the claims it makes. The anytime-validity claim (RQ1) is tested via a targeted diagnostic constructed specifically to expose the closest prior method's double-dipping failure mode, rather than only reporting favorable aggregate coverage numbers; the resulting gap (0.161 observed vs. 0.05 nominal miscoverage for Cer-Eval, versus 0.021-0.030 for every e-process-based method including CELEUS) is a genuine stress test of the specific mechanism the paper identifies as broken in prior work. The efficiency claim (RQ2) is validated across 50 independent runs per configuration, six distinct surrogate-target model pairs, and three benchmarks, with full per-configuration breakdowns reported in the appendix (not only pooled averages), showing the claimed ordering holds on every one of 18 tested configurations. The mechanism claim (RQ3) is decomposed into three separate, independently-verified sub-claims (estimator bias, signal MSE, sampling-induced variance reduction) rather than asserted only via the end-to-end efficiency number. The paper also reports a hyperparameter-sensitivity sweep and a computational-cost accounting that candidly discloses where CELEUS is worse than its baseline (higher per-round latency), and explicitly surfaces and correctly explains a "corner case" where its practical method's stopping time slightly beats its own theoretically-optimal oracle reference on one specific configuration, attributing this to finite-sample variability rather than silently omitting it. The main scope limitation is that all three evaluated benchmarks (SST-2, AG News, MMLU) are classification-style tasks with free, automatically-computable ground-truth labels, whereas the paper's own introduction motivates the importance of efficient, label-frugal evaluation specifically for "open-ended generation and human-preference benchmarks" where evaluation requires costly human annotation or LLM-judge calls (citing AlpacaEval and LLM-as-judge as examples). No experiment in the paper tests the method in that open-ended or preference-based setting, so it remains untested whether the same efficiency gains, or the behavior of the uncertainty-guided sampling rule, would hold when surrogate scores are themselves noisy, expensive to obtain, or systematically biased (e.g., a weaker LLM judge standing in for a stronger one) — precisely the use case the paper's motivation leads with.

## Field context

CELEUS positions itself directly against Cer-Eval (Wang et al.), the closest prior "certifiable evaluation" framework, whose adaptive-partition double-dipping issue this paper specifically diagnoses and resolves via the anytime-valid e-process construction; it draws its "surrogate-assisted approximation" ingredient from the prediction-powered-inference lineage (Angelopoulos et al.) and its "select informative samples" ingredient from the active-testing literature (Kossen et al., Berrada et al.'s application of active testing to LLM evaluation). This is a statistics/evaluation-methodology paper quite distinct in kind from the LLM-agent and model-architecture papers that dominate the surrounding batch; no direct technical connection to other reviews in this corpus was identified, though the paper's central question (how many evaluation samples are actually enough to trust a reported benchmark score, and does a monitored, adaptively-stopped evaluation procedure actually retain the coverage guarantee it claims) is a rigorous statistical answer to a concern that recurs informally throughout several other reviews in this same batch (for example, single-run-per-configuration comparisons and small-sample user studies flagged as "missing-uncertainty" elsewhere) without those other papers drawing on e-processes or anytime-valid inference themselves.

## Critical discussion

The paper's core claims are well-supported within the settings it tests. The anytime-validity fix is demonstrated via a targeted diagnostic that directly exercises the specific mechanism (adaptive re-partitioning using the same evaluated data) the paper argues invalidates the closest prior method's guarantee, rather than relying only on a favorable aggregate comparison. The efficiency gains are demonstrated with genuine breadth (three benchmarks, six surrogate-target pairs, 50 seeds each, full per-configuration transparency) and are mechanistically decomposed into three separately-verified sources rather than asserted as a single opaque end-to-end number. The paper is also candid about a real trade-off it does not need to disclose but does: CELEUS's per-round computational overhead exceeds its uniform-sampling baseline's, and this is quantified rather than glossed over.

The clearest limitation is a gap between motivation and empirical scope. The introduction frames the paper's importance around expensive-to-score evaluation — open-ended generation and human-preference benchmarks judged by human annotators or LLM judges, where reducing the number of evaluated samples has real cost savings — but every evaluated benchmark (SST-2, AG News, MMLU) is a classification task with free, automatic ground-truth labels, and "surrogate scoring" in the experiments amounts to a smaller model's automatic prediction agreement with the target model rather than a proxy for a genuinely expensive human or LLM-judge label. This does not undermine the theoretical results, which are general, but it means the paper's own leading real-world motivation (costly open-ended/preference evaluation) is not the setting its efficiency numbers were actually measured in, leaving open whether the reported 54-62% sample savings, or the specific behavior of the uncertainty-guided sampling rule under noisier or more expensive surrogate signals, would transfer to that setting.

What remains solidly supported after this caveat: within the classification-style settings actually tested, CELEUS's anytime-valid coverage is demonstrated via a specific, targeted stress test rather than merely claimed, and its label-efficiency advantage over the closest valid (non-double-dipping) baseline is consistent across every one of 18 tested model-pair-and-benchmark configurations, with each of its three claimed efficiency mechanisms independently verified rather than only inferred from the aggregate result.
