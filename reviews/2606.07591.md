Citation: Xu, W., Li, S., Ye, T., Cao, Q., Chen, Y., Gao, H., Wang, Y., Li, Q., Li, K., et al. ResearchClawBench: A Benchmark for End-to-End Autonomous Scientific Research. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07591v5

## What this paper is about

Can an AI system, given the raw data and related literature behind a real published scientific paper (but not the paper's own findings), actually reproduce or extend that paper's discovery end to end — not just answer a quiz question about it? This paper builds ResearchClawBench (RCBench), 40 such tasks across 10 scientific domains, each graded against an expert-built rubric anchored to the real target paper, and finds that today's best autonomous research agents and frontier LLMs both plateau around 20-22 points on a 0-100 scale, far short of the 50-point threshold the authors define as "reproduced the target paper's result."

## Extended summary

Each of the 40 RCBench tasks is built from one real, published scientific paper (Astronomy, Chemistry, Earth, Energy, Information, Life, Material, Math, Neuroscience, Physics; 4 tasks per domain) and hides that target paper during evaluation, while supplying the system with the paper's underlying raw data, roughly four related-literature papers, and a stated scientific objective. The system must produce a final research report. Scoring uses the Reference-Anchored Discovery Score (RADS, 0-100), built from expert-curated, multimodal (text and image) weighted rubric items enumerating the specific evidence, figures, and claims a correct reproduction would contain; a score near 50 is calibrated to mean "matches the target paper's own reported result," below 50 marks partial/insufficient recovery, and above 50 is only flagged as potential (not validated) evidence of a novel discovery beyond the target paper. Rubric construction involved domain experts cross-checking rubric items and, per the paper's own description (Fig. 3), re-running or verifying against the target experiments, with inter-rater agreement checks.

Two system populations are evaluated under fixed compute/tool budgets. Seven "autonomous research agents" run under a unified harness: Claude Code, Codex CLI, ARIS Codex, OpenClaw, Nanobot, two EvoScientist versions (v0.0.4, v0.1.1), and ResearchClaw (a system distinct from the separately reviewed AutoResearchClaw, see metadata_notes). Seventeen native LLMs are evaluated through a purpose-built lightweight ReAct-style harness, ResearchHarness (tool categories: web/retrieval, local file read/write, local code execution, 128k-token auto-compaction), spanning Claude-Opus-4.6/4.7, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, GLM-5.1, GPT-5.4/5.5, Gemini-3.1-Pro/3.5-Flash, Grok-4.1/4.3, Kimi-K2.5/2.6, MiMo-V2-Pro/V2.5, and three Qwen3.5/3.6/3.7 variants. Final reports are scored against the rubrics by an LLM judge, GPT-5.1.

Headline results (Table 5): the strongest autonomous agent, Claude Code, averages 21.5/100 overall (autonomous-agent-frontier mean 25.8); the strongest native LLM, Claude-Opus-4.7, averages 20.7/100 (LLM-frontier mean 26.5) — both well below the 50-point re-discovery threshold, and neither population reliably outperforms the other. Claude Code wins only 12 of 40 tasks outright. Median pairwise task-level score correlation across the 28 autonomous-agent-configuration pairs is 0.77 (range 0.64-0.86): which tasks are hard is highly consistent across systems, even though no single system dominates across tasks. Different LLMs lead in different domains (e.g., Claude-Opus-4.6 in Astronomy/Earth/Energy/Math, GLM-5.1 in Chemistry, Gemini-3.5-Flash in Information, GPT-5.4 in Life, DeepSeek-V4-Pro in Material, Claude-Opus-4.7 in Neuroscience, Qwen3.7-Max in Physics). Table 8 (per-task, in Appendix C) shows extreme per-task volatility: some tasks (e.g., Chemistry_002, Earth_003) score near zero (0-4/100) across almost every system tested, agents and LLMs alike, suggesting a subset of tasks may be close to universally unsolved or mis-specified regardless of system quality.

Four supplemental dimensions (Table 6: Comprehensiveness, Depth, Instruction Following, Professionalism) are scored alongside RADS. Systems consistently score much higher on Professionalism (>70) than on the other three, and all four correlate only weakly with the core RADS score — the authors read this as evidence that "the central challenge is not producing a polished report, but recovering rubric-critical scientific evidence." A Pareto-style cost/runtime analysis (Fig. 5, single run per task-system pair, 280 autonomous-agent runs = 7 agents x 40 tasks) finds only a weak positive relationship between resource investment and score overall, calling Claude Code (high score, high cost, long runtime) an outlier that "largely elevates" the apparent correlation; the cost-efficient frontier point is Qwen3.7-Max, the runtime-efficient point is OpenClaw. An error-type analysis across the 280 autonomous-agent runs (Fig. 6, six categories) finds failures concentrate in Experiment Design Mismatch, Evidence Mismatch, and Scientific Core Missing rather than in simple Execution Failure or Reliability/Reporting Failure — systems mostly run without crashing and produce polished reports, but drift from the correct experimental protocol or miss the underlying scientific mechanism. A worked case study (Fig. 7, Physics_002, random quantum circuit sampling fidelity) shows the best-performing agent on that task, OpenClaw, scoring only 27.45/100: it recovers the most direct observable trend (linear XEB fidelity decay with circuit depth) but misses several rubric-critical verification components (a multi-estimator cross-check at fixed depth, an MB-regression/mirror-circuit inference step, and the full gate-count error-propagation model).

Authors' stated limitations (Section 6): (1) tasks are dry-lab only — existing data, code, and literature — and cannot assess wet-lab research requiring physical experiments, sample preparation, or instrument operation; (2) scoring targets the final report rather than fine-grained intermediate research steps; (3) evaluating genuinely novel scientific conclusions (as opposed to reproduction) would require more reliable evaluation methods than a rubric anchored to an existing target paper. No funding or acknowledgments section is present in the extracted text.

## Learnings

The Reference-Anchored Discovery Score design — deliberately anchoring "good" at 50 to mean "matches the target paper's result" rather than either penalizing any deviation from the exact target (which would punish legitimate alternative valid approaches) or scoring fully open-ended with no anchor at all (which loses evaluability) — is a reusable rubric-design pattern for any benchmark that must score open-ended generation against a known-good reference without treating that reference as the only acceptable answer.

The error-taxonomy finding that failures cluster in Experiment Design Mismatch, Evidence Mismatch, and Scientific Core Missing rather than Execution Failure is a specific, transferable diagnostic: current systems can already execute code and write polished reports without crashing, but the actual bottleneck is choosing and following the correct experimental protocol and recovering the underlying scientific mechanism. This corroborates a "domain judgment, not execution ability, is the bottleneck" pattern seen elsewhere in this batch (e.g., the file-localization-vs-line-recall split in [reviews/2606.07297.md](2606.07297.md), SWE-Explore).

The observed weak relationship between resource investment (cost, runtime) and RADS score is a useful negative result against assuming test-time-compute scaling alone will close this gap: the paper's own read is that the bottleneck is a scientific-judgment capability gap rather than an iteration-budget gap, though this is based on a single run per task-system pair (see Critical discussion) and so should be read as suggestive rather than conclusive.

## Verification

Ground-truth construction is comparatively rigorous: rubrics are expert-curated per task, multimodal (text and image), weighted, and per the paper's description (Fig. 3) cross-checked by domain experts with inter-rater agreement and re-verification against the target experiments — more scaffolding than many rubric-based benchmarks provide. The downstream judging step, however, is a single LLM judge (GPT-5.1) scoring every report against these rubrics, and the extracted text reports no human-agreement check on the judge's own scores, despite the paper's own Related Work section (Section 2) explicitly citing LLM-judge bias as a known risk in this space. Separately, the headline comparison and the cost/runtime analysis both rest on a single run per task-per-system (280 autonomous-agent runs = 7 x 40, no repeats), so the reported "weak positive cost-score relationship" and the close task-level correlations (0.64-0.86) carry no run-to-run variance estimate. Coverage is comprehensive by domain (10 domains, 4 tasks each) but each domain rests on only 4 tasks, so per-domain leaders (e.g., "GLM-5.1 strongest in Chemistry") should be read as based on a small task count.

## Field context

RCBench positions itself (Section 2, Table 1) as the first benchmark combining full paper/data/execution/report coverage across 10 domains, contrasting itself with QA-style science benchmarks (SciQ, GPQA, HLE), simulated-environment benchmarks (ScienceWorld, DiscoveryWorld), narrower ML-research benchmarks (SciCode, ScienceAgentBench, MLAgentBench/MLE-bench, PaperBench, MLGym/AIRS-Bench/MLR-Bench), and prior autonomous-research systems it cites as motivating context rather than benchmarks (The AI Scientist, AI Co-Scientist, AI-Researcher, InternAgent-1.5). Within this same batch, it sits closest to [reviews/2605.26087.md](2605.26087.md) (a physics-only "hide the target result, score re-discovery" design at smaller scale) and [reviews/2604.15411.md](2604.15411.md) (PRL-Bench, real-paper-grounded, rubric-scored, physics-focused, whose reported top score of ~44/100 is of a similar low order of magnitude to RCBench's ~21-22/100, though the two use different task pools, domains, and rubric definitions so are not directly comparable). It also shares its broad evaluated-system pool (many of the same frontier LLM backbones: GPT-5.4/5.5, Claude-Opus-4.6/4.7, Gemini, GLM, Kimi, Qwen, Grok) with [reviews/2606.05405.md](2606.05405.md) (Agents' Last Exam), reviewed earlier in this batch, though that benchmark spans professional domains generally rather than reproducing specific published scientific results. Related autonomous-research systems already in the corpus but not directly benchmarked here include [reviews/2605.26340.md](2605.26340.md) (ScientistOne) and [reviews/2605.20025.md](2605.20025.md) (AutoResearchClaw) — the latter's name is easily confused with this paper's own "ResearchClaw" baseline agent, which is a separate system (see metadata_notes).

## Critical discussion

The central claim — that current autonomous research agents and frontier LLMs both fall far short of reproducing published scientific results end to end — is well supported: two independently evaluated system populations (agent harnesses and native LLMs via ResearchHarness), spanning 10 domains and 40 tasks, converge on essentially the same headline range (20.7-21.5/100 for the best system in each population), and the qualitative case study (Physics_002) and error taxonomy both illustrate concretely what specific rubric-critical evidence is being missed rather than asserting failure abstractly.

Two verification gaps temper confidence in the finer-grained comparisons. First, the single LLM judge (GPT-5.1) that scores every report against the rubrics has no reported human-agreement validation in the extracted text, even though the paper's own Related Work explicitly names LLM-judge bias as a live concern in this literature; the paper does invest heavily in the rubric-construction side (expert cross-checks, inter-rater agreement) but that rigor is not shown to carry through to the scoring step itself. That said, the ranking outcome argues against severe same-family favoritism: nearly all evaluated autonomous agents run on GPT-family backbones (GPT-5.4 appears to back most of the seven agent harnesses per Table 5), yet the overall leaderboard is topped by Claude Code and Claude-Opus-4.7 rather than a GPT-5.4-backed system, which is not the pattern a GPT-5.1 judge favoring its own family would produce. Second, every headline comparison (Table 5, the cost/runtime Pareto analysis in Fig. 5, and the 0.77 median task-correlation figure) rests on a single run per task-per-system, with no reported variance — for a highly stochastic, multi-step agentic task, this leaves open how much of the numerical spread between closely-scoring systems reflects genuine capability differences versus run-to-run noise, a gap the paper's own limitations section (Section 6) does not name (it focuses instead on dry-lab-only scope, report-level rather than step-level scoring, and the difficulty of evaluating genuine novelty).

What remains solidly supported after these caveats: current systems, whether packaged as autonomous agents or driven through a lightweight harness, plateau well below the paper's own re-discovery threshold on tasks derived from real, already-published, already-solved science; the shortfall traces predominantly to Experiment Design Mismatch, Evidence Mismatch, and Scientific Core Missing rather than to code-execution or report-writing failure; and task difficulty is strongly consistent across systems even though no single system wins uniformly, indicating some tasks are close to universally hard regardless of which frontier system attempts them.
