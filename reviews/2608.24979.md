FrontierChallenge: Evaluating Scientific Workflow Completion. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24979v2

## What this paper is about

Most agent benchmarks score a final answer, a single program, or one discipline. FrontierChallenge instead asks whether an agent can execute a fully-specified, multi-stage scientific workflow — using real domain software (ORCA, CP2K, LAMMPS, AmberTools, PLUMED) on fixed inputs — and deliver a complete, mutually consistent bundle of required outputs (code, tables, figures, and a report), not just a plausible-sounding conclusion. The headline finding: the best frontier agent/model configurations complete only about a fifth of tasks in full, even when their average partial-progress score looks strong, and in two of six domains a near-perfect average score coexisted with a 0-4% full-completion rate.

## Extended summary

**Benchmark construction.** From a curated pool of 300 end-to-end scientific workflows across six domains (quantum chemistry, molecular dynamics, materials characterization, analytical chemistry, life science, electrochemistry/environment), the authors release and evaluate 97 (74 Hard, 23 Medium), retaining 203 as an internal held-out set (including all GPU-requiring tasks). Each released task is packaged with a task description, fixed inputs, available software/tools, an explicit output-deliverable contract, and a task-specific executable Grader; tasks with purely subjective outputs or that couldn't support reproducible automated scoring were excluded during curation. The authors explicitly flag that domain sizes (6 to 23 tasks) are a "descriptive slice" of the release, not a probability sample of the underlying scientific fields, and caution against reading cross-domain score differences as intrinsic difficulty rankings.

**Metrics.** Each task's Grader returns a native score in [0,100], with some rubric criteria delegated to an LLM Judge (GPT-5.6 Sol, sampled 3 times per criterion and averaged). Pass Rate is the primary metric: the fraction of tasks meeting a strict full-completion threshold (score ≥ 99.9, chosen only to absorb minor Judge-averaging noise, not to relax any requirement). Avg. Score is reported only as a complementary partial-progress measure and is explicitly not comparable across tasks as a calibrated scale.

**Main results.** Across twelve frontier models and three agent scaffolds (Codex, Claude Code, Frontier Agent), Pass Rate ranged from 3.1% to 20.6% while Avg. Score ranged from 67.5 to 87.9 — every configuration with a high average score still failed to fully complete the large majority of tasks. The best configurations (GPT-5.6 Sol with Codex; Grok 4.6 with Claude Code) tied at 20.6% Pass Rate. The gap between partial progress and full delivery was most extreme in analytical chemistry (Avg. Score up to 87.6, best Pass Rate 4%) and electrochemistry/environment (Avg. Score up to 94.9, Pass Rate 0% for every configuration). Domain-level profiles varied substantially by model: no single configuration dominated every domain, and some (e.g., the Frontier Agent scaffold with Apodex 1.1) completed zero tasks in three of six domains despite reasonable performance in quantum chemistry.

**Process assessment (HDS6).** Using the HDS6 framework from a companion paper by the same team ("Apodex Discovery"), the authors retrospectively score 1,164 frozen trajectories across six process dimensions (Coherence, Evidence, Alternatives, Scope, Tools, Repair) independently of final task success, using a judge/reviewer/sweeper/arbiter panel built from GPT-5.6 Terra, GPT-5.6 Luna, and Claude Opus 5. HDS6 scores correlate strongly with both Avg. Score (Pearson r=0.874) and Pass Rate (r=0.828) across configurations, and moderately at the per-task level (mean per-task Pearson r=0.475, positive on 85% of tasks). Scope (boundary/failure-reasoning) is consistently the weakest dimension across every configuration (2.19-3.51 on a 1-4 scale), while Evidence is consistently the strongest.

**Failure analysis.** Restricted to the 970 Claude Code trajectories (the only scaffold with a common event schema), 75.5% of non-passing runs (641/849) ended with a final message containing completion language, and this rate stayed above 78% for every non-passing score band above 50 — meaning an agent's own confident final statement is a weak signal of actual delivery. Tool errors occurred in both passing (94.2%) and non-passing (80.7%) trajectories at similar median rates, so raw error presence alone does not predict outcome. Judge-assessed artifact shortfalls (missing or incorrect deliverables) accounted for 85-97% of non-passing submissions in materials characterization, analytical chemistry, and electrochemistry/environment, versus 43% in quantum chemistry, where deterministic Grader failures were comparatively more common.

**Self-stated limitations.** The Conclusion explicitly states the findings are bounded by the released task set, the evaluated configurations, the Claude Code-only scope of the trajectory-level failure analysis, provider-specific and non-hardware-normalized resource accounting, and single runs per task.

## Learnings

The central methodological contribution — measuring "Pass Rate" (full contract satisfaction) separately from "Avg. Score" (partial rubric credit) — is a reusable and important distinction for any agentic benchmark: this paper demonstrates concretely, not just argues, that a high average score can systematically mask near-total failure to deliver a complete artifact bundle, which is exactly the gap a benchmark reporting only an aggregate score would hide.

The finding that an agent's own completion language in its final message is a weak, near-useless signal of actual success (75.5% of non-passing Claude Code runs still claimed completion) is a concrete, quantified instance of a broader and recurring theme in this corpus's AI-agent reviews: self-reported confidence or self-assessed success is not a trustworthy substitute for external, artifact-level verification.

## Verification

Central to the paper's design: each task has an explicit output-deliverable contract checked by a task-specific executable Grader (files, numerical values, formats, figures, code execution, cross-artifact consistency), with LLM-judge involvement limited to specific rubric criteria and averaged over 3 samples to reduce judge noise. The HDS6 process assessment is presented as a complementary, independent check (it grades agent behavior without access to the verifier's reference outputs), and its correlation with the outcome-based metrics is offered as convergent validity rather than asserted. A genuine limitation, which the paper does not flag itself, is that the HDS6 framework used to validate FrontierChallenge's construct validity was introduced by the same team in a companion paper (Wang et al., "Apodex Discovery"), so this correlation is a self-consistency check between two of the same team's own instruments rather than an independently developed validation method. Similarly, the Judge used for semantic grading criteria (GPT-5.6 Sol) and the panel used for the HDS6 assessment (GPT-5.6 Terra as judge/reviewer, Claude Opus 5 as arbiter) are drawn from model families that are simultaneously evaluated as competing systems in the same benchmark (GPT-5.6 Sol ties for the highest Pass Rate; GPT-5.6 Terra and Claude Opus 5 are both top-ranked HDS6 performers) — the paper does not report an independent human check on whether this overlap introduces any grading bias toward those families' outputs.

## Field context

FrontierChallenge positions itself within a fast-growing space of scientific-agent benchmarks it explicitly compares against in Table 1 (HLE, ALE, Frontier-Bench, BLADE, ScienceAgentBench, CORE-Bench, PaperBench, BioAgent Bench, BiomniBench, NatureBench, AstaBench), arguing its distinguishing combination is cross-domain coverage, multi-artifact deliverable contracts, and executable per-task evaluation together, rather than any one of these individually. This corpus already contains reviews of several AI-Scientist and AutoResearch systems evaluated this same batch (Spark-to-Paper, [2608.11924.md](2608.11924.md); Faraday/Replica, [2608.13331.md](2608.13331.md); OmniScientist, [2608.13558.md](2608.13558.md)) that all pursue some version of end-to-end scientific-task automation; FrontierChallenge is complementary to all three as an independent, third-party-style benchmark (though authored by a company with its own competing agent product, Apodex 1.1/Frontier Agent) rather than a system paper, and its central finding — that high partial-progress scores mask low full-completion rates — is a methodological caution directly relevant to interpreting the self-reported success metrics in those system papers.

## Critical discussion

The benchmark's core design choice — separating strict Pass Rate from descriptive Avg. Score, and backing Pass Rate with an executable, contract-level Grader rather than a holistic LLM judgment — is a genuine strength, and the headline finding (high Avg. Score coexisting with near-zero Pass Rate in two domains) is demonstrated with specific, checkable numbers rather than asserted. The completion-language and tool-error analyses are similarly concrete, quantified, and appropriately scoped to the one scaffold (Claude Code) for which a common event schema was available, rather than over-generalized to all three scaffolds.

Three things temper how far the results can be pushed. First, the benchmark's construct-validity argument (that HDS6 process scores corroborate the outcome metrics) rests on a framework introduced by the same team in a companion paper, and the LLM judge/panel used for both the primary Grader's semantic criteria and the HDS6 assessment is drawn from model families (GPT-5.6 variants, Claude Opus 5) that are simultaneously ranked competitors in the same evaluation — a same-family-judge concern the paper does not address with an independent human-rating check (`same-family-judge`). Second, several of the paper's most dramatic domain-level contrasts (electrochemistry/environment's 94.9 Avg. Score against a 0% Pass Rate for every configuration; analytical chemistry's 4% best Pass Rate) are computed over domains with only 6 and 23 released tasks respectively, and results are reported from single runs per task per the authors' own stated limitations — with such small per-domain, single-run samples, a percentage like "0%" or "4%" carries substantial sampling uncertainty that is not quantified anywhere in the reported results (`narrow-evaluation`, `missing-uncertainty`). Third, resource-use comparisons (tokens, execution time) are explicitly and correctly caveated by the authors as non-hardware-normalized and provider-specific, which is honest but also means the six-fold token-usage spread reported cannot itself support any efficiency ranking, only a resource-consumption description.

One point worth noting rather than treating as a flaw: the company's own model and scaffold (Apodex 1.1, in both its Frontier Agent and Claude Code configurations) rank in the bottom half of Table 2 (12.4% and 10.3% Pass Rate, well below the 20.6% leaders), which is a point in favor of the benchmark's evenhandedness — an industry-authored benchmark that reports its own product underperforming several competitors is not the pattern one would expect from a benchmark constructed to flatter its authors.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the central claim that partial-progress scores and self-reported completion language are each, independently, weak proxies for full scientific-workflow delivery is demonstrated with concrete, task-verifiable evidence, and is a genuinely useful corrective for how this class of benchmark should be read and reported going forward. The precise domain-by-domain percentages and the specific magnitude of the HDS6-outcome correlation should be read as the current release's snapshot on 97 of a planned 300 tasks, evaluated with single runs, rather than as fully statistically characterized population estimates.
