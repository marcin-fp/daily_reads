Citation: Khalifa, M., Agarwal, R., Logeswaran, L., Kim, J., Peng, H., Lee, M., Lee, H., and Wang, L. Process Reward Models That Think. arXiv preprint, 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16828

## What this paper is about

A process reward model (PRM) scores each step of a model-written solution. At test time, a search or selection procedure can then prefer candidates whose reasoning holds up, not only those with a plausible final answer. The standard PRM is a classifier trained on hundreds of thousands of human step labels. The authors ask whether an open-weight reasoning model, which already writes long chains of thought (CoTs), can be turned into a step verifier that writes a critique of every step, labels it, and needs very little labelled data.

They fine-tune such models on about 1,000 synthetic verification chains, roughly 8K step labels, kept only when their step verdicts agree with human labels. The resulting verifier, ThinkPRM, is reported to beat both the same models used as prompted judges and a classifier PRM trained on all 712K labels of PRM800K. The comparisons cover math error detection, best-of-N selection, and step-guided beam search, and ThinkPRM shows some transfer to physics multiple-choice questions and to code.

## Extended summary

Data. The teacher is QwQ-32B-Preview, prompted (Fig. 13) to critique each step of a PRM800K solution prefix and to end each step with `\boxed{correct}` or `\boxed{incorrect}`. It is told to stop at the first incorrect step. Four chains are sampled per prefix at temperature 0.1. A chain is kept if (1) every step has an extractable label, (2) all step labels match the PRM800K human labels, (3) it is at most 4,096 tokens, and (4) the kept set is roughly balanced on final-answer correctness. About 20% of samples pass (1) and (2), so about 5K samples yield the 1K training chains. The final set (Table 2) covers 869 unique questions with 9.5 steps per prefix on average (range 1 to 53). It has 7,474 correct step labels (92.3%) against 625 incorrect (7.7%), and 48.6% correct versus 51.4% incorrect solutions. The average chain is 1,037 tokens (range 207 to 3,669). Filtering uses only step labels, not gold rationales.

Training. Four verifiers are trained: R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, -7B, -14B, and QwQ-32B-Preview. The 1.5B and 7B models are fully fine-tuned (3 epochs, batch 32, learning rate 6e-5, four RTX A6000 GPUs, about 0.5 and 2 hours). The 14B model and QwQ use LoRA (rank 32, alpha 16, learning rate 4e-4, batch 16, 3 and 5 epochs). This takes 1.5 and 4.5 hours on one A100 80GB. The authors found QwQ hard to train with LoRA, and it still produces many invalid judgments after training. Reinforcement learning and preference training are deliberately not used.

Scoring and baselines. ThinkPRM generates a verification chain of up to 8,192 tokens. The string "Is the solution correct?" is then force-decoded, and the solution score is P(yes) / (P(yes) + P(no)). DiscPRM is the same base model trained for one epoch with binary cross-entropy on "+"/"-" tokens over all of PRM800K (98K examples, 712K step labels), aggregated by its last-step score. LLM-as-a-judge is the same base model prompted with the ProcessBench template (Fig. 26), which asks for a per-step critique and one final yes/no. Unweighted majority voting is the remaining baseline.

Standalone verification. On ProcessBench, the evaluation uses the OlympiadBench and OmniMath subsets (1K problem-prefix pairs each). The metric is a binary F1 for "does the solution contain a mistake". Table 3 gives LLM-as-a-judge versus ThinkPRM F1, with bad-output rates in parentheses. R1-Qwen-1.5B goes from 5.0 (51.4%) / 5.4 (55.1%) to 76.3 (1.4%) / 75.7 (2.4%). R1-Qwen-7B goes from 44.8 / 45.7 to 73.4 / 74.0. R1-Qwen-14B goes from 72.8 (13.3%) / 67.8 (18.6%) to 87.3 (2.3%) / 85.7 (2.3%). QwQ-32B-Preview goes from 50.6 / 55.5 to 73.1 (15.1%) / 73.2 (7.9%). The random baseline is 39.1 / 32.7. The authors describe untuned judges as prompt-sensitive, often trying to solve instead of verify, and prone to overthinking and infinite loops. Accurate judge chains are mostly under 3K tokens, while inaccurate ones spike at 7K to 8K tokens. ThinkPRM chains stay at 1K to 5K tokens.

Prompt ablation. Table 5 compares three prompts. For QwQ, single yes/no gives 50.6 / 55.5, bad-step-index 65.4 / 69.0, and decision-for-each-step 40.1 / 36.6. For R1-Qwen-14B the same prompts give 72.8 / 67.8, 77.7 / 76.6, and 73.3 / 71.4. Invalid rates range from 3% to 18%.

Test-time scaling setup. ThinkPRM-1.5B and ThinkPRM-14B are the main models. MATH-500 is a 100-problem subset spanning all difficulty levels, chosen for compute reasons. The other tasks are AIME 2024, the physics subset of GPQA-Diamond (86 questions), and a 200-problem subset of LiveCodeBench v5. The generators are:

- Qwen2.5-14B for MATH-500 best-of-N.
- Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct for beam search.
- Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct for AIME and GPQA.
- Qwen2.5-Coder-7B for code.
- Qwen3-1.7B in thinking mode for long-trace verification.

Best-of-N uses verifier-weighted majority voting, except on AIME, where the top verifier score is chosen directly. Beam search samples 4 candidate next steps per beam at temperature 0.6, for at most 20 steps, with double newlines as step delimiters.

Test-time results. Most of these results are reported only in figures, so exact values could not be read from the extract.

- On MATH-500 best-of-N, ThinkPRM-14B is higher than or comparable to DiscPRM-14B at all budgets, and the same holds on AIME 2024.
- In beam search, ThinkPRM-1.5B beats DiscPRM-1.5B and LLM-as-a-judge. It also beats two off-the-shelf PRMs trained on more data, MathShepherd-7B and RLHFlow-Deepseek-PRM (8B), and exceeds the latter by more than 7% across all beam sizes.
- Out of domain, ThinkPRM-14B beats DiscPRM-14B by 8% on GPQA-physics, where DiscPRM drops at N=32, and by 4.5% on LiveCodeBench. Qwen2.5-7B-Math-PRM struggles on code. The authors read this as a sign that discriminative PRMs are fragile under domain shift.
- Parallel verifier scaling, which averages K sampled chains, lifts ThinkPRM-1.5B@4 by more than 5 points in beam search. For ThinkPRM-14B best-of-N, K=8 is only comparable to K=4.
- Sequential scaling forces re-checks with the trigger phrases "Let me double check", "Let's verify again", and "Did I miss something?", up to 32K tokens. It was run on only 200 OmniMath problems. ThinkPRM keeps improving there, while the prompted judge drops after 16K tokens. ThinkPRM ends 15 F1 points above DiscPRM-14B, and the abstract states a 7.2% margin over LLM-as-a-judge at matched token budget.
- At equal token budgets, parallel and sequential scaling are close, with a slight edge for parallel scaling.

Analyses.

- Data efficiency. ThinkPRM-14B trained on 500 or 1K chains (about 4K or 8K labels) is compared with DiscPRM-14B trained on 1K, 10K, 50K, and 98K examples. The authors report that ThinkPRM scales much better with two orders of magnitude fewer labels.
- More data. Training on 65K filtered chains improves MATH-500 best-of-N. The authors note "some performance drop on out-of-domain tasks" after this extra math training.
- Chain length (Table 1). gpt-4o-mini was used to compress the same 1K chains into short versions. Training on the short versions lowers R1-Qwen-14B from 87.3 / 85.7 to 55.3 / 60.8, and R1-Qwen-1.5B from 87.3 / 75.7 to 64.8 / 66.7. The 1.5B long-CoT OlympiadBench value of 87.3 conflicts with the 76.3 reported for the same model in Tables 3 and 4. The raw dump carries the same number, so the conflict is in the paper, not the extraction.
- Long traces. ThinkPRM-1.5B, trained only on short step-delimited solutions, verifies Qwen3-1.7B thinking traces on the full MATH-500 and beats the baselines. It does this by extracting the steps embedded in the trace.
- Compute-matched comparison. Against FLOP-matched self-consistency on MATH-500 and GPQA-physics, ThinkPRM is comparable at low budgets and better at high budgets.
- Automatic labels (Table 4). Filtering with Math-Shepherd Monte Carlo labels instead of PRM800K labels gives 75.8 / 76.5 versus 76.3 / 75.7 for ThinkPRM-1.5B.
- Difficulty. Gains over DiscPRM concentrate on harder problems: MATH levels 3 to 5, and GPQA bins defined from Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct pass@1 over 32 samples.
- Filtering. Process-based filtering (65K chains) gives a better beam-search verifier for R1-Qwen-1.5B than GenRM-style outcome filtering (128K chains).

Author-stated limitations (Sec. 5.8). Scores from yes/no token probabilities are overconfident and cluster near 0 or 1. "Step label interference" occurs: once a step is judged incorrect, later correct steps are more likely to be labelled incorrect. Generating a verification chain adds overhead compared with a classifier, which the authors argue the gains justify. They also note that the sequential-scaling results may depend on the trigger phrases, which they did not optimize.

## Learnings

Much of the apparent inability of small reasoning models to verify is a failure to finish with a parsable verdict. R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B as a prompted judge scores 5 F1, below random, with more than half its outputs lacking a label, and 1K filtered examples bring it to 76. When comparing a prompted judge with a trained one, separate termination and format failures from wrong verdicts before crediting the training with "better verification".

Filtering teacher critiques by agreement on every step, not only on the final verdict, is what makes the cheap recipe work. A 65K process-filtered set beat a 128K outcome-filtered set, and the filter needs only step labels, not gold rationales. The caveat is that this rests on a single ablation with a 1.5B model on a 100-problem beam-search subset.

The length of the verification text appears to matter. Compressing the same 1K critiques into short versions cost 20 to 30 F1 points at 14B. That warns against shortening a learned verifier's output to save tokens, although the compression was done by a different model (gpt-4o-mini), so some of the loss may come from what the rewrite dropped.

Cheap Monte Carlo step labels (Math-Shepherd) worked as well as human PRM800K labels for the filter at 1.5B (75.8 / 76.5 versus 76.3 / 75.7). This suggests the recipe tolerates noisier label sources, at least on math at this scale.

A generative PRM's P(yes) is not a calibrated probability. The authors report scores piling up near 0 and 1, and early wrong labels contaminating later steps. Any downstream use that weights candidates by the score, such as weighted majority voting, inherits both problems.

## Verification

Verification is the subject of the paper. The object checked is each step of a math solution: PRM800K training prefixes drawn from MATH, and ProcessBench OlympiadBench and OmniMath prefixes. By extension the verifier also scores GPQA physics answers and code solutions, which are out of domain.

The verdict comes from a fine-tuned LLM. It writes a critique, emits `\boxed{correct}` or `\boxed{incorrect}` per step, and gives a final yes/no, and the solution score is the normalized P(yes). Human labels (PRM800K) or Monte Carlo rollout labels (Math-Shepherd) enter only as a filter on teacher chains. Deterministic checks play no part.

The verifier is itself evaluated in two ways. The first is ProcessBench binary solution-level F1 on 2K problem-prefix pairs, which asks whether a mistake exists; whether the flagged step is the erroneous one is not scored. The second is downstream accuracy against answer keys or code pass rates after best-of-N or beam search.

The verifier has no abstention option. Outputs without a parsable label are counted as invalid and reported as rates, but the extract does not say how they enter F1. The data-generation prompt stops at the first incorrect step, so steps after an error carry no training labels, and 92.3% of the training step labels are "correct".

Verifier compute is scaled in parallel by averaging K chains, and sequentially by trigger phrases that force re-checks. The paper documents several failure modes: prompt sensitivity (up to 3 to 4 F1 points from slight rewording, and larger gaps between prompt designs in Table 5), looping and overthinking in untuned judges, overconfident scores, and step label interference.

Several things were not tested. The verifier was never used as an RL reward, so its hackability under optimization pressure is unknown. Calibration was not measured with any metric. Step-localization accuracy was not reported, and no human audit of critique content was done.

## Field context

The authors place the work at the meeting point of three lines. The first is discriminative PRMs trained on human step labels (Uesato et al. 2022; Lightman et al. 2023, which built PRM800K) or on automatic rollout labels (Math-Shepherd; Luo et al. 2024). The second is generative verification: LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al. 2023) and GenRM (Zhang et al. 2024a). The authors characterize GenRM as outcome-only with short CoTs. The third is test-time scaling with verifiers, via best-of-N and beam search (Snell et al. 2024; Beeching et al.), plus budget forcing borrowed from s1 (Muennighoff et al. 2025). ProcessBench (Zheng et al. 2024) supplies the standalone benchmark. Their stated change is to use long-CoT reasoning models, R1-distilled Qwen and QwQ-32B-Preview, as the verifier backbone with light rejection-sampling fine-tuning. That makes verifier compute a second scaling axis next to generator compute. v1 appeared in April 2025, a few months after the R1 and QwQ-Preview releases it builds on (cited as 2025 and November 2024), and v5 (December 2025) adds an ablation.

Within this corpus, the closest later work is MaxProof ([reviews/2606.13473.md](2606.13473.md), June 2026). It takes generative verification into proof RL, using a conservative multi-judge verifier as the reward and a Verifier Expert trained to localize errors rather than regress a score. ThinkPRM uses its verifier only for selection and search, so it never faces the reward-hacking pressure MaxProof documents. That history is the relevant warning for anyone who would reuse ThinkPRM as a training reward. This connection is our inference, not the authors'.

ThinkPRM cites Huang et al. 2023 ([reviews/24selfcor.md](24selfcor.md)) for the claim that LLMs often fail to recognize their own reasoning errors. ThinkPRM's sequential scaling is a form of intrinsic re-checking by the verifier. It helps after fine-tuning, while the prompted judge degrades beyond 16K tokens, which fits the earlier finding that unaided self-revision is unreliable. The compute-matched comparison with self-consistency is the kind of matched-cost baseline that review emphasizes.

The long, looping, non-terminating judge chains are a verifier-side instance of the overthinking reported in [reviews/2604.10739.md](2604.10739.md). The repetition loops also resemble the degeneration that [reviews/1904.09751.md](1904.09751.md) attributes to maximization decoding; see Critical discussion on decoding settings.

ThinkPRM improves the selector in best-of-N, while [reviews/2510.00931.md](2510.00931.md) argues for replacing selection with fusion. The two are complementary, not competing, results. [reviews/2604.14188.md](2604.14188.md) points out that PRM-style verifiers judge only the steps that are written down. ThinkPRM's long-trace experiment shows it can find embedded steps, which is a different problem from unstated ones. [reviews/2604.18176.md](2604.18176.md) is a physics counterpoint: its reward model is grounded in symbolic and execution checks rather than a learned critique, and ThinkPRM's recipe contains no executable check at all. The survey [reviews/2607.07663.md](2607.07663.md) lists cheaper PRMs among current verification approaches, and this paper is a concrete instance.

## Critical discussion

The central claim is that a reasoning model fine-tuned on about 1K gold-filtered verification chains verifies better than the same model prompted as a judge, and better than a same-base classifier trained on all of PRM800K. Table 3 supports it most directly, and the test-time scaling curves, which are reported only as figures, support it least directly. The 14B result is the strongest single piece of evidence: 72.8 / 67.8 to 87.3 / 85.7 F1 with 1.5 GPU-hours of LoRA training. I would not dismiss it.

The ProcessBench number measures less than the "process" framing suggests. The authors report a binary F1 on whether a solution contains a mistake. That is outcome-style error detection over a prefix, and it does not score whether the verifier identified the erroneous step, which is what a step verifier adds over an outcome verifier. The step-level usefulness of ThinkPRM is supported indirectly by beam search, where prefix scores steer generation. It is not measured directly.

The "over 70 F1 points" gain for the 1.5B model is mostly a repair of termination and format. The prompted 1.5B judge scores 5.0, below the 39.1 random baseline, with 51% bad outputs, and the text does not say how those outputs are scored. The gains at 7B and 14B are more informative.

The prompted-judge baseline is also not the strongest one the paper itself reports. Section 3.1 says the single yes/no prompt was chosen because it worked "best overall". Yet Table 5 shows the bad-step-index prompt beating it for both models: 77.7 / 76.6 versus 72.8 / 67.8 for R1-Qwen-14B, and 65.4 / 69.0 versus 50.6 / 55.5 for QwQ, although with higher invalid rates. Against that prompt, ThinkPRM-14B's margin shrinks from about 15 to 18 points to about 9 to 10. The conclusion survives, but the margin in Table 3 and in every downstream LLM-as-a-judge curve is somewhat inflated.

The "1% of the labels" framing is literally true but incomplete as an account of supervision. Each training chain is a QwQ-32B-Preview critique, and roughly 5K teacher samples were drawn to keep 1K. The DiscPRM cannot use that signal. The comparison is therefore between "8K labels plus a 32B reasoning teacher" and "712K labels", not a clean label-efficiency contrast. It is still notable that ThinkPRM-14B (87.3 / 85.7) far exceeds QwQ as a judge (50.6 / 55.5) and QwQ after the same fine-tuning (73.1 / 73.2). The gold-label filter, not imitation of the teacher alone, is doing real work.

The evaluation is small, and no uncertainty is reported. MATH-500 is a 100-problem subset, GPQA-physics has 86 questions, LiveCodeBench has 200 problems, and sequential scaling uses 200 OmniMath problems. The 8% GPQA margin corresponds to about 7 questions, and the 4.5% code margin to 9 problems. There are no seeds, repeated runs, or intervals anywhere, and most test-time results exist only as figures I could not inspect. The direction of the downstream results is consistent across settings, which counts for something, but single-point gaps of a few points on these sizes are not established.

The out-of-domain story is broader than its evidence. The verifier is trained only on math. Its OOD test is one generator per task on two subsets, against one same-base DiscPRM and one off-the-shelf math PRM, and GPQA is multiple choice, so the verifier is selecting answers, not checking physics derivations. The authors' conclusion about "the fragility of discriminative PRMs under domain shifts" generalizes from this. Their own Appendix E.7 note, that 65K chains of math training lowers OOD performance, suggests that part of the OOD advantage may come from how lightly the 1K model is specialized, not from generative verification as such.

Compute is matched against self-consistency but not against DiscPRM. ThinkPRM spends up to 8,192 tokens per candidate, and more with @K or forced re-checks, while DiscPRM needs one forward pass. All DiscPRM comparisons are at equal N. The authors acknowledge the overhead (Sec. 5.8). A practitioner with a fixed budget would still want to know whether DiscPRM with more samples closes the gap, and that experiment was not run.

There are several reporting inconsistencies:

- Table 1 gives 87.3 for the 1.5B long-CoT OlympiadBench score, while Tables 3 and 4 give 76.3.
- The Fig. 5 caption says both verifiers are R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, while the chart shows four models.
- QwQ's single yes/no OmniMath invalid rate is 10.9% in Table 3 but 3.2% in Table 5.
- Appendix C.1 mentions fine-tuning a "Qwen-14B-Instruct" that appears nowhere else.

None of these overturns a conclusion, but together they lower confidence in the exact numbers.

Decoding is a possible confound. ThinkPRM K=1 runs use greedy decoding (App. E.6), and the decoding settings for the LLM-as-a-judge baselines are not stated. Maximization decoding is itself associated with repetition loops ([reviews/1904.09751.md](1904.09751.md)), so some of the looping blamed on untuned judges may come from decoding. That is an open question, not a demonstrated flaw.

The calibration and interference caveats are stated but not quantified. There is no calibration metric and no count of interference cases. One possible contributor to interference is the training data itself: the prompt stops at the first incorrect step, and incorrect labels make up only 7.7% of step labels, so the model rarely sees a correct step judged after an error. That is our conjecture; the paper does not test it.

What remains supported after these caveats is the following. On the two hardest ProcessBench subsets, light fine-tuning on 1K step-filtered verification chains turns R1-distilled Qwen models into much better solution-level error detectors than the same models prompted as judges, and mostly removes non-terminating output. At 14B, the trained verifier beats a same-base classifier trained on the full PRM800K. Long training chains matter (Table 1). Filtering on step labels beats filtering on outcome labels in the one ablation run. Monte Carlo labels can substitute for human labels at 1.5B. The downstream selection and search gains point the same way throughout but are small-sample and unquantified. The OOD and "discriminative PRMs are fragile" claims are suggestive only.

## Relevance to us

VERIFICATION.md leaves open whether precise-but-partial executable labels beat noisy-but-complete learned judges. This paper is a clean example of the complete-coverage side: a learned LLM judge that labels every step. The mechanism that interests us is how it was made. Step labels enter only as a filter on teacher critiques, and Table 4 suggests the filter tolerates cheaper label sources. That points to a concrete experiment for B12, which the paper does not run. We could use our deterministic checker's step verdicts (dimensions, numerical entailment, family oracles) as the filter for teacher critiques of physics derivations, and train a complete verifier from partial but precise labels.

The obvious gap is that ThinkPRM's filter requires every step to match. With coverage around a quarter of steps, we would have to filter only on decided steps. The trained verifier would then issue verdicts well outside the checker's scope, with no guarantee there. That is exactly where our "undecided is fine; false pass/fail is not" rule bites.

The failure modes the authors report support rather than weaken our presentation rule, which prefers neutral flags weighted by known reliability, with the LLM as orchestrator rather than judge. The paper provides no calibration evidence, its scores cluster at 0 and 1, and early errors bias later step labels. A ThinkPRM-style verdict would enter our stack as one uncalibrated signal among several, not as a gate. The training prompt also treats the first "incorrect" step as the end of the story. Physics derivations that drop terms on purpose would be labelled incorrect at the approximation step, with everything after it unlabelled.

The physics evidence here is thin. It amounts to outcome-level selection on 86 GPQA multiple-choice physics questions and says nothing about verifying derivation steps, units, or intentional approximations. Like any stated-step verifier, it cannot see tacit steps ([reviews/2604.14188.md](2604.14188.md)). For B01, the paper does include the right cheap baseline, FLOP-matched self-consistency, and its advantage appears only at higher budgets. That is a useful calibration for how much a learned verifier buys over voting. Table 1 is a practical warning if we ever train a learned verifier: compressing its critiques to save tokens may remove most of the gain.
