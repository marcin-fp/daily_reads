Citation: Luo, Y., Chen, Z., Wang, H., Hu, X., Zhang, Y., Sha, Z., Liu, S. Learning from the Self-future: On-policy Self-distillation for dLLMs. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18195v2

## What this paper is about

Diffusion language models (dLLMs) generate text by iteratively unmasking tokens in any order rather than strictly left-to-right, which makes a popular LLM post-training trick — on-policy self-distillation, where a model teaches itself by comparing its own predictions against a privileged version of itself that has been shown the answer — awkward to apply directly, since the existing recipe assumes left-to-right prefix conditioning and token-by-token supervision. This paper adapts the recipe to dLLMs by (1) building the "privileged teacher" from the student's own self-generated final answer revealed as a suffix, rather than an externally-supplied reference solution, and (2) supervising at the level of each denoising step rather than each token, and shows this new recipe beats reinforcement learning (RLVR) and supervised fine-tuning baselines while needing roughly 10x fewer optimization steps.

## Extended summary

dLLMs generate a response by starting from a fully masked sequence and iteratively revealing the most confident predictions at each of T denoising steps until nothing is masked. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) for autoregressive (AR) models works by having a single model act as both a weaker "student" (conditioned only on the prompt) and a stronger "self-teacher" (conditioned on the prompt plus privileged information, typically a reference solution, appended as a prefix — the only option available to AR models, which can only condition on what comes before), then minimizing the token-level KL divergence between their next-token distributions on the student's own sampled trajectory.

The paper identifies two AR-specific assumptions in this recipe that don't fit dLLMs. First, because dLLMs support suffix conditioning (predicting earlier content given later, already-generated content), privileged information doesn't need to come from an external reference — it can instead be the student's own final self-generated answer, with a subset of its tokens (retaining ratio rho_teacher, default 0.25) randomly revealed to the teacher as suffix context. This "self future-experience" framing means the teacher differs from the student only in already knowing part of what the student itself will eventually produce. Second, because dLLMs decode via simultaneous multi-token, multi-step transitions rather than strict next-token prediction, the paper replaces token-level KL with step-level KL, computed only over the top-k currently-most-confident masked positions that actually transition at each denoising step (with the top-k subset determined by the teacher's distribution, since an ablation shows this outperforms determining it from the student's distribution). The resulting method, d-OPSD, samples an on-policy trajectory via a pass@k-style strategy (default k=8, resampling until a correct final answer is found or the iteration budget is exhausted), constructs the corresponding self-teacher input at each step, and minimizes step-level reverse KL across the full trajectory; a fixed (non-updating) teacher policy and per-token pointwise KL clipping are both used following prior AR OPSD work and shown in ablations to improve stability and performance.

Experiments use LLaDA-8B-Instruct (a pre-existing, not-yet-post-trained dLLM; the authors explicitly exclude the Dream dLLM due to output-format instability affecting RLVR baselines) across four reasoning/planning tasks: GSM8K, MATH500, Countdown, and 4x4 Sudoku. A preliminary "toy verification" (Table 3) confirms the self-teacher construction is informative before running the full method: even a moderate retaining ratio (rho=0.10) lets the teacher substantially outperform the student's own pass@1, and higher ratios approach the student's pass@8 ceiling. Main results (Table 1) show d-OPSD consistently matching or beating both SFT baselines (an SFT variant and the off-policy self-distillation method d3LLM, which uses static ground-truth answers and single-forward-pass "fake trajectory" supervision) and RLVR baselines (diffu-GRPO, VRPO) across most task/sequence-length combinations, with especially large margins on the planning tasks (Countdown, Sudoku). Sample efficiency is the paper's other headline result (Table 2, Figure 1): d-OPSD converges in roughly 10% of the optimization steps required by diffu-GRPO under comparable per-step rollout cost.

A dedicated comparison (Section 4.3) isolates the value of the specific self-teacher construction by building an "AR-style" ablation baseline that instead appends a reference solution as a prefix (matching prior AR OPSD work) while keeping everything else, including the step-level divergence, identical; d-OPSD's suffix-based self-future construction outperforms this AR-style variant (e.g., GSM8K 81.0 vs. 78.4). The paper's proposed explanation is an "Overlap Top-K" metric measuring how much the teacher's and student's top-K predicted-token sets already coincide at each masked position: the AR-style teacher's overlap is "nearly 1" (meaning appending a reference solution barely changes what the model already predicts, so there is little new signal to transfer), whereas d-OPSD's self-future teacher sits in an intermediate overlap range, which the paper interprets as providing more genuinely new information for the student to learn from. Ablations further examine the divergence measure (reverse KL beats forward KL, attributed to reverse KL's mode-seeking versus mode-covering behavior), the retaining ratio (moderate values work best; a very high ratio that produces a stronger teacher does not necessarily produce the best student, suggesting teacher accuracy alone doesn't determine distillation value), top-k subset source (teacher-derived beats student-derived), pass@k value (k=1 still beats the RLVR baseline with even greater sample efficiency, though k=8 performs best), teacher-fixing (fixing the teacher policy helps, though even an unfixed teacher still nearly matches RLVR), and loss masking (restricting the loss to correct generations only slightly outperforms using all generations, both beating RLVR). The paper transparently reports a failure mode: like RLVR, d-OPSD is prone to policy collapse after reaching peak performance in some settings, which the authors attribute to reverse KL's mode-seeking behavior becoming overly narrow over training. ## Learnings

The core architectural insight — that a non-autoregressive, bidirectional generation model can build a self-distillation "teacher" purely from its own successfully-completed rollout (suffix conditioning on self-generated content), rather than requiring an externally-supplied privileged reference answer, as autoregressive on-policy self-distillation does — is a specific, transferable idea for any self-improvement scheme built on non-causal or iterative-refinement generation paradigms: it converts "self-distillation" from something that still needs an external oracle answer into something achievable purely from the model's own successful attempts, extending the on-policy principle one step further than AR-model OPSD.

The "Overlap Top-K" diagnostic (measuring how much a proposed teacher-construction scheme's top-K predicted tokens already coincide with the student's, at the positions being supervised) is a specific, reusable technique for sanity-checking whether a self-distillation teacher construction is actually informative or is close to tautological (near-total overlap implies the "privileged" conditioning barely changes what the model already predicts, so there is little genuine new signal to transfer) — directly applicable to evaluating any proposed privileged-information injection scheme in a self-teacher setup, independent of the specific model architecture involved.

Matching a distillation objective's supervision granularity to the model's native generative unit — moving from token-level KL (natural for autoregressive next-token prediction) to step-level KL over the specific subset of positions that actually transition at a given denoising step (natural for masked-diffusion decoding) — is a specific, transferable principle for adapting AR-designed training objectives to other iterative or non-causal generation paradigms generally, not only diffusion language models.

## Verification

The paper includes a genuine sanity check before the main experiments: a "toy verification" (Table 3) confirming that the proposed self-teacher construction can actually recover correct answers at various retaining ratios before the full training pipeline is run, which is good practice for validating a design assumption rather than only observing downstream benchmark gains after the fact. The ablation suite (Tables 5-9) is reasonably thorough for a single-model, single-scale study, isolating divergence measure, retaining ratio, top-k subset source, pass@k value, teacher-fixing, and loss-masking choices one at a time against a consistent comparison point. The clearest comparability gap is in how the baseline numbers were obtained: per Table 1's own caption, the SFT variant and diffu-GRPO numbers are "sourced from the original paper," while VRPO, d3LLM, and the base model are "evaluated using their open-sourced models" by the present authors — meaning the headline comparison in Table 1 blends numbers produced under at least two different training pipelines (the authors' own LoRA-based d-OPSD runs on 4 GPUs, versus other groups' or the authors' own re-runs of externally released checkpoints/code), rather than every method being trained and evaluated under one single, fully controlled protocol. The sample-efficiency comparison (Table 2, Figure 1) similarly compares d-OPSD's own optimization-step count against a step count "sourced from the original paper" for diffu-GRPO, rather than from a diffu-GRPO run reproduced under identical batch size, hardware, and logging conventions in this paper (the paper does state the per-step rollout cost is matched, i.e., both use group-k rollouts, but full training-loop equivalence beyond that is not established). No repeated-seed variance is reported for any main result or ablation table.

## Field context

The paper positions d-OPSD directly against two lines it explicitly cites and contrasts against: on-policy self-distillation for autoregressive LLMs (the recipe it adapts and whose specific self-teacher-construction and token-level-divergence choices it argues are AR-specific and suboptimal for dLLMs), and existing dLLM self-distillation methods it explicitly differentiates from (d3LLM, Cd4lm), which the paper argues remain off-policy (using static ground-truth answers rather than the student's own generations) and single-forward-pass rather than full-trajectory supervision. Within this corpus, the closest connection is [reviews/2606.13473.md](2606.13473.md) (MaxProof), reviewed earlier in this same batch: both papers independently document a policy-collapse-style instability as a known, recurring failure mode of RL-flavored post-training for reasoning models — MaxProof's M2-cycle reward-hacking collapse under a naive single-judge verifier, and this paper's own reported post-peak policy collapse under d-OPSD's reverse-KL self-distillation objective (Section 4.5) — two structurally different post-training paradigms (generative-verifier RL versus on-policy self-distillation) converging on the same qualitative failure pattern (a training run that looks healthy on an aggregate score before degrading sharply), reinforcing that this is a broadly recurring risk across RL-adjacent LLM/dLLM post-training methods rather than a quirk of any one specific algorithm.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claims are supported by a coherent set of evidence: the toy verification directly checks a necessary precondition (a viable self-teacher) before the full method is deployed; the AR-style ablation (Section 4.3) isolates the specific contribution of the self-future teacher construction while holding the step-level divergence fixed, giving a fairly clean comparison for that one design choice; and the Overlap Top-K metric provides a plausible, quantified mechanistic account for why the self-future construction outperforms the AR-style alternative, rather than asserting the result without explanation.

Two comparability concerns temper the headline numbers. First, the main results table mixes evidence sources: some baseline numbers are copied from the original publications while others are obtained by the present authors re-running open-sourced checkpoints, and the authors' own method is trained via LoRA on 4 GPUs while the reproduced RLVR baseline uses 8 GPUs — none of this necessarily biases the comparison in any particular direction, but it does mean Table 1 and Table 2's headline gains are not evidence from one single, uniformly controlled experimental pipeline, which is the gold standard this kind of comparison would ideally meet. Second, every experiment uses a single base model (LLaDA-8B-Instruct) at a single scale, with the paper explicitly noting a second candidate dLLM (Dream) was excluded due to output-format instability rather than because it was tested and found comparable; combined with a compact four-task benchmark suite, this leaves open how much of the reported advantage over RLVR and SFT is specific to this one model family and evaluation setting versus a more general property of the d-OPSD recipe.

What remains solidly supported after these caveats: the core architectural adaptation (suffix-based self-future teacher construction plus step-level divergence) is shown, via a controlled internal ablation holding the divergence objective fixed, to outperform a direct AR-style transplant of the existing OPSD recipe onto a dLLM, which is the paper's most tightly-isolated and best-evidenced specific claim; and the qualitative pattern of substantially improved sample efficiency relative to an RLVR baseline under matched per-step rollout cost is consistent with, and mechanistically explained by, the broader on-policy-distillation literature's general finding that dense teacher supervision outperforms sparse outcome rewards, even though the precise magnitude of the reported ~10x figure rests on a comparison across two not-fully-identical training pipelines.
