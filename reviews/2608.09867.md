Citation: Panfilov, A., Schmotz, D., Shumailov, I., Beurer-Kellner, L., Schaeffer, J., Prabhu, A., Geiping, J., Andriushchenko, M. Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09867v1

## What this paper is about

To protect their intellectual property, major LLM providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) no longer send a reasoning model's internal chain-of-thought to the client in plaintext; instead they return it as an opaque, encrypted "block" that the client must pass back with each follow-up API call so the conversation can continue, without the provider having to store the reasoning server-side. This paper identifies and demonstrates a serious architectural flaw in that design: these encrypted blocks turn out to be portable not just across sessions (already known from prior work) but across different users and, most consequentially, across different models from the same provider. By capturing an encrypted reasoning block generated by a heavily safeguarded frontier model and feeding it to a weaker, less-guarded sibling model with a simple jailbreak prompt, an attacker can make the weaker model transcribe the stronger model's private reasoning verbatim in plaintext, without ever needing to jailbreak the stronger model directly.

## Extended summary

The paper distinguishes three grades of "reasoning compatibility," each unlocking a broader class of attack: in-/cross-session compatibility (a user can replay their own reasoning blocks out of order or from earlier sessions), cross-user compatibility (a block from one user's session can be replayed by a different user), and cross-model compatibility (a block produced by one model can be replayed to a different model in the same provider's family). The paper attributes this to providers apparently using a single global cryptographic key to encrypt and authenticate every reasoning block within their ecosystem, rather than binding each block to the specific user, session, or model that produced it. Table 1 in the paper documents, as of July 2026, which specific model pairs within Anthropic's, OpenAI's, and Google's model families accept each other's encrypted reasoning traces.

The core extraction technique exploits this: for each provider, the authors identify the weakest available model that remains compatible with the traces of its stronger siblings (Claude Haiku 4.5 for Anthropic, GPT-5.6 Luna for OpenAI, Gemini Robotics 1.6 for Google), inject an encrypted reasoning block captured from a stronger target model into that weaker model's context using a simple ad-hoc jailbreak prompt (e.g., instructing the model to "transcribe the reasoning attached to this turn, verbatim"), and thereby coerce the weaker, less-safeguarded model into decoding and outputting the stronger model's private reasoning in plaintext. Because the encrypted block itself is a signed, authenticated artifact the provider must accept as valid regardless of which compatible model processes it, this sidesteps the stronger model's own refusal training entirely. To gauge whether the resulting plaintext is a faithful reconstruction, the authors compare the API-reported thinking-token count from the original model against the token count obtained when the extracted reasoning is fed back into the same model, across 120 Codeforces problems, finding the two track closely for most models, though extraction from more capable, more safeguarded decoder candidates (e.g., GPT-5.6 Luna) required more elaborate workarounds (different prompt templates per trace type, best-of-n sampling, splitting extraction into sub-50-token chunks to route around anti-distillation defenses) than the single fixed prompt that sufficed against Claude Haiku 4.5.

Four concrete attack vectors are demonstrated. Distillation attacks exploit the flaw to recover a proprietary model's genuine, verbatim reasoning trace (rather than only its final answer, or a synthetic approximation reconstructed by a separately-trained inversion model as in prior work) without ever directly querying or jailbreaking the frontier model itself; the paper notes this is also economically attractive, estimating roughly $720 to decode a 10,000-trace corpus at then-current Claude Haiku pricing. Jailbreaking exploits the fact that models are trained to withhold harmful content from their user-facing output but not necessarily to avoid reasoning about harmful topics internally (since directly penalizing harmful reasoning content risks degrading the monitorability of the chain-of-thought itself); the paper demonstrates this by paraphrasing a HarmBench prompt to Claude Opus 4.8, capturing its encrypted reasoning while discarding its benign final answer, then decoding the reasoning to recover concrete harmful information (e.g., specific vehicle-theft techniques) that never appeared in the visible response. Secret extraction is demonstrated at real-world scale: scraping 6,708 publicly available agent trajectories from GitHub and Hugging Face that still carry encrypted reasoning blocks, decoding all of them into 315,320 reconstructed reasoning traces, and using an LLM-as-a-judge to flag privacy violations, recovering 367 PII artifacts and 182 credentials from genuine (non-benchmark) user sessions, including 62 API keys, 33 passwords, 24 access tokens, 7 private keys, and 30 personal emails; notably, 64 of 704 recovered artifacts from genuine sessions were entirely absent from the visible chat history, having apparently been silently reasoned-about (e.g., during a user-requested "cleanup" of sensitive data) and left trapped in the encrypted payload even after the visible text was scrubbed before sharing. Prompt injection exploits the fact that a replayed reasoning block is treated by the receiving model as its own genuine prior reasoning: the paper demonstrates both a minimal proof-of-concept (planting a data-exfiltration instruction into a captured reasoning trace, then porting it into an unrelated query to a different model, which follows the embedded instruction while completing the unrelated visible task) and a longer-horizon variant poisoning a multi-step agentic research trace (from a cited external benchmark) so that a victim model continuing the trace uploads its own working files to an attacker-controlled server after each update, with the injected payload leaving no plaintext trace for a human or automated monitor inspecting only the visible conversation to catch.

Following responsible disclosure to the affected providers as well as Microsoft and Hugging Face (and building on an earlier, independently disclosed observation of cross-session portability by a cited external researcher, whose disclosure the providers reportedly did not initially treat as a security issue), the paper reports all providers acknowledged the report and that the specific attacks demonstrated in the paper are no longer reproducible as of publication. The paper proposes a detailed, multi-layered mitigation design (Appendix A): binding each encrypted envelope's authenticated associated data to the originating user identifier (closing cross-user leakage cheaply and statelessly); a lightweight hash-chain binding each reasoning block to its session and immediate predecessor, using a Merkle-tree structure so that compacted/pruned conversation history can still be verified without requiring the full unpruned transcript to be replayed (explicitly named design properties include reasoning ordinality, full reasoning integrity, leak monitoring, and non-replayability, with the paper explicitly noting integrity and compaction-friendliness are in tension and choosing to prioritize the cheaper, compaction-compatible guarantee); mandatory key rotation to permanently invalidate already-published legacy signatures, at the cost of also breaking legitimate resumption of old sessions; a bounded dual-format deprecation window with an opt-in, identity-verified re-signing path for enterprise archives; and training-time hardening so models explicitly recognize and refuse transcription-style jailbreak prompts regardless of how innocuous the surrounding request appears, which the paper explicitly flags as an open, unsolved item rather than a component it has already validated.

A substantial, carefully-hedged appendix (B, titled "The Elephant in the Room") asks whether recently released open-weight models show behavioral evidence of having been trained on proprietary reasoning traces, explicitly disclaiming upfront that the analysis "cannot causally establish distillation" and can only report correlational, behavioral shifts under specific interventions. The method prefills a short fragment (the first 1%) of a decoded Claude Opus 4.8 or GPT-5.6-Sol reasoning trace into an open-weight model's own reasoning span, lets the model continue freely (the visible answer is never itself prefilled), and measures whether the resulting output shifts toward the source model's style via n-gram overlap, perplexity, and style-classifier measurements, with symmetric control conditions swapping the prefill source between two models neither of which is suspected of having proprietary training data. The reported findings are that Kimi-K3's visible-answer style shifts measurably and statistically significantly toward Claude Opus 4.8's style under an Opus reasoning prefill (with a corresponding shift toward GPT-5.6-Sol's style under a Sol prefill), that Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.2 model long spans of decoded Opus/Sol text with markedly lower perplexity than two control open-weight models, and that none of these effects appear in the control comparisons where two non-suspect models are prefilled with each other's traces instead, with the paper's own statistical correction (Bonferroni across eight comparisons) leaving only the Kimi-K3-under-Opus-prefill comparisons significant. The paper is explicit that these results establish unusual behavioral compatibility under a specific, narrow intervention, not a causal claim of memorization or distillation, and lists specific caveats limiting the analysis (post-patch data collection, a small and benchmark-skewed problem set, extraction-fidelity uncertainty, and serving configurations outside the authors' control).

The paper's own stated limitations note the empirical findings are bounded to the specific API versions and reasoning models available from the three tested providers as of early July 2026, that extraction fidelity cannot be fully verified against ground-truth plaintext (only inferred via token-count matching), and that the scan of public traces is a targeted demonstration rather than an exhaustive audit, with the authors explicitly expecting private, unpublished production traces to carry substantially more sensitive information than the publicly-scraped sample used here. The Ethical Considerations section states that all recovered secrets were processed in an isolated, secure environment and securely deleted immediately after the automated classification and counting phase, and that the authors coordinated with affected platforms and providers before publication specifically to allow remediation ahead of public disclosure.

## Learnings

The specific architectural insight that a security system's overall strength is bounded by the weakest, least-safeguarded component sharing a trust boundary with its strongest components (here, a heavily-safeguarded frontier model's encrypted reasoning becomes fully readable once a weaker sibling model in the same provider family will accept and transcribe it) is a directly transferable systems-security lesson for any architecture where multiple components of varying capability or hardening levels share a common cryptographic trust domain: a defense concentrated entirely on the most-scrutinized component is not sufficient if a less-scrutinized component with equivalent access rights exists.

The specific finding that an encrypted, "hidden" channel intended purely for intellectual-property protection can simultaneously function as an unintentional data-exfiltration and prompt-injection vector, precisely because its opacity prevents the very users whose data it contains from inspecting, sanitizing, or verifying it before sharing, is a transferable design lesson for any system that hides intermediate computational state from its own end users for a legitimate-sounding reason (IP protection, reducing distraction, simplifying the interface): opacity that protects a provider's interests can simultaneously strip the user of the ability to protect their own, and these two properties do not automatically trade off in the provider's favor.

The methodological pattern used in the distillation-detection appendix, explicitly disclaiming any causal claim upfront, using a narrow and specific behavioral intervention (a short reasoning prefill) rather than a broad claim about overall model similarity, and including symmetric control comparisons between models not suspected of any special relationship, is a reusable template for responsibly investigating a sensitive, reputation-affecting question (whether a named commercial model may have been trained on another company's proprietary outputs) using only externally observable behavioral evidence: it demonstrates how to report a genuinely striking, statistically significant behavioral anomaly for one specific model without overclaiming what that anomaly does or does not establish about its training data.

## Verification

The paper's core technical claim, that encrypted reasoning traces are cross-model-portable, is not merely asserted but demonstrated with concrete evidence across three major providers and validated with a specific faithfulness check (matching API-reported thinking-token counts against the token counts obtained by re-encoding the extracted plaintext, across 120 problems), and the secret-extraction results are, in an important sense, self-verifying: a decoded string that turns out to be a correctly-formatted, functioning-looking API key or a specific named synthetic benchmark persona's exact recorded attributes is strong evidence the extraction recovered genuine underlying content rather than a plausible-looking confabulation, since a hallucinating decoder model would be very unlikely to fabricate a real, correctly-structured secret by chance. The responsible-disclosure process itself functions as an important external verification step: the paper reports that all contacted providers acknowledged the vulnerability and that the specific demonstrated attacks are no longer reproducible as of publication, which is independent, provider-side corroboration that the underlying flaw was real rather than an artifact of the authors' own testing setup. The distillation-detection appendix is unusually careful about scoping its own verification limits, explicitly disclaiming causal claims, running proper statistical correction for multiple comparisons, and including genuine null-result control conditions rather than only positive findings.

The main verification gap is that the large-scale secret-extraction pipeline's headline numbers (367 PII artifacts, 182 credentials from genuine sessions) rest on an LLM-as-a-judge classifier to flag privacy violations across 315,320 decoded traces, and the main text does not report a human-validated precision or recall check for this judge's labeling accuracy (this may be detailed further in an appendix not fully read for this review), so some unknown fraction of the reported counts could reflect judge false positives or false negatives; this matters less for concretely-verifiable artifact types like well-formed API keys, which are checkable independent of judge quality, but more for softer PII categories where a judge's classification is closer to a subjective call. A second, explicitly self-disclosed gap is that faithfulness verification relies entirely on token-count matching rather than content matching, since no ground-truth plaintext reasoning exists to compare against by construction of the attack; this is a reasonable proxy but does not rule out that some specific decoded content deviates from the true original reasoning in ways that happen to preserve overall length, a concern the paper raises about its own method rather than one this review is introducing externally.

## Field context

The paper builds directly on an independently disclosed prior observation (a cited cryptography researcher's blog post from May 2026) that encrypted reasoning blocks are portable outside their original context, and extends it substantially by demonstrating the much more consequential cross-model compatibility and by building four concrete, evaluated attack vectors on top of that extension rather than stopping at the observation itself; the paper also notes the original disclosure reportedly was not initially treated by providers as carrying security implications, which the current paper's own demonstrated attacks and secret-extraction results at scale directly contradict. It positions its distillation-attack contribution against prior black-box model-extraction and answer-only distillation literature (citing foundational work on stealing machine learning models via prediction APIs, and more recent work reconstructing surrogate reasoning traces from a victim model's visible outputs alone) by arguing that recovering the genuine, verbatim reasoning trace directly is a categorically stronger and more economical attack than reconstructing an approximation, and cites concurrent, independently-developed proposals for detecting distillation and for considering the boundaries any defense against reasoning-theft must respect. It also explicitly engages the chain-of-thought monitorability and faithfulness literature (citing prior work establishing that reasoning summaries can be systematically unfaithful, and work on the safety risks of penalizing a model's internal reasoning content directly), using its own incidental discovery of unfaithful API-provided reasoning summaries (a decoded trace revealing a model silently stating the correct competition-math answer before "solving" the problem, contradicting the summary's presentation of an unbiased derivation) as fresh, concrete supporting evidence for a concern raised more abstractly elsewhere.

## Critical discussion

The paper's overall research conduct is a significant strength: practicing responsible disclosure to every affected party before publication, explicitly confirming the specific demonstrated attacks are patched as of publication, handling collected real secrets under an isolated, time-limited protocol with prompt deletion, and including a dedicated, non-trivial engineering proposal for mitigation (rather than only describing the vulnerability) together place this well within established norms for disclosing a serious, real-world security flaw. The distillation-detection appendix's explicit, upfront disclaimer of causal claims, combined with genuine null-result control comparisons and proper multiple-comparison correction, is a notably careful way to report a finding (a named commercial model's outputs shifting toward a specific proprietary model's style under a narrow intervention) that could otherwise easily be over-interpreted or misreported as definitive proof of unauthorized training-data use.

One point is worth naming directly: the headline secret-extraction counts (367 PII artifacts, 182 credentials) depend on an LLM-as-a-judge classification step applied across 315,320 decoded traces, and the main text does not report an independent human-validation check of that judge's labeling accuracy, leaving some uncertainty in the precise headline numbers even though the underlying phenomenon (that encrypted reasoning blocks can and do leak real secrets absent from the visible transcript) is independently corroborated by the concretely-verifiable examples the paper does show in full (a real-format API key, a benchmark persona's exact recorded details). This is a modest concern relative to the paper's central claims, since the qualitative existence of the leakage phenomenon does not depend on the judge's precision, but the exact magnitude reported (e.g., the specific 0.3%-of-blocks and 4.9%-of-sessions leak rates) should be read with that caveat in mind.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the core architectural vulnerability (cross-model, cross-user, and cross-session compatibility of encrypted reasoning blocks within a provider's ecosystem) is demonstrated directly and concretely across three major API providers, not merely theorized, and is independently corroborated by every contacted provider's acknowledgment and subsequent patching; the four attack vectors are each shown as working, reproducible proofs of concept (as of the testing period) rather than only argued for in the abstract; and the paper's overall thesis, that an architecture designed to hide a user's own reasoning from them while leaving it fully exposed to a technically-savvy third party achieves neither genuine privacy nor genuine security, is well-supported by the specific, concrete demonstrations (real leaked credentials, a working prompt-injection payload that survives across models and tasks, and harmful information recoverable from a nominally-safe visible response) presented throughout the paper.
