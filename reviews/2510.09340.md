Citation: Maltoni, D., Ferrara, M. Deductive Logic in Language Models: Horizontal vs Vertical Reasoning. arXiv preprint arXiv:2510.09340 (v1 2025, v2 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09340v2

## What this paper is about

How does a transformer actually carry out a chain of logical deductions? The authors train very small GPT-2-style models from scratch on two symbolic tasks. In the first, the model decides whether a query implication follows from five given implications. In the second, it outputs the root-to-leaf path in a 16-node binary tree. They then reverse-engineer what the models learned. They separate two modes. In horizontal reasoning, the model writes out intermediate steps and reuses them. In vertical reasoning, the model must finish most of the computation inside its layers before it emits the first output token. The claims are as follows. With chain-of-thought (CoT) supervision, a 2-layer attention-only model learns an interpretable rule-following algorithm built from induction heads. In the vertical case, CoT seems to act more like a curriculum than like step-by-step guidance. Without CoT, the models either memorize or exploit biases in how the data were generated.

## Extended summary

Task 1 (logical consequence). The prompt gives five Horn implications a_i > b_i and a query q0 > q1. Literals come from the first 20 uppercase letters. The implications have no cycles, and a positive example has exactly one path from q0 to q1. Chains range from 1 to 5 steps. The generator (Appendix A) first builds a negative example: it picks m+2 = 7 distinct literals, forms a 5-rule chain, sets the query to the chain's endpoints, breaks one rule by injecting the seventh literal, and shuffles the rules. This gives 468,840,960,000 possible negatives. A negative whose break point is b > 1 becomes a positive by setting q1 to the injected literal, so the two classes look alike. A hash table prevents train/validation overlap. Without CoT, the output is one token (1 or 0). With CoT, the output lists the chain's implications in reasoning order, pads to five slots with underscores, and ends with the decision token. The guiding example is prompt `C>D,A>B,B>C,E>F,D>E|A>F` with target `A>B,B>C,C>D,D>E,E>F1`. The prompt is 24 tokens, the output 23, and the vocabulary 28.

Task 2 (root-to-leaf navigation). This task comes from Brinkmann et al. (2024). The prompt is a shuffled list of the parent-child links of a 16-node binary tree, followed by a target leaf. The output is the path from the root to that leaf, up to 15 steps. There are about 2 x 10^19 labeled trees. Going up from a leaf is deterministic, but going down requires a choice at every node with two children. So the first output token (the root) depends on the whole backward path. For a no-CoT variant, the authors delete one link, either on the true path (label 0, unreachable) or on another branch (label 1), and train a binary reachability classifier.

Models. All models are NanoGPT-style decoders in TransformerLens, trained from scratch, with greedy decoding and full-sequence accuracy. The base configuration has 6 layers, 8 heads, d_model 64, and MLPs. The Task 1 model has 2 layers, 1 head, d_model 128, no MLP, and tied input/output embeddings: 144,384 parameters. The Task 2 model has 6 layers, 1 head, d_model 128, and MLPs: 1,203,605 parameters. Appendix C lists architecture only. It gives no optimizer, learning rate, batch size, or weight decay.

Horizontal result (Section 5). The 2-layer model trains on 3,072 examples (50% positive) and validates on 1,024. It reaches about 100% on both within a few hundred epochs. Only about 20% of runs (different data and seeds) converged within 250 epochs. With the full NanoGPT architecture the rate was 80%. The authors say the success rate is not critical, since they analyze a few converged models. They combine four techniques: a custom attention visualizer, LogitLens decoding of the residual stream, attention averaged over many prompts (which leaves only position-dependent links), and a new truncated-pseudoinverse decoding of queries, keys, and values. The inference breaks into three stages.

- Rule completion. Layer-1 links copy tokens one or two positions forward, independent of token content. A layer-2 induction head at each `>` position then takes the current literal (say A) as query, finds the rule whose head holds A, and releases its tail (B).
- Rule chaining. At each `,` position, a layer-2 induction head searches for a rule whose head is the previous tail. Only rule-head positions are valid targets. Because the syntax is rigid, rule heads sit at fixed positions, and the authors say learned positional encodings tag those positions. At rule-head positions, `,` appears as the second-ranked decoded token, which suggests the key-query match also uses the separator.
- Start and final decision. Averaged attention shows a link copying the query head into the start token and another copying the query tail into the last position. The tail ranked only third in LogitLens there and was found by manual inspection. Links 61 to 65 then check whether that tail appears at any tail position of the generated chain, which handles chains of 1 to 5 steps.

Statistical validation (Section 5.7) uses a fresh set of 4,096 examples and covers only rule completion and rule chaining. The copied token has mean LogitLens rank 1.072 (completion) and 1.011 (chaining). The induction-head attention puts 99.2% (completion) and 99.8% (chaining) of its weight on the single expected source position. Pseudoinverse decoding gives mean expected-token ranks of Q 1.006, K 1.040, V 1.000 for completion and 1.000, 1.015, 1.000 for chaining. The authors say other converged models showed the same process "aside from minor variations", but they give neither the count nor the variations.

The truncated pseudoinverse (Appendix B) works as follows. W_Q, W_K, and W_V are square and full rank when there is a single head. The method keeps the top-k singular directions, chosen by a cumulative singular-value threshold, maps the projected vector back to residual space, and applies LogitLens. Thresholds of 0.75 to 0.85 worked best for W_Q and W_V, keeping about 50 of 128 dimensions. W_K needed 0.95 to 0.99, keeping about 110. The authors say choosing thresholds automatically needs more work.

Training dynamics (Appendix C). One Task 1 run shows two jumps before converging near epoch 65: 20% accuracy at epoch 30 (t1) and 60% at epoch 50 (t2). At t1 the rule induction heads exist but work only on the first implications in a chain, which matches the training distribution. At t2, sequence accuracy excluding the decision token is already 100%, but the decision circuit has not formed, and last-token accuracy is 54.7%. Other runs showed plateaus at 20 to 30% and 60 to 80%. The authors link the jumps to induction-head formation, in line with Nanda et al., and suggest the refinement can take several stages.

Vertical result (Section 6). The authors started from Brinkmann et al.'s code and data and fixed three things. The original code measured token-level rather than sequence-level accuracy. The generator had a bias (see below). Parent and child nodes used different tokens, which the authors unified. Their 6-layer model trained on 150K examples reached 99.9% train and validation accuracy in about 50 epochs, matching Brinkmann et al. The mechanism described follows Brinkmann et al. At the last prompt position, the model chains backward from the leaf, one induction-head hop per layer, and stores the intermediate nodes in the residual stream. "Register" token positions serve as working memory. When a path is longer than the depth, partial paths are computed in parallel and merged, by a mechanism neither paper explains. Figure 6 illustrates backward chaining with an idealized 5-layer model. The authors note that repeated boolean squaring of (A + I) doubles the reachable path length per step, so 6 layers could in principle cover depth 15 (log2 15 < 6), and they relate this to Sanford et al.'s logarithmic-depth results. With the 2-layer attention-only Task 1 model, root-to-leaf CoT training reaches only 54% validation accuracy after 50 epochs. Reversing the output to leaf-to-root makes the task horizontal: the same 2-layer model reaches 99.7% after 2 epochs, with induction-head circuits like those for rule completion.

Without CoT (Section 7). On Task 1, the full model (6 layers, 8 heads, d_model 128, MLP) trained with a binary target fits the training set almost perfectly, with up to 150K examples, but stays near chance on validation. The authors read this as memorization. An earlier generator made every positive a full 5-step chain and used the extra literal only in negatives. On that data, a 2-layer, 2-head model with MLP learned the task without CoT from 36,864 examples. It had no induction heads. Targeted tests showed it counted distinct literals, which is one higher in negatives. A CoT model trained on that same data behaved like the Section 5 model. On Task 2, the no-CoT full model first reached 80 to 90% after 50 epochs. The authors traced this to a bias in Brinkmann et al.'s gen.py (Appendix D). Nodes off the main path are inserted in increasing ID order, so in every such parent-child pair the child has the larger ID. Brinkmann et al.'s CoT findings still hold. After removing the bias, no-CoT accuracy was 50 to 60% after 50 epochs.

CoT as curriculum (Section 8). To explain why Task 2 with CoT converges even though the first token needs the full backward path, the authors tracked what the model got right during training. At epochs 1, 2, and 10, teacher-forced token accuracy is higher for nodes nearer the target leaf, and autoregressive sequence accuracy is higher for shorter paths. At epoch 2, mid-length paths are hardest, which the authors attribute to balanced trees requiring more binary choices. The bar values are only in Figures 7 and 8 and are not in the extract. They conclude that CoT lets the model learn easy sub-problems first and then reuse them.

Author caveats (Section 9). The Task 1 analysis depends on shallow depth, tied embeddings, and rigid symbolic syntax, which is what makes LogitLens and pseudoinverse decoding work. It may not transfer to large pretrained models or natural language. Path merging in Task 2 is not understood. The authors contrast these results with their earlier arithmetic study, where the model behaved like a statistical regressor. They suggest models may use symbolic or sub-symbolic strategies depending on the task. Future work: backtracking tasks, multiple proof paths, path fusion, and one-layer induction via decoupled key and value positions.

## Learnings

A 2-layer, single-head, attention-only transformer can implement multi-step Horn-clause deduction as a repeated induction-head lookup. Layer 1 copies tokens forward by position so that each rule's head and tail share a residual stream. Layer 2 then searches the rule list for a head that matches the current literal and releases its tail. The caveat is that the search depends on rule heads sitting at fixed prompt positions, so the circuit is tied to the template, not to deduction in general.

Training on a one-bit outcome label picked up whatever label-correlated statistic the generator left in the data: a count of distinct literals in one case, an ordering of node IDs in another. When no such statistic existed, the model memorized. Two small generator choices produced these shortcuts, and in one case mechanistic inspection exposed it (no induction heads were present). Any synthetic benchmark with planted structure deserves the same audit before its accuracy is read as capability.

The direction of the output trace can decide whether a shallow model can learn a task at all. The same 2-layer model scores 54% when it must emit a tree path root-first and 99.7% when it emits the path leaf-first, because leaf-first order lets each emitted token be computed from the previous one. When designing CoT or proof formats, order the steps the way the model can compute them incrementally.

In the vertical case, the emitted path is a readout of a backward computation the model has already finished inside its layers. It is not a step-by-step trace of how the answer was reached. Correct CoT output therefore does not by itself show that the tokens were the computation. By the authors' account, the tokens still helped training, because the later, easier tokens and the shorter paths were learned first.

Accuracy plateaus in training lined up with distinct circuits forming in turn. The rule circuits worked fully (100% accuracy on everything except the final token) while the decision token sat near chance (54.7%). A single sequence-accuracy curve would hide this, and per-position accuracy would show it.

## Verification

This is not a paper about verifiers, but it does audit evaluations. It scores with full-sequence accuracy and notes that Brinkmann et al.'s code scored token-level accuracy, which is more lenient. It identifies two generator biases that inflated no-CoT results: literal counting in the authors' own earlier Task 1 generator, and child-ID-greater-than-parent-ID ordering in Brinkmann et al.'s tree generator (Appendix D). Removing the latter dropped no-CoT Task 2 accuracy from 80 to 90% to 50 to 60%. It uses a hash table to keep training and validation examples disjoint. Its main argument is that mechanistic analysis, not accuracy alone, is needed to tell rule learning from shortcut exploitation. In one case, inspection plus targeted tests showed a high-accuracy model was counting literals. Inside the learned model, the Task 1 decision circuit is itself a small check: it tests whether the query's tail appears among the tails of the generated chain. All checks on the model's inner workings are passive (attention patterns, LogitLens, pseudoinverse decoding). There are no ablations or activation patching.

## Field context

Authors' positioning. They split prior mechanistic work on deduction into two groups. Group A studies pretrained models of medium size on 1- or 2-step problems: Kim et al. 2025 on syllogisms, Hong et al. 2025 on propositional circuits in Mistral and Gemma, Chen et al. 2026 on Qwen3. These studies find high-level circuits but no low-level mechanism, and cannot rule out shortcuts. Group B trains small models from scratch: Guo et al. 2025 on two-hop reasoning with distractors, Ye et al. 2025 on implicit reasoning, and Brinkmann et al. 2024 on binary-tree traversal up to 15 steps. The authors place themselves in group B, with Brinkmann et al. as the closest predecessor and the source of Task 2. They say their work began in parallel with it. The mechanistic vocabulary comes from the induction-head literature (Elhage et al. 2021, Olsson et al. 2022) and the grokking phase-change result (Nanda et al. 2023). The depth argument draws on Sanford et al. 2024 (ICML) on parallel computation in logarithmic depth. The authors contrast this paper with their own 2024 Neural Networks study of arithmetic, where the model looked like a statistical regressor.

What changes. The paper adds three things to the group-B line: longer explicit chains in Task 1 (up to 5 steps against the usual 2), a separation of horizontal from vertical reasoning within one framework, and the reading that CoT works as a curriculum for vertical learning. It also corrects Brinkmann et al.'s accuracy metric and exposes a generator bias that matters for no-CoT experiments, while confirming Brinkmann et al.'s CoT results. v1 (October 2025) covered the horizontal Task 1 analysis. v2 (June 2026) adds the tree task and the horizontal/vertical framing.

Connections to reviewed work (my inference). The closest corpus neighbor is reviews/2405.17399.md (Abacus Embeddings). It also trains small transformers from scratch on algorithmic tasks, finds that position tracking is what the task hinges on, and shows looped recurrence helping multi-step computation. Here the Task 1 circuit also leans on positional tags for valid rule positions, but the paper never tests the length or format generalization that the Abacus work is about. reviews/2410.05229.md (GSM-Symbolic) shows pretrained LLMs are sensitive to surface changes in math word problems. This paper shows a toy-scale version of the same worry: outcome-only training will latch onto generator regularities. The scale and mechanism differ, but the caution about benchmark regularities is shared. The looped and latent-reasoning line in reviews/2602.11451.md, reviews/2606.18023.md, and reviews/2608.09888.md assumes multi-step computation can happen inside depth or recurrence without verbalizing. This paper gives a small mechanistic picture of what that costs: about one hop per layer, with parallel path fusion when depth runs short. It tests no looped models. Its Task 1 model is the attention-only kind that reviews/2309.08593.md argues is easier to interpret. The vertical-case point, that emitted CoT tokens are a readout of a computation already finished, is a toy instance of the gap between stated and actual reasoning that reviews/2401.07927.md measures for self-explanations. This paper does not discuss faithfulness itself.

## Critical discussion

For rule completion and rule chaining, the horizontal account is well supported, more so than most circuit stories. With one head and two layers there are few possible mechanisms. The attention concentration (99.2% and 99.8% on the single expected source) and the near-1 decoded ranks come from 4,096 fresh examples, not one showcase prompt. Still, all the evidence is passive. The related-work section discusses causal mediation and activation patching, yet the paper never ablates or patches the claimed links to show they are necessary or sufficient. The decision circuit (links 41, 51, and 61 to 65) is weaker still. It rests on the guiding example, on averaged attention over positives, and on a manual inspection in which the query tail ranked only third, and Section 5.7 does not cover it. The text also never explains how the model handles a broken chain in negative examples, that is, what it attends to when no rule head matches and how it decides to emit padding. "Largely explained" is fair. "Fully explainable", as Section 5's opening puts it, goes further than the evidence.

The generalization claim is in-distribution only. Validation examples come from the same generator: exactly five rules, 20 literals, a fixed 24-token layout, and no distractor rules beyond the chain and one break. The model's search uses positional encodings to mark rule-head positions (Section 5.5), so the learned algorithm is very likely specific to this template. The paper does not test more rules, longer chains, other literal counts, or shifted layouts, even though its introduction motivates the work with LLMs failing on out-of-distribution inputs and harder multi-step problems. "Rule-based rather than statistical shortcuts" is therefore supported in a narrow sense: the model does not use a label-correlated surface statistic, and it runs an explicit lookup-and-chain procedure on the training format. That it learned deduction portable beyond this format is not shown. This is the basis for the narrow-evaluation flag.

The no-CoT negative results carry much of the argument, but their training conditions are not reported. Appendix C gives architectures but no optimizer, learning rate, batch size, weight decay, epoch count for the Task 1 no-CoT runs, or number of seeds. The Task 2 no-CoT figures (80 to 90% biased, 50 to 60% debiased) are "after 50 epochs". Even the successful 2-layer CoT model converged in only about 20% of runs within 250 epochs. The authors themselves cite the grokking literature, where a phase of memorization with chance-level validation can come before delayed generalization under suitable regularization. The Task 1 no-CoT runs show exactly that pattern, so they do not rule out that longer training or different regularization would generalize. There is also a design confound. The no-CoT Task 2 is a different task (reachability after deleting a link, one bit per example), so the CoT versus no-CoT comparison changes the task and the amount of supervision at the same time. The supported claim is narrower: under the authors' unreported budget, binary supervision did not produce generalizing solutions. This is the main basis for the incomplete-reporting flag, together with the unreported count of "additional models" checked for consistency.

The curriculum reading of CoT in vertical reasoning is plausible, but it rests on training dynamics alone. That near-leaf tokens and short paths are learned first (Figures 7 and 8, values not in the extract) fits the curriculum idea. It also fits the simpler explanation that CoT supplies up to about 15 supervised tokens per example instead of one bit. No experiment separates the two. The obvious controls are missing: no-CoT training with an explicit short-to-long curriculum (the capacity is there, as the authors themselves argue in Section 7.1), and CoT training with loss only on the first token or with the easy tokens removed. The introduction says the paper analyzes conditions "in the presence and absence of CoT and Curriculum Learning", but no explicit curriculum condition appears in the experiments. The abstract's hedge ("appears to act") is the right strength. Finding 2 in the introduction states it more firmly than the evidence allows. This is the basis for the missing-control flag.

The vertical mechanism is mostly inherited. Section 6 reproduces Brinkmann et al.'s result with useful fixes, but gives no statistics of its own on the Task 2 circuits. Figure 6 is an idealized 5-layer illustration, and its caption is internally inconsistent: it says the layer-2 head "finds 4 → 9, and stores 4", while the Figure 1 link list has 5 → 9 and the figure's own picture text reads K=9, V=5. The repeated-squaring argument is an in-principle bound on depth. It is not shown to be what the model does, and the authors say path merging is unexplained. The claim that horizontal reasoning is easier than vertical is made partly by comparing Task 1 with Task 2, which differ in chain length, branching, model depth, MLPs, and data size (roughly 4K versus 150K examples). The cleaner evidence is the within-task comparison on Task 2: the same 2-layer model gets 54% root-first and 99.7% leaf-first. That comparison does support the claim.

The truncated-pseudoinverse decoder is a reasonable heuristic, not a validated instrument. The thresholds are set by hand per matrix and called "optimal" without a stated criterion. The Section 5.7 decoded ranks are computed under those chosen thresholds, so the agreement with expected tokens partly reflects the tuning. The method depends on tied embeddings and a shallow model, which the authors acknowledge. For multi-head models, where the projections are rectangular, it is untested.

What remains supported: a tiny attention-only transformer trained with full-trace supervision on a fixed-format 5-rule chain task learns an induction-head lookup-and-chain procedure that generalizes within the distribution. With the same supervision, it ignores a literal-count bias that a no-CoT model exploits. The audit of the Brinkmann et al. generator and metric is concrete and useful to anyone reusing that dataset. Within Task 2, reversing the output order turns a task a shallow model cannot learn into one it learns in two epochs. The claim that CoT works as a curriculum, and the claim that no-CoT training fails in general rather than under this budget, remain hypotheses.

## Relevance to us

For B20 (structured memory and looped computation for Transformers), this is a small but clean data point. It shows that capacity to reason implicitly is not enough. The 6-layer Task 1 model had enough depth for a 5-hop vertical chain and still memorized under outcome-only supervision. On the tree task, whether a trace was present decided whether the depth was used. By this paper's account, implicit multi-step inference costs about one hop per layer, with parallel path fusion when depth runs short. A looped or recurrent design would need to show what training signal makes it use its extra iterations, not only that the iterations exist. The paper tests no looped models, and its tasks are toy symbolic chains, so this is a design caution, not evidence about physics-scale reasoning.

For our synthetic and planted-problem evaluations (the label-factory and family-oracle ideas in the verification programme), the generator audit is directly useful. Two innocent generator choices, all positives at full length and nodes inserted in ID order, let outcome-trained models reach 80 to 90% without doing the task. Any family-oracle problem generator we build should be checked for label-correlated surface statistics, and scored at the sequence or process level. The CoT-versus-outcome contrast is also weak, toy-scale support for training on verified intermediate steps rather than final labels only. The confounds noted above (supervision density, unreported budgets) mean we should not cite it as a clean process-versus-outcome result.
