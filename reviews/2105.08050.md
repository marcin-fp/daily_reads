Citation: Liu, H., Dai, Z., So, D. R., Le, Q. V. Pay Attention to MLPs. NeurIPS 2021. arXiv:2105.08050.

## What this paper is about

Transformers combine two separable ideas: parallel (non-recurrent) per-token computation, and multi-head self-attention that dynamically decides how tokens interact based on their content. This paper isolates the second idea and asks whether it is actually necessary, by building gMLP, a Transformer alternative with no self-attention at all, just channel-mixing MLPs (like a Transformer's feedforward block) plus a spatial "gating" MLP with static, input-independent spatial weights. Tested on ImageNet classification and on BERT-style masked language modeling, gMLP matches or nearly matches Transformer performance and scales with data and compute just as well; where it falls short (multi-sentence NLP tasks needing cross-sentence alignment), the gap closes either by making gMLP much bigger or, more efficiently, by adding back only a tiny sliver of single-head self-attention.

## Extended summary

A gMLP block is a channel-mixing MLP (identical in structure to a Transformer's FFN, same U/V channel-projection matrices) wrapped around a spatial-interaction layer s(.). The paper's central design contribution is the Spatial Gating Unit (SGU): rather than a plain linear spatial projection s(Z) = W.Z + b, the input Z is split into two channel-wise halves (Z1, Z2), the second half is linearly projected across the spatial (token) dimension with a matrix W initialized near-zero and bias initialized to ones (so the gate starts as an identity, s(Z) is approximately Z, and each block behaves like a plain FFN early in training before gradually learning cross-token interactions), and the output is the element-wise product Z1 times f(Z2). Unlike self-attention's dynamically input-generated interaction weights, W is static, learned once and applied identically regardless of input content; the paper notes this constrains the SGU to at most 2nd-order token interactions versus self-attention's up to 3rd-order interactions, while having comparable quadratic-in-sequence-length compute cost. gMLP requires no position embeddings, since the token-mixing information is captured directly in the spatially-indexed W. Table 3's ablation over several s(.) variants (identity/plain-linear, additive, multiplicative, multiplicative-with-split) establishes that the multiplicative, split-channel gating (i.e., the full SGU) is specifically what closes most of the gap to a full self-attention Transformer, rather than gating or spatial projection alone being sufficient.

For image classification (Section 3), gMLP models (Ti/S/B scales, Table 1) trained on ImageNet-1k without extra data, using DeiT-style regularization (AutoAugment, mixup, cutmix, stochastic depth, label smoothing, but explicitly not DeiT's repeated augmentation or random erasing), reach accuracy comparable to DeiT (ViT with improved regularization) at matched capacity, and surpass MLP-Mixer and ResMLP at matched parameter/FLOP budgets (Table 2): gMLP-S reaches 79.6% top-1 with 20M parameters versus Mixer-B/16's 76.4-77.3% with 59M parameters, i.e., roughly 66% fewer parameters for 3 points higher accuracy, which the paper attributes to the SGU specifically rather than the general MLP-mixing idea (validated by Table 3's within-paper ablation). Standard deviation across independent ImageNet runs is reported as about 0.1 points. gMLP still trails the best dedicated ConvNets (EfficientNet-B7, NFNet-F0) and hybrid attention-convolution models. Visualizing gMLP-B's learned spatial projection matrices (Figure 3) shows they exhibit both locality and spatial invariance, i.e., each spatial weight matrix behaves like a convolution with a data-driven, non-square kernel shape, despite no convolutional structure being imposed architecturally.

For masked language modeling (Section 4, BERT setup, C4 pretraining data), the paper first observes that gMLP's spatial weight matrices consistently learn Toeplitz-like (shift-invariant) structure on their own during MLM pretraining, consistent with MLM's inherent shift-invariance (any uniform offset of the input sequence should not change slot-filling answers); the paper then constrains W to be exactly Toeplitz going forward, reporting this constraint as empirically quality-neutral while reducing redundant parameters. Table 3's ablation (125K-step runs on a C4 subset) compares BERT-Transformer baselines (absolute position embeddings; T5-style relative position biases; relative-position-only with content-based attention removed, equivalent to a Random Synthesizer), MLP-Mixer adapted to MLM, and four gMLP variants at matched scale: the full multiplicative-split SGU reaches 4.35 perplexity, close to the strongest Transformer baseline's 4.26 and clearly ahead of the attention-removed Transformer (5.64) and MLP-Mixer (5.34) baselines, with reported run-to-run standard deviation around 0.01. Table 4's scaling study (depth scaled 0.5x-4x, three fixed data regimes) finds gMLP and Transformer perplexity both follow an approximate power law with model size and gMLP is competitive or better in absolute perplexity at every scale tested; however, finetuning results diverge by task, gMLP outperforms Transformer on the single-sentence SST-2 task at every scale but underperforms on the sentence-pair MNLI task by 2-4 points, despite comparable or better pretraining perplexity, which the paper interprets as evidence that finetuning transfer depends on architectural inductive bias, not perplexity alone, and specifically suspects self-attention's role is cross-sentence alignment.

Section 4.3 tests that hypothesis directly by building aMLP, gMLP with a single, tiny (64 or 128-dimensional, single-head) self-attention module attached inside the gating path, dramatically smaller than a standard Transformer's multi-head attention (12 heads x 64 = 768 total). aMLP consistently outperforms both plain gMLP and full Transformer baselines on both SST-2 and MNLI across the same scaling sweep (Figure 8), and Appendix D's visualization of the tiny attention's learned weights on MNLI examples shows they concentrate specifically on aligning corresponding words/phrases across the premise and hypothesis sentence pair. In the full BERT setup (Table 5-6, 1M training steps on full English C4, GLUE/SQuAD finetuning results reported as the median of five runs), gMLPbase and gMLPlarge roughly match or slightly beat Transformer perplexity at comparable capacity, and gMLPlarge reaches 89.5% F1 on SQuAD v1.1 with no self-attention at all, above the original BERTbase's 88.5% and only 1.4 points below original BERTlarge; a much larger gMLPxlarge (941M vs. BERTlarge's 336M parameters) exceeds BERTlarge on SQuAD v2.0 with no self-attention, which the paper explicitly labels "not a fair comparison due to different model sizes" but offers as an existence proof of MLP-only competitiveness. The hybrid aMLPlarge (with only a 128-dim single-head attention) outperforms BERTlarge on every finetuning task reported, including a 4.4-point F1 gain on SQuAD v2.0, using fewer total parameters (316M) than BERTlarge (336M).

## Learnings

The paper's most transferable finding is the precise localization of what self-attention is actually buying a Transformer on NLP finetuning tasks: not perplexity (gMLP matches that with no attention at all), but specifically cross-sentence alignment on tasks that require relating two separate text spans (MNLI's premise/hypothesis pairs), evidenced both by the SST-2/MNLI performance split and directly visualized in the tiny-attention weight maps concentrating on cross-sentence word alignment. This is a concrete instance of a broader reusable diagnostic: when a component seems to matter on some tasks but not others, checking whether the tasks it helps share a specific structural property (here, needing to relate two distinct inputs) can localize a mechanism's actual function far more precisely than an aggregate benchmark score would.

A second, practically valuable learning is that a full-capacity replacement is not the only way to recover a missing capability: rather than making gMLP substantially larger (3x, as the paper notes is one working but expensive fix) to close the MNLI gap, attaching a disproportionately tiny amount of the original mechanism (a single 64-128 dimensional attention head, versus a standard 768-dimensional multi-head block) recovers, and in this case exceeds, the missing capability at a small fraction of the parameter cost. This is a specific, quantified example of a general principle worth carrying elsewhere: when ablating a component reveals a capability gap, the fix that restores the gap is not always "put the whole component back," and a minimal reintroduction can be both cheaper and more effective than either extreme (fully removing or fully restoring).

A third, quieter architectural learning: gMLP's spatial gating weights, despite having no built-in constraint to do so, spontaneously learn shift-invariant (Toeplitz) structure during MLM pretraining specifically because the task itself (predicting a masked token) is invariant to a uniform sequence offset; the paper's response, explicitly constraining the weight matrix to Toeplitz form afterward, is a useful example of using an emergent, task-induced regularity discovered empirically to justify a stronger architectural prior later, rather than imposing that structure a priori without first checking whether the task actually calls for it.

## Verification

The paper substantiates each of its major claims with a specific, targeted experimental design rather than only aggregate benchmark numbers. The SGU design choice itself is checked via a controlled ablation (Table 3) isolating exactly which part of the gating formulation (linear vs. additive vs. multiplicative vs. multiplicative-with-channel-split) drives the improvement, rather than presenting the final design without justifying its components. The paper's central mechanistic claim, that self-attention's specific contribution in NLP finetuning is cross-sentence alignment, is checked two ways: indirectly through the SST-2-versus-MNLI performance split across a controlled scaling sweep, and directly through visualizing what the tiny attention module in aMLP actually attends to on real MNLI examples (Appendix D), which is a genuine mechanistic verification of the paper's own causal story rather than an assumption. Variance is reported for the core ablation and scaling experiments (image classification runs: SD ~0.1; MLM ablation runs: SD ~0.01), giving a basis for judging whether reported differences (e.g., 4.35 vs. 4.26 perplexity, explicitly called "insignificant" by the authors relative to the effect of scaling) are likely to be noise. The full BERT-setup finetuning results in Table 6, the paper's most-cited headline numbers (gMLPlarge's 89.5% SQuAD F1, aMLPlarge beating BERTlarge across the board), are reported only as the median of five runs with no accompanying spread or variance statistic, so their exact margins cannot be independently checked for robustness the way the smaller-scale ablation numbers can. The authors are also explicit and transparent where a comparison is not apples-to-apples: the gMLPxlarge-vs-BERTlarge SQuAD-v2.0 comparison is directly flagged in the text as unfair due to differing model sizes, rather than presented without qualification.

## Field context

This paper sits in the same brief, dense 2021 research moment as MLP-Mixer, ResMLP, and RepMLP (all cited directly, with MLP-Mixer and ResMLP included as head-to-head baselines in Table 2), collectively asking whether self-attention specifically, as opposed to the Transformer's general non-recurrent, feedforward-block structure, is necessary for the era's vision and NLP results. Within this corpus, its relationship to [2105.01601] (MLP-Mixer) is unusually direct and worth noting explicitly: this paper does not just share a research question with MLP-Mixer, it directly benchmarks against it in both the vision experiments (Table 2, reporting an accuracy-per-parameter advantage) and, notably, adapts MLP-Mixer itself as one of the ablation baselines in the BERT masked-language-modeling experiment (Table 3), where MLP-Mixer's plain spatial-MLP mixing (5.34 perplexity) is shown to lag substantially behind gMLP's gated version (4.35 perplexity) at matched scale, a direct, paper-to-paper comparison of two architectures now both reviewed in this corpus. It also connects to [1911.03864] (Sandwich Transformer) as part of the same broader 2019-2021 current of papers testing which specific pieces of the canonical transformer stack (sublayer order, self-attention itself, or, in this paper's more targeted framing, self-attention's specific finetuning role in cross-sentence tasks) are actually load-bearing versus conventional. The paper's own framing distinguishes its contribution from MLP-Mixer's: where MLP-Mixer's spatial mixing is a plain linear projection, this paper's ablation-driven Spatial Gating Unit is presented as the specific ingredient that narrows the remaining gap to full self-attention, a claim it substantiates with the direct MLP-Mixer comparison rather than only asserting it.

## Critical discussion

The paper's core claims are unusually well-triangulated for an architecture paper: the SGU design is justified by an internal ablation rather than presented as a final answer, the central "self-attention's specific NLP role is cross-sentence alignment" claim is supported by both an indirect scaling-curve signature (task-specific SST-2/MNLI divergence) and a direct visualization of what the proposed fix (tiny attention) actually learns to attend to, and the paper is transparent about the one place its evidence is not apples-to-apples (the differently-sized gMLPxlarge-vs-BERTlarge comparison), rather than concealing the caveat.

The clearest gap is in the headline full-scale BERT results (Table 6): reporting only the median of five runs, with no spread, interquartile range, or standard deviation, for the paper's most quoted numbers (gMLPlarge's SQuAD F1, aMLPlarge's across-the-board wins over BERTlarge) means a reader cannot independently judge how much of, say, aMLPlarge's specific 4.4-point SQuAD-v2.0 F1 margin over BERTlarge reflects a robust architectural advantage versus run-to-run variance, in contrast to the smaller-scale ablation and scaling experiments earlier in the paper, which do report standard deviations. Given that the smaller-scale ablations show meaningful run-to-run noise (perplexity SD ~0.01, ImageNet accuracy SD ~0.1), a similar-magnitude spread at the full BERT scale would still leave most of the paper's reported margins intact, but this cannot be confirmed from the paper as written. A second, more minor point: the paper's interpretation that self-attention's specific value is "cross-sentence alignment" rests on a single task-pair contrast (SST-2 single-sentence vs. MNLI sentence-pair); while the tiny-attention visualization in Appendix D lends real, direct support to this specific interpretation, the paper does not test a broader range of single- versus multi-sentence tasks to establish how far this particular explanation generalizes beyond the two GLUE tasks and SQuAD examined here.

What remains well supported: self-attention is not necessary to match Transformer pretraining perplexity or single-sequence task accuracy in either the vision or NLP domains tested, a gated but otherwise static spatial-interaction mechanism can substitute for it at comparable or better parameter efficiency, the specific capability that a full self-attention module contributes beyond what gMLP already provides is concentrated in cross-sentence-pair tasks and is directly visualized as such, and that capability can be recovered with a disproportionately small amount of attention capacity rather than requiring the full mechanism back.
