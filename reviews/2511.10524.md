Citation: Eren, M.E., Perez, D.M. Rethinking Science in the Age of Artificial Intelligence. arXiv:2511.10524v1, 2025.

## What this paper is about

A short policy-oriented commentary from two Los Alamos National Laboratory researchers arguing that AI has moved from a computational tool to an active collaborator across the whole research lifecycle, literature triage, collaborator matching, forecasting, hypothesis generation, experimentation, and evaluation, and that this shift needs deliberate governance now, not later. The paper's own contribution is not new experiments but a workflow-centric synthesis of the current AI-for-science tooling landscape, paired with four concrete policy recommendations.

## Extended summary

Section 2 walks the research lifecycle stage by stage, citing a named tool or method at nearly every step: literature navigation (DiscipLink for interdisciplinary search with human-steerable "information scent" annotation; LitSearch/RAG pipelines with provenance-aware retrieval); collaborator and team formation (hypergraph-based "human-aware forecasting" that models author-concept pathways to surface well-staffed versus "alien" cross-field gaps; KG-plus-LLM ideation tools that pair researchers by publication history); forecasting and hypothesis generation (evolving knowledge graphs with link-prediction for future high-impact concept pairs; SCIMON's retrieval-grounded, contrastive-revision hypothesis generation; ResearchAgent's citation-graph traversal with reviewer-style critique agents; TOMATO/MOOSE's retriever-proposer-checker loop; DSPy's argument for replacing manual prompting with optimizer-tuned declarative programs); agentic experimentation (role-structured multi-agent frameworks like MetaGPT and CAMEL; lab-facing systems including Coscientist for autonomous chemistry planning and instrument control, ORGANA for human-in-the-loop wet-lab procedures, LLaMP for materials property prediction, and ChemCrow for chemistry-specific tool use); and evaluation (citing evidence that end-to-end ML-engineering benchmarks expose contamination and brittle generalization, that LLM-as-judge scoring shows measurable inconsistency and bias, and naming specific process-level checks: CiteME for citation faithfulness, SCoR for response consistency, and complexity-controlled stress tests for reasoning collapse). A closing subsection maps psychological research on human scientific judgment (overconfidence, premature closure, availability bias, calibrated optimism, from Chadwick and Segall 2010) onto design features of the surveyed AI systems, arguing that critique-and-revise loops function as "commitment brakes" against premature closure and that provenance-heavy retrieval counters availability bias.

Section 3 turns this synthesis into four policy recommendations: (1) fund open, interpretable, mixed-initiative tooling and interdisciplinary fellowships, with funding conditioned on shared benchmarks, standardized reporting of model versions/prompts/datasets/uncertainty/agent logs, and contamination/generalization audits; (2) require domain-specific, IRB-like third-party oversight bodies for autonomous experimentation, evaluating fail-safes, data provenance, dual-use risk, and reasoning calibration, with deployment conditioned on human override, red-teaming, and staged rollout; (3) require disclosure standards, preregistered AI-involvement statements naming models/versions/prompts/decision points, a mandatory "AI Contributions" section in manuscripts (with AI never listed as an author), and disclosure of AI assistance in peer review with a prohibition on delegating final review decisions to AI; and (4) integrate human-AI literacy into research training and incentivize collaborative practices that reward human-in-the-loop checkpoints, critic loops, and transparent reporting of exploration settings.

The paper explicitly positions itself against three named prior surveys, Wang et al. 2023b (a broad cross-domain AI4Science methods survey), Krenn et al. 2022 (which frames AI's epistemic role as computational microscope, inspiration source, or potential agent of understanding), and Gridach et al. 2025 (a survey of agentic scientific-discovery architectures by autonomy level), describing its own contribution as a complementary workflow-centric lens feeding directly into policy design, and states that its specific recommendations have not, to the authors' knowledge, been addressed by six named prior AI-in-science policy papers.

## Learnings

The mapping from documented human cognitive biases in scientific judgment (premature closure, availability bias, poor calibration) onto specific design features of AI research tools (critique-and-revise loops as "commitment brakes," provenance-heavy retrieval as an availability-bias counter) is a compact, reusable framing: it gives a non-arbitrary rationale for why certain agent-design choices (mandatory critic passes, explicit provenance) matter beyond generic "more rigor is better" advice, by tying them to a specific, named failure mode in human reasoning that the design choice is meant to counter.

The paper's citation of LLM-as-judge inconsistency and bias as a reason to require "human grounded protocols and blinded, rubric-driven reviews" is a useful, concrete instance of an idea we already hold (same-family self-critique or single-LLM judging is not an independent check); the twist worth remembering is the specific proposed remedy of blinded, rubric-driven human review as a complement rather than a replacement, mirroring how CASP-style blind evaluation is used elsewhere.

## Verification

The paper does not build or test a verifier; it surveys existing verification-adjacent tools and proposes verification-adjacent governance. On the tooling side, it names citation-faithfulness checking (CiteME) and response-consistency scoring (SCoR) as concrete process-level checks, and cites community findings that LLM-as-judge preference scoring is measurably inconsistent and biased, arguing that automated assessors should be paired with human-grounded, blinded, rubric-driven review rather than trusted alone. It also lists the process metrics it thinks realistic evaluation should capture beyond task accuracy: provenance adherence, claim-level citation correctness, tool-call success rate, agent handoff quality, confidence calibration, and cost/time to solution under realistic constraints (API failures, shifting corpora, latency/compute budgets). On the governance side, its policy proposals are themselves a verification mechanism at the institutional level: mandatory disclosure of AI involvement with agent traces and uncertainty tags, IRB-like third-party oversight of autonomous experimentation (fail-safes, provenance, dual-use risk, calibration), and an explicit prohibition on AI systems making final peer-review decisions. None of this is newly demonstrated by the paper; it is assembled from cited prior work and stated as recommended practice.

## Field context

The authors explicitly place this commentary alongside three other surveys rather than presenting it as filling an empty niche: a broad cross-domain AI4Science methods survey (Wang et al. 2023b), an epistemics-focused framing of AI's role in scientific understanding (Krenn et al. 2022), and an autonomy-organized survey of agentic scientific-discovery architectures (Gridach et al. 2025), positioning its own distinct contribution as a workflow-stage-by-stage lens that feeds directly into concrete policy recommendations, a framing the authors state (not merely reviewer inference). Read alongside other papers already in this corpus, this commentary's central normative claim, that AI should augment but not replace human judgment in peer review, ethical evaluation, and validation, converges with two independently-motivated arguments already reviewed here: the Google/DeepMind/MIT FutureTech survey (`slug:26aisc`) reports that scientists themselves spend a substantial share of any AI time-savings on verifying AI output rather than treating it as a finished answer, and the Hugging Face commentary (`arxiv:2509.06580`) argues on social-infrastructure grounds that current systems can optimize within a well-posed problem but cannot themselves decide which questions are worth asking. Three papers with different methods (survey data, bibliometric-adjacent social critique, and a policy commentary grounded in a systems-tooling literature review) arrive at compatible versions of the same underlying claim: current AI-for-science capability outruns the field's ability (or willingness) to keep humans meaningfully in charge of judgment and validation, and that gap, not raw model capability, is this literature's actual center of gravity right now.

## Critical discussion

This is a synthesis commentary, not an empirical study, so the usual critical-discussion questions about baselines, controls, or evaluation design mostly do not apply in their standard form; the more relevant question is how much independent scrutiny the synthesis itself applies to the claims it cites. The paper's evidentiary base is close to wall-to-wall citation of primary system papers (SCIMON, ResearchAgent, TOMATO/MOOSE, MetaGPT, CAMEL, Coscientist, ORGANA, LLaMP, ChemCrow, DiscipLink, and others), most of which are described in terms of what their own authors claim the system does, without the commentary independently distinguishing which of these have been replicated, stress-tested, or contested elsewhere in the literature, versus which rest on a single lab's own reported demonstration. The paper does incorporate some genuinely critical sources (Shojaee et al. 2025 on reasoning brittleness under rising complexity; Chiang et al. 2024b and Zheng et al. 2023 on LLM-judge inconsistency and bias), which shows the authors are not simply cataloguing positive results, but the critical lens is applied unevenly: it is used to justify the paper's own policy conclusions (oversight, disclosure) rather than to qualify the reliability of the many individual tool claims marshaled in Section 2 to establish that AI is "already" transforming each stage of the research lifecycle.

The claim of policy novelty, that these specific recommendations have not been addressed by prior global policy memos or position papers, is checked against six named comparison papers rather than a systematic review of the AI-science-policy literature, so it should be read as "not covered by this specific comparison set" rather than an audited claim of first-in-field novelty; the hedge ("to our knowledge") is appropriately cautious given that scope.

What remains supported: the workflow-stage organization of the current AI-for-science tooling landscape is a genuinely useful map, and the paper's normative core, that current systems should augment rather than replace human judgment in peer review, ethical evaluation, and result validation, is well-motivated by the brittleness and judge-bias findings it does cite carefully, even though the broader claim that AI is "already" an active collaborator across every named stage rests more on the accumulated weight of many individually unverified system demonstrations than on any evidence this paper itself generated or independently checked.

## Relevance to us

This is a third, independently-motivated arrival (alongside the Google/DeepMind survey and the Hugging Face social-infrastructure critique already in this corpus) at the same underlying claim we build Theo around: the interesting bottleneck right now is not raw model capability but keeping a human meaningfully in charge of judgment, verification, and problem selection. A policy commentary from a national lab reaching that conclusion via a completely different route (tooling survey plus institutional governance) than a data-driven adoption survey or a social-science-of-science critique is worth registering as convergent, low-cost evidence that this framing is not idiosyncratic to how we read our own interviews.

The specific process-level evaluation metrics the paper lists as what "realistic evaluation should capture beyond task accuracy" (provenance adherence, claim-level citation correctness, tool-call success rate, agent handoff quality, confidence calibration, cost-to-solution under realistic constraints) is a concrete, reusable checklist for scoping what a graded verification and trace-monitoring stack (B12, and our trace/watchdog verification line) should actually report, distinct from any single pass/fail accuracy number; several of these items (citation faithfulness, calibration of confidence, tool-call success) map directly onto checks we already want and are worth cross-referencing when specifying what Theo's own agent traces should log.

The paper's proposed disclosure norm, a mandatory "AI Contributions" section naming specific tasks (ideation, retrieval, drafting, analysis) and the responsible human supervisor, with AI never listed as an author, is a concrete, adoptable convention that intersects with our own collaborator arc (verified edits and re-runs as signal, intent as an inspectable object): it suggests a lightweight, externally-legible format for exactly the kind of human-AI task attribution our own product would need to support if it is used in work that gets published.
