Citation: Dennis, S., Patil, R., Shabahang, K., Guo, H. Compiling Agentic Workflows into LLM Weights: Near-Frontier Quality at Two Orders of Magnitude Less Cost. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22502v1

## What this paper is about

Most production LLM agents are built with an external orchestration framework (LangGraph, CrewAI, and similar tools with a combined 290,000+ GitHub stars) that sits between the user and the model, injecting instructions and routing decisions every turn. Prior work by the same group showed that for procedural tasks, simply giving a frontier model the whole procedure in its system prompt beats orchestration outright, but that approach requires a frontier model on every call and leaks the proprietary procedure into every API request. This paper asks: instead of prompting a model with a procedure every turn, why not fine-tune the procedure directly into a small model's weights so it "just knows" how to do the task, with no orchestrator and no procedural text in the prompt at all? The authors identify quality, cost, and flexibility as the three commonly assumed barriers to this "compilation" approach and test each empirically across three synthetic customer-service domains of increasing complexity.

## Extended summary

The method ("subterranean agent"): represent a procedure as a directed graph of dialogue nodes and conditioned edges, use a frontier model (Claude Sonnet 4.5) to generate synthetic conversations by traversing valid paths through the graph with varied scenario details, then fully fine-tune (not LoRA — the authors cite a companion paper, Dennis et al. 2026b, finding LoRA fails to internalize multi-step procedures) a small open-weight model on these conversations. At inference the compiled model receives only a minimal generic system prompt and no procedural scaffolding; the orchestrator is used only offline, during data generation.

Three domains test increasing complexity: travel booking (14 nodes, 3 hubs, 86 paths), Zoom technical support (14 nodes, product-specific domain knowledge), and insurance claims (55 nodes, 6 hubs, 2,381 paths, nested loops and cross-phase dependencies). All evaluations use n=200 scenarios per condition, a dynamic Claude-Sonnet-4.5-driven user simulator blind to the underlying flowchart, and an LLM-as-judge rubric (five 1-5 criteria: task success, information accuracy, consistency, graceful handling, naturalness) scored primarily by Claude Sonnet 4.5, with a full independent replication using GPT-4.1 as judge (Appendix C) to check for judge self-preference bias. Statistics use Wilcoxon signed-rank (paired, same-run) or Mann-Whitney U (unpaired, cross-run) tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction across the five criteria, Cohen's d, and bootstrap 95% CIs.

Quality: on travel booking, a controlled same-base-model comparison (3B compiled vs. the identical 3B model given explicit orchestration) shows the compiled model wins on four of five criteria (task success, consistency, graceful handling, naturalness; all p<.001), isolating the effect of compilation itself from model capacity. Against the LangGraph orchestrator (~70x more parameters, Claude Sonnet 4.5 backbone), the 3B compiled model reaches only 82% of in-context quality on graceful handling and naturalness; scaling to 8B (Zoom, insurance) closes this to 87-98% of in-context quality and matches or beats the LangGraph orchestrator on several criteria, with the 8B model beating the orchestrator specifically on naturalness (Zoom, insurance) and graceful handling (insurance), while the orchestrator retains an edge on information accuracy in most domains. Compiled-model failure rates (task success <=3) are lower than the orchestrator's in travel (5.5% vs. 24.0%) and insurance (9.0% vs. 17.0%), comparable in Zoom; the orchestrator's high travel failure rate is attributed to routing errors at decision hubs, a failure mode the compiled model eliminates by construction (no explicit routing step exists at inference). Cost: combining a ~65x per-token reduction (self-hosted Qwen3-8B on a $2.50/hr A100 via vLLM, using published third-party inference-throughput benchmarks rather than the authors' own measured serving numbers) with a 2-7x reduction in prompt token volume (the compiled model's prompt is constant-size; the in-context baseline's system prompt grows with procedure complexity), the compiled model is 128x (travel) to 462x (insurance) cheaper per conversation than the in-context baseline, and 77-249x cheaper than the LangGraph orchestrator; one-time compilation cost ($50-80) breaks even within 500 conversations. Flexibility: a full recompile cycle (regenerate ~1,600 conversations, fine-tune, spot-check) takes 30-50 minutes on an 8xH200 cluster, or 3-4 hours on a single A100.

The GPT-4.1 judge replication (Appendix C) confirms the qualitative ranking (in-context > LangGraph ~ compiled > same-model-orchestrated) and a comparable overall quality-ratio range (83-99% vs. 82-102% under Claude), but the two judges diverge substantially on specific criteria: naturalness scores compress to near-parity across all conditions under GPT-4.1 (3.92-4.04) versus a wide spread under Claude Sonnet 4.5 (4.12-5.00), and the LangGraph orchestrator (also Claude-Sonnet-4.5-backed) beats the compiled model on more criteria under GPT-4.1 than under Claude. Related work review positions this against dialogue-pipeline-collapsing work (SimpleTOD, AutoTOD), frontier-trajectory distillation (FireAct, AgentTuning, Agent Lumos), and workflow-scale compilation (WorkflowLLM, SynTOD), arguing that none of these prior efforts quantified the inference-cost advantage, measured a recompile cycle, or isolated compilation's effect from model capacity via a same-base-model orchestrated control — the three specific contributions this paper claims. No funding is disclosed.

## Learnings

The controlled same-base-model design (3B compiled vs. 3B given the identical procedure via explicit orchestration) is a clean and reusable way to separate "does compilation itself help" from "is the compiled model just smaller/worse than the baseline" — a confound that is easy to introduce by comparing a compiled small model only against a much larger orchestrated frontier model, as most of the prior compilation literature the paper reviews (FireAct, WorkflowLLM, etc.) apparently did not do.

The paper's proposed mechanism for why a much smaller compiled model can match a ~70x larger orchestrated model is a transferable, testable claim about orchestration's structural costs rather than raw capacity: orchestration fragments reasoning by generating from local per-node context only, introduces routing failures as an architecturally distinct error mode, and constrains conversational style through template injection — and a compiled model avoids all three by construction, not by being smarter. The observed asymmetry between quality dimensions (compiled models close the gap fast on information accuracy and consistency, but lag longest on graceful handling and naturalness, closing only when parameter count increases from 3B to 8B) suggests procedure-following and stylistic/edge-case fluency draw on different capacities, with the former learnable from compilation of a moderate amount of synthetic data and the latter apparently requiring more raw model capacity even after compilation.

## Verification

The paper's core comparisons are reasonably well verified for an LLM-as-judge evaluation: pre-registered five-criterion rubric with explicit behavioral anchors at each score level, appropriate paired vs. unpaired significance tests matched to the actual study design, multiple-comparison correction (Holm-Bonferroni) across the five criteria, bootstrap confidence intervals, and — most notably — a full independent replication of every quality result with a second, different-vendor judge (GPT-4.1) specifically to probe self-preference bias, since Claude Sonnet 4.5 is simultaneously the training-data generator, the in-context and LangGraph baseline backbone, the user simulator, and the primary judge. This is good practice and the paper reports the replication's disagreements honestly rather than only citing the parts that confirm the headline claim. The cost analysis is verified less rigorously: the ~65x per-token self-hosting cost reduction is derived from third-party published inference-throughput benchmarks for generic 8B models on A100 hardware, not from the authors' own measured deployment of their specific fine-tuned models, and does not appear to account for GPU idle time, engineering/ops overhead, or multi-tenancy costs of running a self-hosted fleet versus a metered API — all real components of "cost" for a practitioner deciding between the two architectures.

## Field context

This paper is an explicit "part two" of a research program: it builds directly on the same group's prior finding that in-context prompting dominates orchestration for procedural tasks (Dennis et al. 2026a, cited throughout as establishing the quality ceiling this paper's compiled models are measured against) and a companion paper on why LoRA fails to internalize procedures (Dennis et al. 2026b, motivating the paper's use of full-parameter fine-tuning). It sits in an interesting structural contrast with [reviews/2512.04388.md](2512.04388.md) ("Learning to Orchestrate Agents in Natural Language with the Conductor"), reviewed earlier in this corpus: that paper trains a small model to become a better orchestrator, coordinating much larger frontier "worker" models via RL-generated natural-language plans, whereas this paper argues for eliminating the orchestrator role entirely by compiling the procedure into the weights of the model that executes the conversation. Both papers use a small trained model as the lever for cost/capability improvement in multi-step LLM workflows, but they represent essentially opposite architectural bets on where structure should live (a small orchestrator directing large workers, vs. a small compiled executor needing no orchestrator at all) — a genuine, well-posed tension for anyone deciding between these approaches rather than two papers making the same point. The paper's own related-work section places it within an established but adoption-lagging "compile agent behavior into weights" literature (SimpleTOD, FireAct, WorkflowLLM, SynTOD, Agent Lumos), which it argues has proven technical feasibility repeatedly without addressing the specific cost/flexibility/isolated-effect questions that (the authors argue) actually explain low developer adoption.

## Critical discussion

The core empirical design is a genuine strength: three domains at increasing structural complexity, a controlled same-model condition that isolates compilation from capacity, appropriate paired/unpaired statistics with multiple-comparison correction, and — unusually for this kind of paper — a full second-judge replication rather than a token robustness footnote. The finding that routing failures at decision hubs (not raw model capacity) explain much of the orchestrator's higher failure rate in travel is a specific, mechanistically grounded claim rather than a bare percentage.

The judge-replication data in Appendix C, however, undercuts part of the headline story more than the paper's own framing acknowledges: naturalness — one of the two criteria driving the "82% gap that motivated scaling to 8B" narrative in Section 4 — compresses from a 4.12-5.00 spread under the Claude judge to a 3.92-4.04 spread under GPT-4.1, and the LangGraph orchestrator (which shares the Claude Sonnet 4.5 backbone with the in-context baseline and the primary judge) outperforms the compiled model on more criteria under the non-Claude judge. This pattern is consistent with the primary judge favoring outputs from its own model family, exactly the effect the paper cites Panickssery et al. (2024) as motivating the robustness check for — but the paper's summary treats the two judges as agreeing on "the core value proposition" without directly addressing that its own most quality-gap-relevant numbers (the ones justifying model scaling and the size of the reported quality deficit) are the ones that shift most under judge choice. The cost analysis, while directionally credible and probably correct in its order of magnitude, rests on generic published throughput benchmarks rather than the authors' measured deployment cost, and omits self-hosting overhead (ops, monitoring, GPU utilization below the assumed batch-64 ideal) that would be a real part of the total-cost-of-ownership comparison the paper's central "two orders of magnitude cheaper" claim is meant to support for a practitioner's actual adoption decision. The evaluation is also, by construction, in-distribution relative to its own synthetic-scenario generator: the "graceful handling" scenarios and the user simulator are produced by the same Claude Sonnet 4.5 pipeline used to generate training data, so the study speaks to how compiled models handle the kind of edge cases this pipeline can imagine, not necessarily the full diversity of real user behavior a production deployment would face. What remains solidly supported: within this evaluation's own scenario distribution, compiling a procedure into an 8B model's weights via full fine-tuning produces conversational quality broadly competitive with a ~70x larger orchestrated frontier model at meaningfully lower serving cost, and the same-base-model comparison credibly demonstrates that compilation itself, not just model choice, drives part of that quality.
