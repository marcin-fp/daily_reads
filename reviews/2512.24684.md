Citation: Li, M., Wang, Z., Li, H., Liu, J. R-Debater: Retrieval-Augmented Debate Generation through Argumentative Memory. AAMAS 2026. arXiv:2512.24684.

## What this paper is about

Getting an LLM to hold up its side of a formal, multi-turn debate is harder than it sounds: the model needs to stay consistent with its assigned position, actually respond to what the opponent just argued rather than talking past them, and back up its claims with real evidence, all while sounding persuasive. This paper argues that what current LLM debating systems are missing is something rhetoric scholars call argumentative memory, the idea that skilled human debaters do not invent arguments from scratch each turn but recall and adapt prior cases, evidence, and rhetorical patterns to the situation at hand. The authors build R-Debater, a system that pairs a retrieval mechanism (pulling relevant prior debate material from a database of real transcripts) with a multi-agent pipeline that explicitly detects logical flaws in the opponent's argument, retrieves well-matched supporting evidence and reasoning patterns, drafts a response, and then checks and revises that response before it is finalized.

## Extended summary

R-Debater has three pipeline stages. First, Database Construction processes a large collection of real debate transcripts into a knowledge base: each individual utterance is embedded, and a Scheme Annotation Agent labels each utterance with which of seven established argumentation schemes from Walton et al.'s theory of argumentation (e.g., example-based, expert-opinion, causal, analogical, value-based reasoning) it exemplifies, while a Scoring Agent rates how well that scheme is executed (poor/general/good/excellent) using few-shot exemplars, producing an enriched database of (utterance, embedding, schemes, quality-score) records. Second, Debate Data Retrieval operates on an ongoing debate: a Logic & Summarization Agent, inspired by a "symbolic chain-of-thought" approach, converts the opponent's accumulated utterances into pseudo-first-order predicates, reconstructs them into a natural-language reasoning chain, and then explicitly flags logical flaws (contradictions, unsupported assumptions, circular reasoning) in that chain as natural-language "control signals," while a separate Keyword Extraction Agent pulls salient keywords from the debate so far, does a coarse keyword-based retrieval pass against the knowledge base, then re-ranks candidates by embedding similarity to the current debate history, keeping only retrieved exemplars whose average annotated scheme-quality score exceeds a "general" threshold. Third, Debate Generation drafts an initial utterance conditioned on the retrieved evidence, logical-flaw signals, and debate history; a Debate Summarization Agent then produces a structured summary of the debate so far; a Judgement Agent evaluates the drafted utterance specifically for stance faithfulness, argumentative relevance, and scheme compliance, returning a binary accept/reject signal plus textual feedback; and if rejected, a Text Modification Agent revises the utterance based on that feedback, with this generate-judge-revise loop repeating until the Judgement Agent accepts the output.

Evaluation uses the ORCHID dataset of Chinese-language formal competitive debate transcripts, with 1,000 debates from the most recent five years forming the retrieval corpus and a separate, non-overlapping set of 32 debates across seven topic domains (society, politics, economy, technology, morality, education, international relations) held out for evaluation. Three baselines are compared: direct zero-shot LLM prompting with no retrieval, a naive RAG baseline that simply concatenates retrieved material into the prompt without filtering or reasoning control, and Agent4Debate, an existing multi-agent debate framework with separate analysis, evidence-gathering, and rebuttal roles; all methods are instantiated on GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Claude-3.7-Sonnet under matched zero-shot, fixed-temperature settings. Two automatic evaluation frameworks are used: InspireScore, an existing metric scoring individual utterances along Subjective (persuasiveness, clarity), Logic (reasoning validity, stance consistency), and Fact (evidence correctness and relevance) dimensions, for single-turn next-utterance generation; and Debatrix, an existing metric scoring full multi-turn debate trajectories along Argument, Source, Language, and Overall dimensions, used in adversarial simulations where R-Debater and Agent4Debate are set against each other across multiple turns until both sides reach a conclusion.

On the single-turn task, R-Debater outperforms all three baselines across all InspireScore dimensions and all three base LLMs, with especially large margins on the Logic dimension, where R-Debater's scores approach the metric's ceiling (approximately 0.985-0.997 across the three base models, versus 0.877-0.971 for the plain LLM baseline and 0.893-0.940 for Naive RAG and Agent4Debate). On the multi-turn adversarial simulation, R-Debater outperforms Agent4Debate across every Debatrix dimension on both GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3, with particularly large gains in source credibility and overall score. A separate expert-alignment evaluation checks whether the framework's own internal automated judgments track real human expert judgment: the Scheme Annotation Agent's labels agree substantially with expert annotations (Jaccard index 0.737, Cohen's kappa 0.72-0.73, Krippendorff's alpha 0.723), and the Scoring Agent's quality ratings correlate moderately-to-substantially with expert scores (Pearson 0.636, Spearman 0.653, Kendall 0.588). An ablation study (using gpt-4o-mini as the base model) removes one component at a time: removing the Judgement/revision "Optimization Module" causes a moderate overall drop concentrated in the Subjective dimension; removing the Logic & Summarization Agent causes a larger drop concentrated in the Logic dimension; and removing the Argumentation Scheme mechanism entirely causes by far the largest degradation, bringing performance close to the plain LLM baseline, which the authors interpret as evidence that structured argumentation-scheme knowledge is the component doing the most overall work. A qualitative case study contrasts R-Debater's and Naive RAG's rebuttals to the same opponent statement on a workplace-culture debate topic, showing R-Debater explicitly identifying a false-dichotomy fallacy in the opponent's reasoning and retrieving a specific, topically-matched case (an employee-feedback-driven corporate culture change) to ground its rebuttal, versus Naive RAG producing a more generic, less directly engaged counter-argument. Finally, a human evaluation study recruits 20 members of a university debate association, who rate anonymized, randomized outputs from all four systems on the same InspireScore-style dimensions on a 1-10 Likert scale and separately indicate an overall preferred response among the four candidates; R-Debater is chosen as the preferred output in 76.3% of cases, compared to 15.8% for Agent4Debate, 7.9% for the plain LLM, and 0% for Naive RAG.

## Learnings

The paper's most transferable methodological idea is the explicit separation of two distinct retrieval-time functions that are often conflated in simpler RAG pipelines: retrieving material relevant to what the current speaker wants to say (the Keyword Extraction Agent's role) versus explicitly modeling and diagnosing the logical structure of what the opponent has already said (the Logic & Summarization Agent's symbolic predicate-extraction-and-flaw-detection pipeline); the ablation result showing these two components degrade different, specific evaluation dimensions (Subjective versus Logic respectively) when removed is a concrete demonstration that this functional separation captures something real rather than being redundant machinery.

A second, more specific and reusable learning is the demonstrated value of enriching a retrieval corpus with structured argumentation-theory metadata (Walton's seven schemes, plus a quality label for how well each scheme is executed in a given passage) rather than treating the corpus as an undifferentiated bag of prior text; the ablation finding that removing this scheme-annotation mechanism alone causes by far the largest performance collapse (down to near the plain-LLM baseline) is a specific, quantified argument for investing in this kind of structured domain-theory annotation over a corpus, rather than relying on embedding similarity alone to select useful retrieved material, at least for a genre (formal argumentation) where such theory-grounded structure exists and is well established.

A third, more general learning, applicable well beyond debate generation, is the case study's illustration of what a genuinely opponent-aware rebuttal looks like in contrast to a genuinely opponent-agnostic one: Naive RAG's rebuttal responds to the general topic but not to the specific logical structure of what the opponent actually said, while R-Debater's rebuttal explicitly names and targets a specific reasoning flaw (a false dichotomy) in the opponent's argument before countering it; this is a concrete, reusable diagnostic for evaluating any multi-turn dialogue or debate system's genuine responsiveness, beyond just checking topical relevance.

## Verification

The paper checks several of its more subjective or potentially self-serving claims against independent evidence rather than relying only on its own pipeline's internal signals. Most importantly, the human evaluation with 20 debate-association members provides a genuinely independent check on the automatic-metric results, and its qualitative conclusion (R-Debater strongly preferred) is directionally consistent with, though not identical in magnitude to, the automatic InspireScore and Debatrix results, which somewhat corroborates that the automatic metrics are not simply diverging from actual human judgment of debate quality. The expert-alignment evaluation is a real, quantified check on whether the framework's own internal automated judgments (the Scheme Annotation Agent and Scoring Agent used inside the retrieval pipeline) actually track expert human judgment, reported with multiple, standard inter-rater agreement statistics (Jaccard, Cohen's kappa, Krippendorff's alpha) and correlation coefficients (Pearson, Spearman, Kendall) rather than a single, potentially cherry-picked summary number. The ablation study isolates the contribution of three specific components individually rather than only comparing the full system against baselines, which helps identify which specific mechanism drives which specific measured improvement (Subjective, Logic, or overall) rather than leaving the source of the gain unattributed. All baselines are instantiated across the same three base LLMs under matched, stated hyperparameters (zero-shot, temperature 0.2), a reasonable control against the concern that R-Debater's advantage might be an artifact of using a stronger or differently-configured underlying model.

Verification is comparatively thinner, and the paper's central quantitative claims are comparatively more vulnerable, on one specific point: R-Debater's own generation pipeline includes a Judgement Agent that iteratively revises each candidate utterance specifically until it satisfies stance faithfulness, argumentative relevance, and scheme compliance, and the primary automatic evaluation metric used for the single-turn comparison (InspireScore's Logic dimension) is explicitly defined as measuring "reasoning validity and stance consistency," a highly overlapping, if not essentially identical, set of properties to what the internal Judgement Agent is explicitly optimizing for via repeated self-revision. None of the three baselines (plain LLM, Naive RAG, Agent4Debate as the paper describes it) are reported to include an equivalent explicit accept/reject/revise loop targeting these same specific criteria before their final output is scored, so R-Debater's near-ceiling Logic scores (0.985-0.997) are, to a meaningful degree, close to a foregone conclusion given how the system is built, rather than an independent discovery that the system happens to reason unusually well. The paper does not report what fraction of utterances required one or more revision rounds, how many revision rounds were typically needed, or the Logic-dimension score before this internal self-revision loop was applied, any of which would have helped separate "the system genuinely reasons better" from "the system iterates on the same axis the metric measures until it scores well on that axis."

## Field context

The paper positions itself against two related traditions it distinguishes directly: classical symbolic and rule-based computational argumentation systems (dating to formal argumentation theory and abstract argumentation frameworks), which it credits with rigor and transparency but criticizes as brittle and difficult to scale to open-domain debate; and more recent retrieval-based and neural argument generation pipelines (e.g., CANDELA's retrieval-planning-realization process, argumentation-knowledge-graph-augmented generation, hierarchical multi-turn dialogue frameworks), which it credits with improved fluency and evidence use but criticizes for treating each debate turn largely as an isolated reasoning task rather than building genuine cross-turn continuity. It also explicitly engages with, and builds beyond, the existing multi-agent debate framework Agent4Debate, arguing that role-specialized agentic coordination without explicit evidence grounding limits argumentative credibility, and beyond standard RAG-for-dialogue systems, arguing these are built for informational question-answering rather than the adversarial, stance-constrained recontextualization debate specifically requires. Within this corpus, R-Debater's architecture, a roster of narrowly-specialized agents (Scheme Annotation, Scoring, Logic & Summarization, Keyword Extraction, Debate Summarization, Judgement, Text Modification) each handling one piece of a larger generation pipeline, sits alongside two other multi-agent orchestration papers reviewed earlier in this same batch, [2512.04388] (the Conductor) and [2512.04695] (TRINITY), both of which also decompose a complex task across specialized LLM roles, though for a different application domain (general reasoning and coding benchmarks rather than debate) and via learned, RL- or evolution-trained role assignment rather than R-Debater's fixed, hand-designed pipeline structure; a natural point of comparison for future synthesis work is whether a learned, reward-driven orchestrator in the style of those two papers could discover a similarly effective division of labor for debate generation without the extensive manual pipeline engineering R-Debater relies on. R-Debater's internal Judgement Agent, which iteratively revises output to satisfy specific evaluation-relevant criteria before finalizing it, is also worth reading alongside [2505.14946] (RLUF, reviewed earlier in this batch), whose central finding is that optimizing a policy against a proxy for a desired property can produce behavior that games the proxy rather than genuinely achieving the underlying goal; R-Debater's self-revision loop is not reward-trained in that paper's sense, but it shares the same underlying structural risk, that a system explicitly engineered to satisfy the same criteria its own evaluation measures will tend to score well on that specific measurement regardless of whether the improvement reflects genuinely better argumentation.

## Critical discussion

The paper's strongest evidentiary components are the human evaluation and the expert-alignment study, both of which provide real, independent checks rather than relying solely on the paper's own internal or closely-related automatic metrics: 20 experienced human debaters preferring R-Debater's output in over three-quarters of trials is a meaningful, hard-to-dismiss result, and the multi-statistic agreement analysis between the framework's internal annotation/scoring agents and expert human annotators is a genuine, appropriately-detailed reliability check rather than an assumed alignment.

The clearest limitation concerns the single-turn InspireScore comparison that anchors much of the paper's headline quantitative claims, particularly the near-ceiling Logic scores: R-Debater's own generation pipeline includes an explicit Judgement Agent that repeatedly revises each candidate utterance specifically until it satisfies stance faithfulness and argumentative relevance, criteria that substantially overlap with what the Logic dimension of InspireScore is defined to measure, and no equivalent explicit accept/reject/revise loop targeting these same criteria is described for the compared baselines. This does not mean R-Debater's advantage is illusory, since the underlying retrieval and logical-flaw-detection machinery genuinely differs from the baselines in ways independent of the revision loop itself (as the ablation study shows), but it does mean the specific, most dramatic reported numbers (Logic scores approaching 0.99) should be read with the knowledge that part of what is being measured is whether a system that was explicitly built to keep revising until it passes a check for "stance faithfulness" and "argumentative relevance" does, in fact, eventually pass a metric checking for "stance consistency" and "reasoning validity," which is a meaningfully circular relationship between the mechanism and the measurement that the paper does not explicitly discuss or attempt to disentangle (for instance, by reporting Logic scores before versus after the revision loop, or by giving baselines an equivalent revision budget). A second, more moderate and self-disclosed limitation is the narrowness of the evaluation domain: all quantitative results come from a single dataset (ORCHID), a single language (Chinese), and a single genre (formal competitive university debate), with the authors' own Limitations section explicitly flagging cross-lingual and cross-domain generalization, including informal or non-competitive debate settings, as untested and left to future work.

What remains well supported after these caveats: R-Debater's structured, theory-grounded retrieval mechanism (argumentation-scheme annotation plus explicit opponent-logic-flaw detection) provides real, ablation-verified value distinct from either naive retrieval or role-based agentic coordination without evidence grounding, since removing these specific components causes large, dimension-specific performance drops that a purely revision-loop-driven explanation would not predict; and the independent human-evaluation result, which is not subject to the same circularity concern as the automatic Logic metric, provides genuine, credible evidence that human debate experts do find R-Debater's outputs meaningfully more persuasive and well-reasoned than the tested alternatives, even if the precise magnitude of the automatic Logic-dimension gap should be interpreted cautiously given how the system's own generation loop is constructed.
