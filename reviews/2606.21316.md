Citation: Saad, S. Large Language Model-Assisted Framework for BSM Model Building. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21316v1

## What this paper is about

Building a theoretical extension of the Standard Model of particle physics (adding new particles to explain neutrino masses, dark matter, or matter-antimatter asymmetry) normally requires a physicist to manually work out every allowed interaction term consistent with gauge symmetry, check that the new particles don't spoil gauge-anomaly cancellation, and derive the resulting particle masses — a process that gets rapidly more laborious and error-prone as more or larger new fields are added. This paper introduces `bsm_agent`, an open-source tool where a user just describes the quantum numbers of new particles in plain English; a chat-based LLM layer translates that description into a structured specification (asking for confirmation if anything is ambiguous), while a completely separate, deterministic Python/SymPy backend does all the actual physics: generating the full renormalizable Lagrangian, checking gauge-anomaly cancellation, expanding everything into explicit particle-level terms, and deriving tree-level mass matrices after electroweak symmetry breaking. The central design choice is that the LLM never touches the physics itself — it only manages the conversation and calls verified backend functions.

## Extended summary

The package is organized into six layers (Fig. 1): a field/group layer defining Standard Model quantum numbers under SU(3)_c x SU(2)_L x U(1)_Y; a model-construction layer that enumerates the complete renormalizable, gauge-invariant operator basis (scalar potential up to quartic order, fermion-mass bilinears, and Yukawa terms combining two fermions with one scalar), automatically assigning symbolic coupling names and indexing multiple independent gauge contractions when they exist, with explicit Hermitian-conjugate deduplication to keep the result deterministic; an expansion layer that maps each gauge-invariant operator to explicit component-field terms via genuine SU(2) Clebsch-Gordan coefficients and explicit SU(3) invariant tensors (a representation-theoretic decomposition, not a heuristic rewriting); an EWSB/mass-matrix layer that shifts every color- and charge-neutral scalar component by a real VEV, derives one stationary condition per independent VEV, and extracts tree-level mass matrices from the surviving bilinears; a report layer producing compact and fully-expanded LaTeX/PDF output; and an agent layer that connects all of this to a conversational interface. Supported gauge representations span arbitrary U(1)_Y hypercharge, SU(2)_L multiplets of dimension 1-7 (motivated by minimal-dark-matter fermionic-quintuplet/scalar-septuplet scenarios, though scalar multiplets above dimension 3 are currently limited to a single field due to computational cost), and SU(3)_c representations 1, 3, 3-bar, 6, 6-bar, 8 (motivated by SO(10) grand-unified constructions, restricted to SU(2) dimension <=3 when combined with a nontrivial color representation).

Runtime scales sharply with representation size because the operator generator must test a combinatorially growing set of candidate field products for gauge-singlet contractions, with the scalar sector being the dominant cost driver since scalars (unlike fermions) admit cubic and quartic self-interactions; several representation-aware optimizations (Bose-symmetrized bases for repeated large real multiplets, closed-form invariant constructions for triplet/quintuplet/septuplet self-interactions, and adjoint-basis construction with contraction pruning for color octets) mitigate but do not eliminate this, and the paper's own runtime benchmarks (Table 1) show most tested field contents constructing and expanding within a few seconds while color-octet scalar models take 48-110 seconds for construction alone. Anomaly checking sums the standard Dynkin-index and hypercharge-weighted contributions over all left-handed Weyl fermions to compute the five canonical anomaly coefficients, reporting whether the model is anomaly-free and optionally suggesting conjugate-representation additions as one possible (not general or complete) remediation strategy.

The LLM-assisted interaction layer restricts the model to five specific orchestration duties: interpreting natural-language field descriptions, translating them into the backend's structured schema, asking clarifying or explicit confirmation questions when input is ambiguous (e.g., whether a field is scalar or fermionic, real or complex, or the sign of a hypercharge — details that can silently change the resulting theory if inferred incorrectly), selecting which backend action to invoke, and formatting deterministic tool output into readable text; the paper explicitly states what the LLM does not do (derive gauge singlets, compute anomaly coefficients, expand operators, or calculate mass matrices). A conversation-state mechanism lets follow-up requests (report generation, Lagrangian expansion, mass-matrix derivation) operate on the same constructed model, or lets the user explicitly reset to a fresh state. Three provider classes are supported with a shared orchestration interface: local Ollama inference, remote self-hosted model servers via a generic remote-provider interface, and commercial hosted APIs (OpenAI, Anthropic), letting users switch backends without changing the model-construction workflow.

Worked examples reproduce the Standard Model's own Yukawa sector, canonical Higgs-potential normalization, tadpole (stationary) condition, and the expected vanishing of Goldstone-boson masses once that stationary condition is imposed — recovering textbook results. A dedicated leptoquark case study (Section VIII) constructs a specific multi-field BSM extension end-to-end (Lagrangian generation, PDF report, full sector-by-sector expansion, mass matrices) and explicitly cross-checks the resulting Yukawa Lagrangian against a cited published leptoquark-model reference, finding agreement up to an identified and explained unphysical field-rephasing convention difference (rather than treating the mismatch as an unexplained discrepancy). Software requirements are modest (Python >=3.10, SymPy, LangChain, and either a local Ollama installation or API credentials; PDF output additionally requires a local `pdflatex`), and installation/usage is demonstrated on Ubuntu Linux, with the author explicitly noting the documented macOS/Windows instructions were not personally verified. The author's stated future directions include support for higher-dimensional representations, additional gauge groups (Pati-Salam, SU(5), SO(10)), discrete and continuous global symmetries, higher-dimensional operators, supersymmetric extensions, and more automated phenomenological analysis.

## Learnings

The core architectural principle — strictly confining the LLM to natural-language interpretation, schema translation, and orchestration, while delegating every domain-critical computation (Lagrangian construction, anomaly coefficients, mass matrices) to a separately verified deterministic backend — is a specific, transferable design pattern for any LLM-assisted scientific-software project where hallucination risk in free-form technical generation is unacceptable: confine the LLM to the interface layer and let established, checkable code own all correctness-critical output, rather than asking the LLM to produce or validate the technical content itself.

The explicit confirmation-workflow design (restating an inferred structured interpretation of an ambiguous natural-language request and requiring the user's explicit confirmation before committing to model construction) is a specific, reusable reliability pattern for any natural-language-to-structured-command interface where a silently wrong inference can produce an entirely different, incorrect downstream result — directly targeting the exact class of input ambiguity (is this field a scalar or a fermion, real or complex, what is the sign of its hypercharge) that this domain's inputs are most prone to.

The representation-aware optimization strategy for the underlying symbolic tensor computations (special-cased Bose-symmetrized bases, closed-form invariant constructions for specific recurring self-interaction structures, and adjoint-basis construction with contraction pruning for color octets) is a transferable engineering lesson for any symbolic or tensor-algebra system facing combinatorial blowup with representation size: hand-optimizing the commonly recurring high-symmetry cases can substantially outperform a fully generic algorithm, even though the paper's own benchmarks show this does not fully tame worst-case scaling for the largest tested representations.

## Verification

The paper provides genuine, specific correctness checks for its deterministic symbolic backend: the Standard Model output reproduces canonical textbook results (Higgs potential normalization, the tadpole stationary condition, vanishing Goldstone-boson masses once that condition is imposed, and standard fermion mass-matrix structure), and a dedicated leptoquark case study cross-checks the tool's generated Yukawa Lagrangian directly against a cited published reference paper, finding exact agreement once an explicitly-identified and explained unphysical field-rephasing convention is accounted for — a real, specific, and correctly-diagnosed validation rather than an unexamined claim of correctness. This is meaningful evidence given that the backend's calculations rest on checkable, deterministic group-theory and symbolic-algebra code (SymPy-based, using explicit Clebsch-Gordan coefficients and invariant tensors) rather than on an LLM's free-form output.

What is entirely absent is any evaluation of the LLM orchestration layer's own reliability, which is the paper's stated central novelty ("the key novelty... is that all of these tasks are performed automatically once the user specifies the quantum numbers... through a natural-language interface"). No experiment in the paper measures how accurately or consistently the LLM interprets a range of natural-language field descriptions into the correct structured schema, how this varies across the three supported provider classes or across model sizes (local `gpt-oss:20b` versus a commercial `GPT-4.1` versus other Ollama models), or what happens under genuinely ambiguous, adversarial, or malformed input beyond the single clean-request-plus-confirmation pattern shown in every worked example throughout the paper. The confirmation-workflow design does partially mitigate the consequences of a misinterpretation (a user can catch and correct an error before it is committed), but the paper offers no quantitative or even systematic qualitative evidence about how often that confirmation step is actually needed, how often the LLM's initial interpretation is correct, or whether interpretation quality degrades for more complex multi-field specifications than the examples shown.

## Field context

The paper positions `bsm_agent` as complementary to, rather than competing with, established symbolic BSM tools such as SARAH, FeynRules, and Susyno: those tools take an already-specified model definition and derive downstream phenomenological quantities (vertices, spectra, observables), whereas `bsm_agent` targets an earlier "pre-implementation" stage — going from a bare specification of new field quantum numbers directly to a complete, reproducible renormalizable Lagrangian, anomaly check, and mass-matrix analysis, without requiring the user to manually enumerate the operator content. It situates this within the broader, rapidly growing use of LLMs and agentic AI across scientific disciplines and specifically within high-energy physics, where it cites prior agentic-AI work supporting simulation workflows, event generation, and reproduction of published analyses. Within this corpus, `bsm_agent`'s design sits at the conservative end of a spectrum that includes considerably more autonomous "AI scientist" systems (broad multi-agent research pipelines that use LLMs to generate scientific content, plans, and even full draft papers end-to-end) reviewed elsewhere in this corpus; `bsm_agent` deliberately takes the opposite approach for its specific technical domain, restricting the LLM to a thin interface layer over a fully deterministic computational core rather than letting the LLM participate in generating or judging the scientific content itself — a meaningful design-space contrast given how prominently the more autonomous paradigm otherwise features in AI-for-science work.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central architectural claim — that a deterministic backend plus a strictly interface-only LLM layer can deliver reliable, reproducible BSM model construction via natural language — is well-supported for the deterministic-backend half of that claim: the symbolic computations are checkable, deterministic, and specifically validated in two concrete instances (the Standard Model and a leptoquark extension) against known and published results respectively, with a genuine, correctly-explained match rather than an unexamined or overstated one.

The main gap is that the paper offers no evidence at all for the reliability of the natural-language interpretation step specifically, despite this being the feature the title and abstract foreground as the work's key novelty. Every demonstrated chat interaction in the paper is a single, well-formed natural-language request that the LLM parses correctly on the first attempt (modulo the built-in confirmation step, which is never shown catching or correcting an actual misinterpretation in any example). There is no systematic test of ambiguous, underspecified, or complex multi-field descriptions; no comparison of interpretation accuracy across the three supported provider classes or across different model sizes and families; and no discussion of what happens when the LLM's interpretation is wrong and the user does not catch it at confirmation time. Given that the entire risk surface for factual errors in this design is concentrated at exactly this interpretation step (since the backend itself is deterministic and separately validated), the absence of any quantitative or systematic qualitative assessment of that step is a real gap relative to the paper's own framing of it as the central contribution. Separately, correctness validation of the backend's physics output, while genuine, is limited to two specific worked cases (the Standard Model and one leptoquark model) rather than a broader validation suite spanning the range of gauge representations and field contents the runtime benchmarks (Table 1) show the tool supports.

What remains solidly supported after these caveats: the architectural separation between LLM orchestration and deterministic physics computation is a sound, explicitly-enforced design choice that removes the specific hallucination risk of an LLM inventing or miscalculating Lagrangian terms, anomaly coefficients, or mass matrices; and the deterministic backend's outputs are shown, in the two cases directly checked, to correctly reproduce known and published physics results. Whether the tool reliably translates a broad range of real user natural-language requests into the correct structured specification — the specific capability that most distinguishes this work from a purely programmatic symbolic tool — remains untested in this version of the paper.
