Citation: Friedman, D., Dieng, A. B. The Vendi Score: A Diversity Evaluation Metric for Machine Learning. Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 07/2023. arXiv:2210.02410.

## What this paper is about

Diversity matters across generative modeling, dataset curation, and decoding, but most existing diversity metrics are narrow: some need a reference dataset to compare against, some need a pretrained classifier and labels, some only work for discrete features like n-grams. This paper borrows an idea from ecology, defining a population's diversity as the effective number of distinct species it contains, and adapts it to machine learning: given any user-chosen similarity function between samples, the Vendi Score is the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalues of the resulting similarity matrix, which the authors show is mathematically identical to the von Neumann entropy from quantum statistical mechanics. Because it needs nothing but the samples themselves and a similarity function, no reference distribution, no labels, no fixed feature representation, the same metric applies unchanged to molecules, images, and text, and the paper demonstrates cases where it catches real diversity failures that the field's existing go-to metrics miss entirely.

## Extended summary

The paper opens by cataloguing the specific shortcomings of existing diversity metrics across domains: reference-based metrics like Fréchet Inception Distance and Precision/Recall require an embedding function and a comparison distribution, so they cannot be used where no reference exists; classifier-based metrics like the Inception Score and Number of Modes (NoM) require labeled data and a trained classifier; discrete-feature metrics like n-gram diversity require an explicit feature vocabulary; and the field's most common flexible fallback, average pairwise similarity (called IntDiv in molecular generation, using Jaccard/Tanimoto fingerprint similarity), has two specific documented failures the paper illustrates with a toy shape-and-color dataset (Figure 1): it becomes progressively less sensitive as the number of distinct groups increases (saturating near a ceiling rather than continuing to grow), and it does not account for correlations between different dimensions of variation (combining two genuinely independent similarity functions, e.g., shape and color, does not increase average-similarity-based diversity the way it intuitively should).

The Vendi Score (Definition 3.1) is built from a user-specified positive semidefinite similarity function k, applied pairwise to n samples to build an n x n kernel matrix K (with k(x,x)=1 by convention); the eigenvalues of K/n are non-negative and sum to one (since the diagonal of K/n is 1/n), so they form a valid probability-like distribution whose Shannon entropy is well-defined, and the Vendi Score is the exponential of that entropy. The paper proves this is mathematically identical to the "effective rank" of K (a concept from signal processing) and to the von Neumann entropy of the normalized kernel matrix treated as a quantum density matrix, which motivates the name (von Neumann diversity). When embeddings phi(x) are available (the common case in ML), the Vendi Score can be computed exactly via the covariance matrix of the embeddings in O(d^2*n + d^3) time rather than the naive O(n^3) eigendecomposition of the full n x n matrix, matching the computational cost of existing embedding-based metrics like FID; when embeddings are not available, Nystrom-method column sampling can approximate it. Four proven properties (Theorem 3.1) formalize what makes it behave like a genuine diversity count: it equals n when all samples are pairwise dissimilar and 1 when all are identical (the "effective number" property); merging two identical elements into one with combined probability weight leaves the score unchanged; for a sample partitioned into groups with zero cross-group similarity, the total diversity factors into a geometric mean of each group's internal diversity weighted by group proportions (mirroring the additive partitioning property of Shannon entropy); and the score is invariant to how samples are ordered. The paper also situates the Vendi Score relative to determinantal point processes (DPPs), noting the DPP likelihood, which is also built from kernel-matrix eigenvalues, collapses to exactly zero whenever any duplicate exists in the sample, whereas the Vendi Score degrades gracefully and remains interpretable even with duplicates present, and relative to spectral clustering's graph Laplacian, where the Vendi Score generalizes the discrete "number of connected components" statistic into a continuous measure that also captures within-component diversity.

Section 4's experiments span synthetic data and three real domains. On synthetic univariate Gaussian mixtures, varying the number of components, mixture proportions, or per-component variance, the Vendi Score behaves as an effective number of modes throughout, while IntDiv is inconsistent across these three manipulations (notably insensitive to within-mode variance, with diminishing sensitivity to additional modes). On molecular generative models from the MOSES benchmark (using Morgan fingerprint similarity, the field's standard choice), the Vendi Score and IntDiv directly disagree on one model (an HMM-based generator): IntDiv ranks it among the most diverse, while the Vendi Score ranks it least diverse; a visual inspection of the similarity matrix (Figure 3, 250 molecules sorted lexicographically) confirms the HMM produces clusters of exact or near-exact duplicate molecules that the Vendi Score correctly penalizes and IntDiv, an average-similarity metric relatively insensitive to a small number of tight duplicate clusters embedded in an otherwise dissimilar sample, does not. On GAN mode collapse (StackedMNIST, 1000 ground-truth classes), two GANs (PresGAN and Self-conditioned GAN) both achieve a perfect Number-of-Modes score of 1000, making them indistinguishable by that metric, but the Vendi Score (746.7 and 866.6 respectively, versus 943.7 for the real dataset) reveals PresGAN is meaningfully more diverse than Self-conditioned GAN even though both capture every mode; a separately-computed "Mode Diversity" statistic (entropy of the predicted mode distribution alone) scores higher than the Vendi Score for both models, which the authors use as evidence the Vendi Score is capturing more than just mode-count imbalance. On unconditional image generation (CIFAR-10, ImageNet 64x64, two LSUN categories, comparing VAE, flow, diffusion, GAN, and autoregressive model families using cosine similarity between Inception embeddings), the Vendi Score generally agrees with existing metrics on lower-resolution datasets, but on LSUN it diverges specifically from Precision/Recall: a diffusion model (ADM) achieves the best Precision (0.63) and Recall (0.52) on LSUN Cat yet scores lower on the Vendi Score than a competing model, which the paper interprets as evidence that the remaining generated images, though individually realistic and reference-covering, may include many near-duplicates that Precision/Recall's coverage-based definition of diversity cannot detect. No evaluated image model matches the diversity score of its own training dataset. On text decoding (MS-COCO image captioning, comparing beam search against diverse beam search at increasing diversity-penalty settings, using an n-gram overlap kernel), the Vendi Score and standard n-gram diversity agree in ranking (human captions most diverse, plain beam search least, diverse beam search in between and increasing with the penalty parameter), with the Vendi Score offering a directly interpretable number (human captions score close to 5, meaning nearly all five human-written captions per image are almost completely mutually dissimilar). Finally, the paper uses the Vendi Score diagnostically on CIFAR-100 categories and other datasets, showing it distinguishes low-level pixel diversity (e.g., "cockroach" images share similar backgrounds, scoring low) from higher-level semantic diversity via Inception embeddings (e.g., "castle" scoring low because ImageNet's classifier treats it as one coherent category, versus "clock" or "keyboard" scoring higher because those categories are visually and semantically less homogeneous to the classifier).

The Limitations section (Section 5), written by the authors themselves, is direct: the Vendi Score is reference-free, so a set of pure random noise samples that are all mutually dissimilar will score maximally diverse regardless of any notion of quality, meaning it must always be paired with a separate quality metric (a limitation the paper notes is equally true of IntDiv and n-gram diversity, not unique to the Vendi Score); it is entirely dependent on the choice of similarity function, an overly sensitive kernel makes everything look diverse and an insensitive one makes everything look uniform, and a poorly chosen kernel can introduce systematic bias into the resulting scores; and computing it exactly can become expensive when no low-dimensional embedding is available for the chosen similarity function.

## Learnings

The paper's central, broadly transferable idea is decoupling the concept of "diversity" from any particular domain's feature representation by routing it entirely through a user-specified similarity function and an entropy computed on that similarity function's induced kernel matrix. This makes the same formal object simultaneously applicable to molecular fingerprints, image embeddings, and n-gram overlap without modification, a genuinely reusable pattern for building general-purpose evaluation tools: rather than hand-designing a metric's mechanics for each new domain, defining it entirely in terms of an abstract pairwise similarity function lets a single mathematical object migrate across domains as long as someone can define what "similar" means there.

A second, more specific and immediately useful learning is the demonstrated failure mode of average-pairwise-similarity metrics (IntDiv and its relatives): a small number of tight clusters of near-identical items embedded within an otherwise genuinely dissimilar sample can be almost invisible to a metric that only looks at the average similarity across all pairs, because the many dissimilar pairs dominate the average and dilute the signal from the few duplicate clusters. Any diversity-adjacent evaluation currently relying on an average-pairwise-distance or average-pairwise-similarity statistic should be checked specifically for this blind spot, duplicate or near-duplicate clustering, since the paper shows concretely (the MOSES HMM case, directly visualized) that this exact failure occurs in a real, actively-used generative-modeling benchmark.

A third, quieter learning is the value of validating a new evaluation metric against cases with known ground truth before trusting it on cases without one: the paper's mode-dropping experiments (Section 9.1, MNIST and MultiNLI subsets with an experimentally controlled, exactly known number of true modes) and the StackedMNIST comparison against Number-of-Modes (a metric with a known, verifiable interpretation) both function as calibration checks confirming the Vendi Score tracks a quantity whose true value the authors can independently verify, before the paper extends the metric to settings (real generative-model comparisons, dataset diagnosis) where no such ground truth exists and the metric's own internal consistency is the only available check.

## Verification

The paper's verification strategy for its own central claim, that the Vendi Score genuinely measures diversity rather than some other correlated quantity, rests on three complementary approaches. First, formal mathematical proof: four properties (effective number, identical-elements invariance, partitioning, symmetry) are stated as a theorem and proven in the appendix, giving the metric provable guarantees rather than only empirically observed good behavior. Second, calibration against known ground truth: synthetic mixture-of-Gaussians experiments with an experimentally controlled true number of components, and real-data mode-dropping experiments on MNIST and MultiNLI where the true number of represented classes is known by construction, let the authors directly check whether the Vendi Score tracks the actual, verifiable diversity of the underlying data rather than relying on the metric's self-consistency alone. Third, and most persuasively, targeted case-level investigation of disagreements with existing metrics: rather than simply reporting that the Vendi Score and IntDiv disagree on the MOSES HMM model, the paper visualizes the actual similarity matrix for 250 sampled molecules (Figure 3) and shows directly that the disagreement traces to a real, visible cluster of duplicate molecules, converting an abstract metric disagreement into a concrete, independently checkable fact about the underlying samples. This same standard of direct visual verification is not applied to the paper's other reported metric disagreement, the LSUN image case where a diffusion model scores highest on Precision/Recall but lower on the Vendi Score; there, the "many near-duplicates" explanation is offered as a plausible interpretation rather than confirmed the way the molecular case was, an inconsistency in how rigorously the paper's various disagreement claims are substantiated.

## Field context

This paper positions itself explicitly against a specific, well-cataloged landscape of prior diversity metrics: reference-based metrics from the GAN evaluation literature (Fréchet Inception Distance, Precision and Recall and their several follow-up variants), classifier-based metrics (Inception Score, Number of Modes), domain-specific similarity-based metrics (IntDiv in molecular generation, pairwise-BLEU and d-lex-sim in text, n-gram diversity), and a smaller set of more exotic proposals (the Birthday Paradox Test, GILBO, quality-diversity grid-based metrics) each of which the paper argues is either domain-restricted or has a specific, demonstrated failure mode. Its closest mathematical relative, a similarity-sensitive ecological diversity index by Leinster and Cobbold (2012) previously imported into ML by Posada et al. (2020), is explicitly distinguished as also failing to capture correlations between features, the same specific failure the paper's toy example (Figure 1) diagnoses in IntDiv. The paper draws a deliberate cross-disciplinary bridge, importing an "effective number of species" framing from theoretical ecology (Hill 1973, Jost 2006, Leinster 2021) and connecting it formally to the von Neumann entropy of quantum statistical mechanics, a genre of "borrow a well-developed formalism from another field and show it solves a persistent measurement gap in ML" contribution that is somewhat unusual for the applied-ML metrics literature and is likely part of why the Vendi Score has since been picked up broadly beyond its original generative-modeling use case, including later use for measuring output diversity in language-model settings not tested in this paper itself. Within this corpus, the paper's core concern (quantifying how genuinely distinct a set of generated candidates is, beyond superficial pairwise dissimilarity) is conceptually adjacent to, though not directly engaged by, [26mira]'s "squad" architecture, which explicitly assigns a "similarity-checking role" to cluster near-duplicate hypotheses so that a crowd of agents does not keep re-proposing variants of the same idea; the Vendi Score, or a similar similarity-matrix-entropy approach, would be a natural, quantitative instrument for exactly the kind of hypothesis-diversity monitoring that role is informally trying to achieve, though neither paper cites or engages the other.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central mathematical claims are on solid footing: the four properties in Theorem 3.1 are proven rather than only illustrated, the connection to von Neumann entropy and effective rank is a genuine, previously-noted mathematical fact rather than a loose analogy, and the computational-complexity argument for the embedding-based fast path is a straightforward, verifiable linear-algebra fact. The empirical demonstrations are, in the strongest cases, genuinely compelling because they go beyond reporting a metric disagreement to explaining it: the MOSES molecular case is directly and visually confirmed as a real duplicate-clustering problem that IntDiv structurally cannot see, which is about as rigorous a validation as a diversity-metric paper can offer without a fully independent ground truth.

The clearest gap is a near-total absence of uncertainty quantification across every empirical comparison in the paper: Table 1's StackedMNIST comparison, Table 2's image-model comparisons, Table 3's decoding-algorithm comparison, and Table 4's molecular-model comparison each report single point-estimate scores (typically from one sample of a few hundred to a few thousand items) with no repeated-sampling variance, bootstrap confidence interval, or other measure of how much a Vendi Score would fluctuate under a different draw from the same model. Since several of the paper's own comparisons rest on differences that are not obviously large relative to plausible sampling noise (e.g., PresGAN's 866.6 versus Self-conditioned GAN's 746.7 on a single StackedMNIST sample; the LSUN Cat diffusion-model ranking that inverts relative to Precision/Recall), a reader cannot currently distinguish a robust ranking from one that might not replicate under a fresh sample, a real limitation for a paper whose main practical pitch is that the Vendi Score can be trusted to reveal fine-grained distinctions existing metrics miss. A second, more specific gap concerns the LSUN interpretation discussed above: the claim that ADM's lower Vendi Score despite higher Precision/Recall reflects "many near-duplicates" is offered without the same direct kernel-matrix visualization that made the molecular case convincing, so this specific mechanistic explanation for the image-domain disagreement remains an unverified, if plausible, hypothesis rather than a demonstrated fact, an inconsistency in evidentiary standard relative to the paper's own strongest example. A third, smaller point, one the authors disclose themselves rather than one this review is raising independently, is that the metric's usefulness is entirely contingent on choosing an appropriate similarity function, and the paper offers no general guidance for how a practitioner in a new domain should validate that their chosen kernel is neither too sensitive nor too insensitive before trusting the resulting score, beyond the domain-specific choices (Morgan fingerprints, Inception cosine similarity, n-gram overlap) already established by prior work in each of the three domains tested here.

What remains well supported: the Vendi Score is a mathematically well-defined, provably well-behaved diversity measure that requires no reference dataset, no labels, and no fixed feature representation, a genuine generality advantage over every specific alternative the paper surveys; it demonstrably catches at least one concrete, real diversity failure (duplicate clustering in a widely-used molecular-generation benchmark) that the field's incumbent metric misses entirely, confirmed by direct inspection rather than only by the metric's own say-so; and its behavior on synthetic and ground-truth-labeled data tracks true underlying diversity more consistently than the average-similarity alternative it is compared against throughout.
