Citation: Dat Tran, Douwe Kiela. Single-Agent LLMs Outperform Multi-Agent Systems on Multi-Hop Reasoning Under Equal Thinking Token Budgets. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460v2

## What this paper is about

Multi-agent LLM systems (a planner plus workers, debaters, role-players, ensembles) often report better accuracy than a single model. They also usually spend more tokens, so it is hard to tell whether the architecture or the extra computation is doing the work. This paper holds one resource fixed, the number of "thinking" tokens a system may spend on intermediate reasoning, and compares a single reasoning pass against five multi-agent designs on two multi-hop question-answering benchmarks, using three model families. The authors also give an information-theoretic argument for why a single agent should win when it uses its context well, and probe when multi-agent designs catch up by deliberately corrupting the single agent's reasoning. They report that the single agent matches or beats every multi-agent design at equal thinking budget, and that Gemini's API budget accounting and benchmark phrasing can both inflate apparent multi-agent gains.

## Extended summary

Setup. The controlled variable is the thinking-token budget B: tokens of intermediate reasoning, excluding prompts and final answers (Section 1). Budgets are 100, 500, 1k, 2k, 5k, and 10k. For the open models the budget is a hard cap on generated think text (Appendix E). For Gemini the paper uses the API's thinkingBudget parameter, which Google documents as a guide rather than a cap (Appendix G). The budget is an upper limit only; systems are not forced to spend it (Limitation iii).

Tasks. FRAMES and the 4-hop subset of MuSiQue, both multi-hop world-knowledge questions with short answers (Section 4.1). The bucket counts in Table 2 sum to 1,175 MuSiQue questions; the FRAMES sample size is not stated. The prompt templates in Appendix D contain only the question, and the paper does not say whether supporting passages are supplied.

Systems. The single agent (SAS) is one call with the full budget and the prompt "think step by step, then answer." SAS-L adds a short scaffold asking the model to list ambiguities, propose interpretations, choose one, and then answer, at the same budget. The multi-agent systems (MAS) share the global budget B: Sequential (a planner writes ordered steps, each worker gets B/k and sees previous step outputs, an aggregator answers without solving), Subtask-parallel (independent subtasks, equal splits, a reducer), Parallel-roles (Solver, Fact Extractor, Skeptic, Second Solver, then an aggregator), Debate (two debaters answer, critique each other once, a judge picks), and Ensemble (independent workers at temperature 0.7, a judge picks). All other runs use temperature 0. Sequential is the main comparator because, like SAS, it is one serial trajectory; the difference is that intermediate states are passed as explicit text messages (Section 4.3, Figure 1). Planner and aggregator are kept "near budget-neutral whenever possible."

Evaluation. An LLM judge receives the question, prediction, and gold answer and decides whether the gold answer's meaning is present in the prediction (Section 4.4, Appendix D.7). Section 4.4 calls the judge "a separate evaluation model"; Appendix D.7 says evaluation runs right after prediction "using the same model." The judge model is not named. Results carry 95% bootstrap confidence intervals; bold in Table 1 marks the best system and every system whose interval overlaps it. The resampling unit is not described.

Theory (Section 3). Let C be the full context available to a single agent and M = g(C) the messages passed between agents. By the Data Processing Inequality, I(Y; C) ≥ I(Y; M). The authors then show that any estimator working from M can be reproduced by an estimator working from C that first samples M itself, so the minimum achievable error from C is no larger than from M. With a degraded effective context C̃α = Tα(C), the guarantee I(Y; C) ≥ I(Y; M) no longer implies I(Y; C̃α) ≥ I(Y; M), so a structured MAS "may recover task-relevant information more reliably than a degraded single pass." The stated prediction is that SAS dominates under low degradation and that the gap shrinks or reverses as degradation grows.

Main results (Table 1, Appendix F). Excluding the 100-token budget, which the authors say produces no useful reasoning for any system, SAS is the best system or overlaps the best on the averaged rows at every budget: averages are 0.390 (SAS) vs 0.376 (Sequential) and 0.380 (Debate) at 500 tokens, 0.418 vs 0.379 and 0.388 at 1k, 0.427 vs 0.386 and 0.420 at 5k. The open models show the clearest gaps. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B on MuSiQue at 2k: SAS 0.418, Sequential 0.327, Debate 0.352. Qwen3-30B-A3B on MuSiQue at 2k: SAS 0.271, Sequential 0.229. SAS also spends less. Qwen3 SAS at a 10k cap averages 1,307 thinking tokens on FRAMES, against 4,061 for Debate and 5,571 for Ensemble. Debate is the strongest MAS variant, and on Gemini-2.5-Pro MuSiQue it is often the best system overall (0.470 at 5k against SAS 0.419). Ensemble is weak at low budgets and becomes the best system on Gemini-2.5-Pro FRAMES at 5k and 10k (0.710, 0.719). SAS-L helps mainly on Gemini: on Gemini-2.5-Flash MuSiQue it is the best single-agent setting in every panel (0.369 vs 0.338 for SAS at 10k), while on Qwen3 plain SAS is usually better. Accuracy rises with budget and flattens around 1k to 2k tokens.

Gemini version sweep (Section 5.2, Table 12). With unlimited thinking on MuSiQue: Gemini-2-Flash-Lite 0.258 vs 0.255 (SAS vs Sequential), Gemini-2-Flash 0.347 vs 0.308, Gemini-2.5-Flash 0.296 vs 0.294, Gemini-2.5-Pro 0.399 vs 0.396, Gemini-3-Pro-Preview 0.491 vs 0.489. The authors report that accuracy increases monotonically "starting from Gemini-2.5-Flash" and that SAS stays competitive with, and usually slightly above, Sequential.

Context degradation (Section 5.3, Appendix E, Table 13). Qwen3-30B-A3B, MuSiQue, 1k budget. The model generates its think text, the text is corrupted, and the answer is then generated from the corrupted reasoning. Four corruptions: word deletion, masking, random-token substitution (fraction α), and k topically similar distractor sentences. Under substitution SAS goes 0.243, 0.231, 0.200 at α = 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 while Sequential stays at 0.217, 0.223, 0.225. Masking at α = 0.7 gives SAS 0.220 vs Sequential 0.231. Deletion shows no consistent crossover, and with distractors SAS stays ahead (0.257 vs 0.228 at k = 10). The authors conclude that MAS becomes competitive, and occasionally better, when the single agent's effective context is corrupted, and that the headline claim is bounded to "matched budgets and proper context utilization."

Gemini accounting (Appendix G, Tables 7 to 10). The authors track the API-reported thought-token count, the visible thought word count, and a proxy of 10/7 tokens per word. For Gemini-2.5-Flash SAS at a 10k request, the API reports 1,687 thought tokens while the visible thought text is 251 words (about 359 tokens), a 4.7x gap. Visible SAS thought text plateaus near 350 tokens between the 1k and 10k requests. Sequential surfaces more visible thought than SAS at the same requested budget (693 vs 390 proxy tokens for Gemini-2.5-Pro at 1k), because its k calls each return a thought block. The authors therefore match on requested budget, call this the only fair and reproducible option, and ask API providers to document how thought tokens are counted.

Paraphrase ablation (Appendix A, Table 11). A rule-based light paraphrase lowers accuracy slightly (Gemini-2.5-Flash SAS 0.331 to 0.326 at 1k; Qwen3 Sequential 0.229 to 0.204 at 2k). A deep paraphrase written by Gemini-2.5-Flash raises it (Gemini-2.5-Flash SAS 0.331 to 0.358 at 1k). The authors read this as evidence that original phrasings may be memorized. The SAS-above-Sequential ordering holds in all three conditions.

Error analysis (Appendix B, Table 2). MuSiQue at 1k tokens, SAS vs Sequential, split into four buckets. When only Sequential is right, the gold answer appears in Sequential's thoughts far more often (41.7% vs 12.5% on Gemini-2.5-Flash; 56.7% vs 18.3% on Qwen3), and Sequential covers about twice as many entities on Gemini. When only SAS is right, the pattern flips (42.7% vs 18.6% on Gemini; 63.5% vs 28.1% on Qwen3). Extraction failures, where the gold appears in the reasoning but not in the final answer, occur in 9 of 72 SAS failures and 23 of 124 Sequential failures on Gemini. When both are wrong, the gold almost never appears (about 3%). The authors label the modes under-exploration (SAS), over-exploration and drift (Sequential), and loss of the correct span at finalization (both).

Stated limitations (Appendix C). Text-only multi-hop reasoning; tools, vision, and safety constraints are out of scope. Gemini thinking accounting is approximate. Only the upper budget is enforced.

## Learnings

A thinking-token cap is a ceiling, not a spend. The open-model single agent used about 1,300 to 1,500 (Qwen3) and under 1,000 (DeepSeek) tokens of a 10k cap, while Debate and Ensemble used about 2,500 to 6,000. "Matched budget" therefore often means a cheaper single agent against a more expensive team. Where the team did win (Debate on Gemini-2.5-Pro MuSiQue), it surfaced more visible thought text at every budget and, from 2k up, reported two to four times the single agent's thought tokens. A prompt that pushes the single agent to think longer (SAS-L) recovered part of that gap on Gemini but not on the open models.

Gemini's thinkingBudget cannot be used as a compute meter. The API-reported thought count ran up to 4.7x above the visible thought text, visible text plateaued at about 350 tokens whatever the request, and splitting a budget across k calls returned more visible thought text. At the 100-token request, Gemini-2.5-Pro multi-agent runs reported roughly three to five times the single agent's thought tokens, so per-call minimums leak compute to whichever design makes more calls. Any budget-matched comparison on this API needs its own accounting audit.

The Sequential pipeline was flat across budgets from 100 to 10k tokens on MuSiQue for both open models and on FRAMES for DeepSeek (DeepSeek MuSiQue 0.328 to 0.327), and on Qwen3 MuSiQue it barely moved under corruption of its reasoning. Its ceiling seems to be set by the planner, the text handoff, or the aggregator, not by the reasoning budget. That is a property of these author-written prompts as much as of sequential decomposition.

Whether the gold answer appears anywhere in the reasoning trace largely decides which architecture wins, and both architectures sometimes find the answer and then drop it at the final step. Splitting failures into "never surfaced" and "surfaced then lost" is a cheap diagnostic that separates search failures from extraction failures. For Gemini it only covers the visible part of the reasoning.

## Verification

Satellite. Answer correctness is decided by an LLM judge with a presence rubric: is the meaning of the gold answer in the prediction? The judge is unnamed, Sections 4.4 and D.7 disagree on whether it is the generating model, and there is no human or exact-match agreement check. The paper's only validation of the judge is that accuracy rises with Gemini capability in the version sweep. The paper does audit its own compute measure: it checks Gemini's reported thought tokens against content-based counts, finds them inconsistent, and switches to requested budget as the matched variable. The paraphrase ablation is offered as a contamination probe. Inside the multi-agent designs, the Skeptic role, the Debate critique, and the Ensemble and Debate judges are same-model LLM checks with no external signal. Debate was the strongest MAS variant. Section 3 suggests MAS might help by imposing "filtering, or verification structure," but no external verifier, tool, or executable check is tested.

## Field context

The authors place the work in the budget-controlled evaluation line, where elaborate prompting and search strategies lose much of their advantage once token budgets are matched (Wang et al., 2024, Reasoning in Token Economies; Han et al., 2025; SelfBudgeter; BudgetThinker). They differ from it by treating thinking tokens as the controlled resource and by comparing architectures rather than allocations within one architecture. They also cite concurrent work arguing that MAS gains depend on regime and implementation: Anthropic's 2025 engineering note attributing much of its multi-agent research system's advantage to extra compute, Kim et al. (2025) on coordination overhead and shrinking agentic benefit with stronger models, Cemri et al. (2025) on multi-agent failure taxonomies, and MAS-Orchestra (Ke et al., 2026). They cite the long-context literature (lost in the middle, context rot, context length alone hurting performance) as motivation for the degradation experiments.

Within this corpus, the closest neighbor is 26masat ("Capable language models can outgrow the benefits of collaboration"), whose author list and topic match the Kim et al. work cited here. That study holds prompts, tools, and compute fixed across six agentic benchmarks and finds that single-agent baseline performance predicts whether coordination helps, with sequential tasks such as PlanCraft losing badly under multi-agent designs. This paper tests a narrower, non-agentic regime (short-answer multi-hop QA, thinking-token caps, no tools) and agrees in direction: sequential decomposition with text handoff hurts. One detail sits awkwardly with a simple capability-saturation reading. In this paper the clearest MAS win (Debate) comes on the strongest model tested under budget caps, Gemini-2.5-Pro, although it co-occurs with higher actual spend. The methodological move also echoes 24selfcor, where multi-agent debate on GSM8K fell below self-consistency once the number of calls was matched. The Data Processing Inequality framing formalizes the text-bottleneck argument that motivates 2511.09149 (Interlat), which passes hidden states between agents instead of text. That paper tries to shrink the bottleneck; this one argues for not splitting the reasoning in the first place.

What this paper adds, by our reading: a thinking-token-matched comparison across five MAS designs, a documented accounting hazard in a widely used reasoning API, and a stated boundary (corrupted reasoning context) for the single-agent advantage.

## Critical discussion

On the two open models the central result is well supported. For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B and Qwen3-30B-A3B at budgets of 1k and above, SAS is at or near the top in every cell and spends a fraction of the tokens the MAS variants use. Some gaps are large (DeepSeek MuSiQue: 0.418 vs 0.327 for Sequential at 2k). These are hard-capped runs, so the compute comparison is honest, and the direction survives any reasonable widening of the error bars.

The word "consistently" leans on the averaged rows. By the paper's own bolding, plain SAS is not in the top group on Gemini-2.5-Pro MuSiQue at any budget from 500 to 10k. Debate (0.435 to 0.470) and, at several budgets, Parallel-roles beat it, and SAS-L ties only at 500, 2k, and 5k. On Gemini-2.5-Pro FRAMES at 500 tokens, Sequential and Debate (both 0.660) beat SAS (0.600) with non-overlapping intervals. In those MuSiQue cells Debate surfaces more visible thought text than SAS at every budget, and from 2k up it also reports two to four times SAS's API thought count (4,644 vs 1,526 at 5k). That fits the authors' compute story, but it also shows that equal caps do not equalize compute: the single agent stops early and the team does not. The supported claim is "at equal thinking caps, a single agent is cheaper and usually no less accurate." The paper does not show "equal thinking spent." The Limitations section concedes that only the cap is enforced, but the title and abstract do not. The 100-token exclusion has the same flavor. On Gemini-2.5-Pro MuSiQue the MAS variants at that setting reported 294 to 403 thought tokens against 84 for SAS, with a similar gap on FRAMES. The large MAS wins there (Sequential 0.654 vs SAS 0.368 on FRAMES) are a compute leak, not an absence of useful reasoning.

The uncertainty reporting does not hold up. With 1,175 MuSiQue questions and accuracy near 0.26, a per-question bootstrap would give a 95% half-width of about 0.025. The reported intervals are often 0.007 to 0.02 wide in total (Qwen3 SAS at 2k: 0.269 to 0.276). The resampling unit is never stated, and three point estimates lie outside their own intervals (Table 4 Sequential at 500: 0.226 against 0.229 to 0.235; Table 5 Ensemble at 10k: 0.458 against 0.442 to 0.455; Table 8 Debate at 10k: 0.458 against 0.461 to 0.477). Run-to-run variation also seems larger than the intervals admit. Gemini-2.5-Flash SAS scores 0.338 to 0.344 on MuSiQue at the 5k and 10k caps (Table 7) but 0.296 with unlimited thinking (Table 12), a shift the paper does not discuss. The practical reading is that gaps under about three to four points should be treated as ties, whatever the bolding says. That weakens several "SAS is indistinguishable from the best" cells and most of the degradation crossovers, while leaving the large open-model gaps intact.

The theory frames the result but does not derive it. The Data Processing Inequality argument compares Bayes-optimal predictors, and the thinking budget appears nowhere in it. A single agent reproducing a multi-agent computation would have to spend tokens to do so, which is exactly the constraint the paper claims to study. Real LLMs are also far from Bayes-optimal, so an ordering of minimum achievable errors does not order actual systems. And in the experiments the MAS messages are not a post-processing of the single agent's context: both systems generate fresh reasoning from the same question, and the inequality then says only that neither can exceed the information in the question. The degradation extension says that once context is degraded, either ordering is possible. That permits the observed crossover without predicting it. The Section 3 display equations are missing from the extract, but the prose is complete enough to follow the argument.

The degradation experiment is narrower than its interpretation. The corruption is applied to the model's own generated think text before answering (Appendix E), not to long or noisy input documents, yet the introduction and conclusion speak of "long or noisy contexts." It covers one model, one dataset, and one budget. The paper does not say how the corruption is applied across Sequential's k step outputs. Sequential's accuracy stays at 0.215 to 0.233 across every corruption type and level, about its clean value (0.229), and that value did not move across budgets either. So the crossover is SAS falling to Sequential's floor, not Sequential proving more robust through "filtering, decomposing, or stabilizing." Only the substitution result at α = 0.7 (0.200 vs 0.225) is plausibly larger than sampling noise.

The multi-agent baselines are minimal and author-written. Debate has one critique round. The Sequential workers are told to "think only for your step," and the aggregator is told not to solve the question. Sequential's flat response to budget on MuSiQue for both open models points to a pipeline bottleneck. The abstract's conclusion that "many reported advantages" of MAS reflect unaccounted compute therefore goes beyond the evidence: no published MAS is reproduced and then shown to lose its gain under budget matching. The comparison tests these five implementations on text-only multi-hop QA. It does not test tool-using or retrieval-parallel systems, whose gains are the ones most often reported. The accounting does push in one direction that favors MAS: step outputs, planner JSON, and inter-agent messages are generated tokens outside the thinking budget. On that axis the SAS result is conservative.

On the paraphrase ablation, the memorization reading does not follow. Memorization of original phrasings would predict that the heavier rewrite hurts more, but the deep paraphrase helped. It was also written by Gemini-2.5-Flash, and the largest gain went to Gemini-2.5-Flash, which fits a model answering better in its own register or on a disambiguated question. What the ablation does support is that the SAS-over-Sequential ordering survives rewording. Similarly, the version-sweep argument that accuracy rising with capability shows "the evaluation is aligned with model quality" is weak. The sweep is non-monotone (Gemini-2-Flash 0.347 above Gemini-2.5-Flash 0.296), and if D.7 is right that each model judges itself, judge and generator change together. Within one model, SAS and MAS share the judge, so the architecture ordering is less exposed. Absolute accuracies across models are not comparable on this evidence. Finally, the paper never says whether MuSiQue and FRAMES are run closed-book or with their supporting passages. That matters for whether "context" in the theory means provided documents or the model's own reasoning.

What remains after these caveats: on text-only multi-hop QA with two open reasoning models, one reasoning pass under a hard thinking cap is at least as accurate as five simple multi-agent designs and much cheaper. Where multi-agent designs win in these tables, they also spend more. Gemini's thinking-budget parameter is not a reliable way to match compute. The information-theoretic argument and the degradation boundary are suggestive framing, not established mechanisms.

## Relevance to us

This is direct evidence for the discipline behind B01 and for the "complex vs deliberately simple agents" tension. A same-budget single-agent baseline belongs in any multi-agent comparison, and the paper adds two concrete hazards worth writing into our evaluation harness. First, a reasoning cap is not a spend, so a fair fight must report tokens actually consumed, not tokens allowed. Second, Gemini's thinkingBudget is a guide with per-call floors, so any Theo-versus-frontier comparison that routes through it needs its own accounting audit. The result also fits the observation in our notes that agent gains, once ablated, often collapse to more compute or a feedback loop rather than to agent count.

It does not settle the question for physics. Short-answer multi-hop QA is a regime where one reasoning chain suffices and no external checker exists. The regime we care about, derivations with solvers, unit checks, or executable feedback, is exactly what the paper excludes, and our current belief is that structure earns its keep there through the verifier, not the org chart. The degradation boundary is tempting for long-lived sessions (B09), where a notebook accumulates noise. But random-token corruption of the model's own trace is not accumulated research context, so this paper does not tell us whether splitting work across agents helps when a session's memory degrades. The same-model Skeptic and Debate critics also do not beat a single pass here, which is consistent with our prior that same-model critique is weak without an external signal (B13).
