## What this paper is about

Command-line coding agents like Claude Code and Gemini CLI can already read and write files across long sessions, but a real research project still fragments across chat windows, IDEs, terminals, and writing tools, and the decisions that would make the whole process auditable get lost along the way. Dr. Claw is not another autonomous research agent — it's an open-source workspace that wraps an existing coding-agent executor in a controllable, human-in-the-loop workflow, adding persistent state, a reusable skill library, and multi-executor coordination on top, so that planning, execution, and writing become one traceable, recoverable loop instead of scattered artifacts.

## Extended summary

**Design.** Dr. Claw is organized around four persistent state objects — Task Graph, Artifact Store, Decision Log, Execution Trace — updated through an explicit Plan→Execute→Verify→Write-back loop, with a three-layer architecture (Interaction, Orchestration, Execution) that lets the backend executor be swapped without changing workflow semantics. A reusable skill library (58 stage-mapped skills across five research stages — survey, ideation, experiment, publication, promotion — out of 171 in the full deployed catalogue) reaches a task through three routes: automatic stage-to-skill mapping, keyword-triggered autoload, or manual invocation from a dashboard; skills are versioned, schema-checked file directories with a SKILL.md manifest. A permission-policy mechanism restricts executable actions to those allowed under the current policy, and non-destructive recovery APIs (update status, revise, append, continue) let a failed step be fixed in place without deleting prior artifacts. The paper positions Dr. Claw's specific combination — wrapping an existing coding-agent executor, making the research process itself a first-class persisted object, and sustaining human takeover across the full ideation-to-publication arc — against a design-space comparison table of eight related systems, none of which the authors claim combines all three axes simultaneously.

**Automated evaluation (Section 5).** Holding the backend executor fixed (codex provider, gpt-5.4, matched permission profile) so that "bare codex" and "Dr. Claw" differ only in the orchestration bundle (task graph, state objects, skill library) as a whole — explicitly not an ablation of individual components — the paper compares completeness on three open-ended, unenumerated research prompts across two medical-imaging classification tasks (Derm7pt) and one clinical-note risk task. Completeness is scored deterministically (no LLM judge) against 21 expected research best-practice elements (modeling rigor plus research-hygiene items: limitations section, subgroup analysis, real literature citations). Dr. Claw wins two of three tasks and ties the third, pooling 0.952 versus the bare agent's 0.873. The gap is entirely in research-hygiene elements — the two conditions are identical (1.00 each) on core modeling rigor (multiple models, cross-validation, calibration, ablation) — with Dr. Claw's skills raising limitations-section and subgroup-analysis pass rates from 0.33 to 1.00 and literature-citation pass rate from 0.00 to 0.67 (the bare agent producing zero real citations across all three tasks). On the one tied task, the relevant reference-audit skill simply failed to fire, which the paper identifies as exactly the mechanism that, when triggered, produces the advantage — skill selection is deterministic given a task's stage/type, but skill invocation is not enforced, only suggested in the next-action prompt. The paper reports this pilot's pooled completion delta (+0.079) with a 95% bootstrap CI of [-0.00, +0.14] that includes zero, explicitly noting the direction is consistent across all three tasks but not statistically significant at this sample size, and that Dr. Claw is slower due to the overhead of recording state. A separate, single-condition failure-recovery walkthrough shows an induced wrong-path error being captured in the execution trace and fixed in place without deleting the five pre-existing files, but this demonstration has no matched bare-agent comparison.

**Human study (Appendix A).** A retrospective, hybrid-design study with seven AI PhD participants compares No-AI, general-purpose Web/Desktop-AI, and Dr. Claw across three research stages (Ideation, Experiment, Publication) on completion-time bands, blind-rated output quality, tool-switching counts, and self-reported experience. Dr. Claw participants used live logs after a three-week familiarization period; the two control conditions were retrospectively self-reported. Friedman tests followed by Holm-corrected pairwise Wilcoxon tests find Dr. Claw associated with shorter completion times, higher blind-rated output quality, fewer tool switches, and higher experience scores, with the Experience metric significant in all three stages against both controls and Time Band significant in stages 2-3; the authors label the whole protocol exploratory rather than causal given the mixed live-log/retrospective design and small sample.

## Learnings

Deliberately choosing a scoring criterion that requires no model-in-the-loop judgment (a deterministic count of 21 concretely defined best-practice elements against produced files) is a reusable evaluation design choice for any paper wanting to avoid the LLM-judge circularity and reliability concerns that affect most other agent-evaluation papers in this space — it trades away nuance (a coverage count is not a correctness check, as the paper itself states) for a result that does not depend on trusting any judge model's calibration.

The distinction the paper draws between skill selection (deterministic, given task stage/type) and skill invocation (best-effort, only suggested in a prompt, not enforced) is a specific, generalizable design lesson for any system that routes an LLM agent toward reusable procedural resources: a recommendation mechanism is not the same as a compliance mechanism, and the single task where this system underperformed its own baseline is directly attributable to exactly this gap — a finding that motivates a concrete next design step (verifying skill execution rather than only recommending it) rather than a vague call for "more reliability."

The observation that an orchestration layer's advantage over a bare coding agent concentrated entirely in "research hygiene" elements (limitations sections, subgroup analysis, real citations) while core modeling rigor was identical across both conditions is a useful, specific finding about where current coding agents' unprompted defaults actually fall short: not in technical execution, but in the scientific-writing conventions that make a result trustworthy and complete, which is exactly the kind of gap a skill/checklist layer is well suited to close.

## Verification

The automated completeness comparison is scored deterministically against produced files with an explicit 21-item rubric and no model-in-the-loop judgment, which sidesteps the LLM-judge reliability concerns present in much of the surrounding literature. The paper is also unusually explicit about the limits of its own evidence: it reports a 95% bootstrap CI for the pooled completion delta that includes zero, explicitly states the pilot is "not statistically powered," and dedicates a full self-authored Limitations section to naming exactly which comparisons are not ablations, which measure (completeness) is coverage rather than correctness, and which confounds (prior tool familiarity, single domain, non-state-of-the-art comparison baseline) remain unaddressed. The human study similarly reports exact test statistics (Friedman χ², Holm-corrected pairwise p-values) rather than only summary claims, and explicitly flags its own hybrid live-log-versus-retrospective-report design as a validity threat. What is not verified: the completeness metric's construct validity (whether checking off 21 elements correlates with actual research quality, as opposed to merely checklist compliance) is asserted but not independently tested, and the single failure-recovery walkthrough has no matched control run, so its recovery capability is demonstrated only in isolation.

## Field context

Dr. Claw positions itself explicitly against three categories of prior systems it tabulates directly (Table 1): end-to-end autonomous research systems (AI Scientist v1/v2, Agent Laboratory, ResearchAgent), general agent-authoring/orchestration runtimes (AutoGen Studio, Flowise, LangGraph), and intervenable research agents (TinyScientist, ResearStudio, IRIS), arguing its distinguishing combination is wrapping an existing coding-agent executor rather than building a new one, shipping built-in research-specific state objects rather than a schema developers must declare themselves, and sustaining human takeover across the full ideation-to-publication arc rather than within a single stage — while candidly noting each individual axis has precedent (TinyScientist also wraps an external coding agent; AI Scientist-v2 persists a structured experiment tree; ResearStudio permits pause at any moment, which the paper states is stronger than Dr. Claw's checkpoint-based takeover). Within this batch, Dr. Claw sits alongside several other AI-Scientist/AutoResearch systems reviewed this session (Spark-to-Paper, [2608.11924.md](2608.11924.md); OmniScientist, [2608.13558.md](2608.13558.md); Faraday/Replica, [2608.13331.md](2608.13331.md)) but occupies a distinct niche within that space: those systems each aim to automate more of the research pipeline end-to-end, whereas Dr. Claw explicitly rejects full autonomy as its design goal and instead targets the orchestration and auditability layer around a human who retains final decision authority at every stage — a genuinely different design philosophy rather than a competing claim about capability.

## Critical discussion

The paper's own candor about its evaluation's limits is a real strength and makes much of the critical work already done by the authors themselves: the Limitations section explicitly states the executor-fixed comparison is not an ablation (so the observed gap cannot be attributed to the task graph, skill library, or state objects individually), that completeness is a coverage count rather than a correctness check, that the comparison target is the bare executor rather than a state-of-the-art competing orchestration framework, and that all tasks come from a single domain (medical), leaving transfer to other research areas untested. This level of self-disclosure is unusual and should be credited rather than treated as something a reviewer needed to independently uncover.

Two things nonetheless remain worth naming explicitly as scope limits on how the results should be read, beyond what the authors themselves flag. First, the automated evaluation is three tasks with one run each, all in one domain, and the reported pooled completion advantage's own 95% bootstrap CI includes zero; the directionally consistent win-two-tie-one pattern is suggestive but the sample is far too small to support a claim of general superiority over the bare executor across research domains or task types, and the one task where Dr. Claw did not outperform the baseline is directly traced to the skill-invocation-is-not-enforced gap that the paper itself identifies as the system's clearest remaining reliability issue (`narrow-evaluation`). Second, because the comparison bundles the task graph, state objects, and skill library together against the bare executor, there is no way from this evidence alone to determine whether the observed completeness gain comes mainly from the skill library's explicit checklisting (which seems most directly implicated by the specific hygiene-element pattern reported) or from the state-object/orchestration machinery more broadly — the paper explicitly names a skill-only-versus-orchestration-only ablation as the natural next experiment, which is the right diagnosis but means the current results cannot yet attribute the benefit to a specific mechanism (`missing-control`). The human study adds a further asymmetry worth noting: Dr. Claw participants received a three-week familiarization period before being measured via live logs, while the two control conditions were assessed only via retrospective self-report with no equivalent structured familiarization, which the paper's own validity-threats list acknowledges but which still means the comparison is not a matched, controlled test of the tool in isolation from a practice effect.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the deterministic, model-judgment-free completeness metric gives a specific, checkable account of exactly which research-process elements a skill-library-augmented workflow adds over a bare coding agent (limitations sections, subgroup analysis, literature citations), and the skill-selection-versus-invocation distinction is a genuine, evidence-grounded diagnosis of the system's current reliability bottleneck rather than a speculative concern. The broader claim that Dr. Claw's specific orchestration design meaningfully reduces context-switching costs and improves research output quality in general practice is supported only by a small, exploratory, non-causal pilot and human study, exactly as the authors themselves describe it.
