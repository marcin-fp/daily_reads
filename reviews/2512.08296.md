## What this paper is about

When does splitting an agentic task across several LLM agents beat giving it to one agent? The authors run one model from each of three families (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic) at three capability tiers through six interactive benchmarks, including web research, financial analysis, Minecraft crafting, workplace tool use, GitHub issue fixing, and terminal tasks. Each runs under a single agent and four multi-agent topologies, holding prompts and tools fixed, for 260 configurations. They report that multi-agent systems help a lot on tasks that split into parallel research threads, hurt a lot on short sequential tasks, and on average do nothing. They fit a regression that relates performance to model capability, task properties, and coordination measurements, and they argue that coordination stops paying once a single agent already succeeds about 45% of the time. This is the preprint of the study later published as slug:26masat.

## Extended summary

Setup (Sections 3 and 4.1, Appendices D and E). The agentic benchmarks are BrowseComp-Plus (100 instances), Finance Agent (50), PlanCraft (100), WorkBench (100), SWE-bench Verified (20, seed-42 shuffle of 500), and Terminal-Bench (first 20 of 86). Tool counts are 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, and 16. The models are GPT-5-nano, GPT-5-mini, GPT-5, Gemini-2.0-Flash, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude Sonnet 3.7, 4, and 4.5; Sonnet 3.7 is missing on SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench, giving 45 + 45 + 45 + 45 + 40 + 40 = 260 configurations. Capability is measured by an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (42 to 71), partly reconstructed by the authors for models without official scores (Appendix A, Table 6).

The five architectures are these. SAS is one agent with at most 10 iterations. Independent is 3 agents whose outputs are concatenated, with no voting or comparison. Centralized is an orchestrator plus 3 sub-agents, up to 5 rounds of 3 iterations each. Decentralized is 3 agents in 3 debate rounds of 3 iterations each, with voting. Hybrid is an orchestrator plus limited peer exchange. Compute matching is described three ways: "matched maximum total iterations," with SAS given "proportionally more reasoning rounds" (Section 4.1); "matched for total reasoning tokens (mean 4,800 per trial)" (Table 5); and MAS representing "1.6 to 6.2x token budgets relative to single-agent at matched performance" (Section 4.3). Prompts are identical across conditions and not tuned per model.

Main results (Section 4.2, Figure 2 caption). Changes are relative to the SAS mean on each benchmark:
- Finance Agent: SAS 0.349, Centralized 0.631 (+80.8%), Decentralized 0.609 (+74.5%), Hybrid 0.604 (+73.1%); every MAS gains at least 57%.
- PlanCraft: SAS 0.568, Hybrid 0.346 (-39.1%), Decentralized 0.332 (-41.5%), Centralized 0.282 (-50.3%), Independent 0.170 (-70.0%).
- WorkBench: SAS 0.629, Decentralized 0.664 (+5.6%), Centralized and Hybrid -1.2%.
- BrowseComp-Plus: SAS 0.318, Decentralized 0.347 (+9.2%), Centralized +0.2%, Independent about -35%.
- SWE-bench Verified: SAS 0.522, every MAS below it (Hybrid -2.1%, Independent -14.9%).
- Terminal-Bench: SAS 0.344, Independent +1.7%, Centralized -19.2%.

Across everything, the mean MAS change is -0.3% (reported as a 95% CI of -58.7% to +77.2%, with SD 37.5%). Trace examples contrast PlanCraft, where a three-step craft gets broken into redundant sub-agent jobs, with Finance Agent, where news, filings, and operations research run in parallel. The authors conclude that decomposability matters more than complexity: Finance (complexity score D = 0.41) and PlanCraft (D = 0.42) have nearly the same score and opposite outcomes. The D scores in Appendix C are partly calibrated on observed MAS degradation.

Coordination metrics (Table 5). These are architecture-level constants applied to every benchmark:

| SAS | 0.466 | 7.2 | 0% | 0.466 | 1.0x | 67.7 |
| Independent | 0.370 | 11.4 | 58% | 0.234 | 17.2x | 42.4 |

Efficiency is defined as success divided by relative turn count. Trace-level error amplification is "estimated from execution-trace token analysis." Task-level error amplification (MAS error over SAS error) is about 1.1 to 1.3.

Scaling regression (Section 4.3, Tables 3, 4, 13). The outcome is the success of each of the 260 configurations. The model has 20 parameters: intelligence and its square, log tool count, log agent count, the single-agent baseline P_SA (the same model's SAS score on that benchmark), the five coordination metrics, and nine prespecified interactions. Five-fold CV over configurations gives R² train 0.463 and CV 0.373 (±0.170). Replacing the Intelligence Index with an Agentic Capability Index (each model's mean SAS score over the six benchmarks, r = 0.45 with the Index) gives CV 0.413. Table 3 shows the build-up in CV R²: intelligence plus tools plus agents 0.360, plus coordination metrics 0.363, plus baseline 0.358, plus interactions 0.373.

The error-amplification main effect and its tool interaction are not significant. A decision boundary from the fit corresponds to about 45% raw single-agent success. The authors report that the model picks the best architecture in 87% of held-out configurations, against 20% for random choice and 54% for a capability-only model. The 45% rule matches the direction of the MAS effect in 15 of 16 SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench model-benchmark pairs (p < 0.001, binomial test). Leave-one-dataset-out CV "highlights the challenge" of predicting absolute success on a new domain; v3 gives no number.

Mechanism analyses (Section 4.4):
- Turn count follows a power law in agent count with exponent 1.724, fit on architecture means. Extrapolation gives about 157 turns at 10 agents.
- Success levels off near 0.39 messages per turn.
- Centralized, Decentralized, and Hybrid reduce factual error by 22.7% on average (31.4% on Finance); Independent amplifies it by 4.6%.
- Error taxonomy baselines and reductions: logical contradiction 12.3 to 18.7%, down to 9.1% with Centralized; numerical drift 20.9 to 24.1%, down to 18.3%; context omission 15.8 to 25.2%, down to 8.3%. Coordination failures reach 12.4% for Hybrid.
- Contradiction is detected with a BERTScore threshold.
- Information gain correlates with the MAS-SAS gap on Finance and WorkBench (r = 0.71) but not on PlanCraft (r = 0.18).
- Kendall τ = 0.89 for architecture rankings across domains.
- Per-vendor cost per point of gain: about $0.008 for OpenAI Hybrid versus $0.024 for Anthropic Hybrid.
- Validator agreement with human raters: Cohen's κ 0.87 to 0.91 on four benchmarks.

Out-of-sample models (Appendix B, Tables 7 to 10). GPT-5.2, Gemini-3.0-Pro, and Gemini-3.0-Flash (all Index 75) were run on BrowseComp-Plus. The equation predicts identical values for all three and picks Hybrid as best (0.560), but Hybrid scored 0.39 to 0.44 and was never best. SAS is over-predicted by 16% to 53%. Overall MAE is 0.077 and MAS-only MAE 0.061. Eleven of fifteen qualitative finding-model pairs are marked validated. At the same Index, SAS scores differ across vendors (0.45 for GPT-5.2 against 0.34 to 0.36 for Gemini), which the authors read as the Index not being comparable across families.

Heterogeneous teams (Table 12, Figure 4, Limitation ii). In 13 configurations on BrowseComp-Plus, centralized mixes underperform the strong homogeneous team by 12.6 points on average, and decentralized mixes gain 2.0 points. Agent-count scaling from 1 to 9 on two Gemini models is described only in the Figure 5 caption (optimum at 7 agents for 2.0 Flash).

Stated limitations (Section 5):
- At most nine agents.
- Heterogeneity only across capability tiers within a family.
- Prompts not tuned per model.
- Six benchmarks; 20-instance subsets on SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench, with about ±20-point per-cell bootstrap intervals (Table 16).
- The token-based communication paradigm limits efficiency.
- Only six clusters make cluster-robust errors conservative.
- Absolute cross-domain prediction fails.

## Learnings

Multi-agent benefit on agentic tasks follows the task's structure more than its difficulty. The same four topologies gained 57% to 81% on Finance Agent, where news, filings, and operations can be researched in parallel, and lost 39% to 70% on PlanCraft, where a short chain of dependent actions gets split into redundant jobs. The two tasks have nearly identical complexity scores. The caveat is that these are gains at each architecture's own token spend, not at equal spend.

Parallel agents with no communication and no comparison step (outputs simply concatenated) were worse than one agent on average (0.370 vs 0.466). That makes "ensemble without a selector" a weak default. It is also not a self-consistency baseline, so it says nothing about best-of-n with voting.

"Compute-matched" in agent comparisons often means matched iteration caps or budgets, not matched spend. Here the multi-agent systems still took 1.6 to 6.2 times as many turns and had 1.6 to 5 times lower success per token. The same pattern appears in reviewed work on thinking-token caps (2604.02460). Any fair comparison should report tokens actually consumed.

A capability index built from static benchmarks predicted agentic single-agent performance poorly across vendors. Three models with the same Index of 75 scored 0.34 to 0.45 as single agents on BrowseComp-Plus, and the Index correlated only 0.45 with mean single-agent agentic success. For agent work, a task-grounded capability measure is the better axis.

## Verification

Satellite. Task success comes from benchmark-native checks: execution and tests on SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench, environment success on PlanCraft, function-call sequence match on WorkBench, expert rubrics on Finance Agent, and an LLM judge on BrowseComp-Plus, which is unnamed. For four benchmarks the paper reports that "domain-specific validators" of factual error agree with human raters at Cohen's κ 0.87 to 0.91; who the raters were and what was sampled are not described.

The paper frames Centralized and Decentralized topologies as verification: an orchestrator that "cross-checks reasoning steps before aggregation," or peer challenge-response. These are same-model LLM checks. The claimed evidence is lower trace-level error amplification (4.4x Centralized against 17.2x Independent) and a 22.7% average reduction in factual error. The regression terms for error amplification are not significant after controlling for other metrics. The error taxonomy (contradiction, numerical drift, context omission, coordination failure) comes with rates but no labeling protocol, and contradictions are flagged by a BERTScore similarity threshold. On the statistical side, the paper checks itself with cluster-robust standard errors, Holm-Bonferroni correction, an alternative capability metric, leave-one-dataset-out CV, and three held-out frontier models.

## Field context

The authors position the work against "more agents is all you need" (Li et al., 2024) and the collaborative scaling-law claims of Qian et al. (2025). They align with work reporting that single strong models often match teams: Gao et al. (2025), and practitioner posts from OpenHands and Cognition. They build on the MAST failure taxonomy (Cemri et al., 2025), the Agentic Benchmark Checklist (Zhu et al., 2025), and Kapoor et al. (2025) on cost-blind agent evaluation. Their main methodological argument is that earlier multi-agent gains were measured on static benchmarks (HumanEval, GSM8K), where voting cancels errors, rather than on interactive tasks, where errors compound.

Timeline and versions. v1 appeared on 2025-12-09 and v3, reviewed here, on 2026-04-08. The journal version, reviewed in this corpus as slug:26masat, was published on 2026-07-24 under a different title. The 26masat review dated the study from journal publication because its text referenced no preprint; this arXiv record is in fact the earliest public version. The two reviews describe one study and one dataset and must not be treated as corroboration of each other. Some numbers and framings differ between versions. The 26masat review also describes the 16 SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench configurations as independently held out; in v3 those benchmarks are part of the six-benchmark regression, and the text does not say the 45% threshold was fixed before they were added.

Other corpus connections. 2604.02460 (Tran and Kiela) cites this work as Kim et al. (2025). In a non-agentic regime (multi-hop QA at matched thinking-token caps), it finds single agents match or beat five multi-agent designs, and it runs into the same caps-versus-spend problem. 24selfcor found multi-agent debate falls below self-consistency once calls are matched. The absent baseline here is of that kind: a single agent given the multi-agent systems' actual spend, via more iterations or best-of-n with voting. Limitation (vi) points to latent-space communication as a way past the token bottleneck, the direction taken by 2511.09149 (Interlat).

## Critical discussion

The descriptive core is solid and useful. Nine models across three families and six genuinely interactive benchmarks are run under a common harness, with code and per-instance results released. The Finance-versus-PlanCraft contrast is large, consistent across architectures, and backed by trace examples. It is the clearest evidence in this corpus that multi-agent value depends on task structure. The paper also does more statistical self-auditing than most agent papers: cluster-robust errors, multiplicity correction, leave-one-dataset-out CV, and held-out models. Several of the problems below are visible only because the authors reported those checks.

Compute is not matched in the sense the paper needs. The text describes matching as iteration caps (Section 4.1) and as matched total reasoning tokens at a mean of 4,800 per trial (Table 5). Yet Table 5 reports success per 1,000 tokens of 67.7 for SAS against 13.6 to 42.4 for the multi-agent systems, at nearly identical success rates (0.45 to 0.48 for three of the four). That is arithmetically impossible if tokens were equal: the implied spend is about 1.3 to 4.8 times the single agent's. Section 4.3 itself says the MAS use "1.6 to 6.2x token budgets." The configurations in Appendix E.2 are also not iteration-matched: 10 iterations for SAS against up to 45 sub-agent iterations for Centralized. The paper therefore compares architectures at their own natural consumption. The large Finance gains may partly reflect a 10-iteration single agent that runs out of room; the trace example shows it doing "sequential exploration with insufficient breadth" in turns 3 to 7. A single agent with the same actual token spend, or best-of-n with a selector, is the missing control. Until it is run, "architecture-task alignment determines success" cannot be separated from "more compute on decomposable tasks helps."

The regression is weaker than presented, and parts of it are circular. The five coordination metrics are architecture-level constants applied to every benchmark (Table 5 caption). In the regression they therefore act as relabelled architecture dummies, not continuous measurements of each run. Efficiency E_c is computed as the architecture's mean success divided by relative turns (0.370 / (11.4 / 7.2) = 0.234 for Independent), so the outcome feeds a predictor. That predictor's tool interaction is one of the three terms surviving Holm correction. P_SA is the outcome itself for the 52 SAS rows. The Agentic Capability Index, which the authors recommend and which lifts CV R² from 0.373 to 0.413, is built from the same single-agent outcomes, including those in held-out folds. Table 3 shows that the whole coordination-metric apparatus adds 0.013 to CV R² over intelligence, tools, and agent count (0.360 to 0.373). Its caption says this "substantially improves predictive power" and that the full model has the best AIC. In fact its AIC (-236.3) is the worst of the four rows; the model with coordination structure but no interactions has the best (-243.2). That caption is simply wrong on the paper's own numbers.

The headline robustness claim is attached to the wrong coefficient. The Conclusion calls capability saturation "confirmed under both cluster-robust inference (p = 0.004) and Holm-Bonferroni correction (p_Holm = 0.018)." Those are the p-values of the P_SA main effect, which Table 4 labels a "task difficulty proxy." It says configurations score higher where the single agent scores higher, which is expected and says nothing about whether coordination helps. The saturation claim lives in the P_SA-by-agent-count interaction, which has cluster-robust p = 0.105 and Holm p = 0.084 (Tables 14, 15). So the paper's most robust finding is a near-tautology, and its most-cited finding is a directional pattern. The 26masat review reports the interaction's failure but still names the baseline as the robust predictor of whether coordination helps. On this text, that conclusion does not follow.

The 45% threshold also has an unaddressed statistical artifact. The same noisy single-agent score is both the predictor and the baseline the MAS gain is measured against, and the comparison uses the best of four MAS variants. With n = 20 per cell and ±20-point intervals (Table 16), regression to the mean alone produces the reported pattern. A low SAS draw makes "some MAS beats it" likely, and a high draw makes it unlikely, even with no true saturation effect. The 15-of-16 check is tested against a 50% chance rate, which ignores this. It also appears to use benchmarks included in the fitted regression. A ceiling effect, where a 75% baseline has less room to grow, is a second non-coordination explanation. A split-half control, estimating P_SA on one half of the instances and the gain on the other, would separate these. It was not run.

Generalization claims outrun the held-out evidence. The abstract says the framework "shows consistent relative architecture preferences on unseen frontier models." In Table 8, however, the equation gives identical predictions for the three held-out models and picks Hybrid as best for each. Hybrid was never best (0.39 to 0.44 against 0.48 to 0.50 for the actual winners), so architecture selection went 0 for 3. The "MAS-only MAE 0.061" hides that the predictions do not vary by model. The 87% within-domain selection accuracy is compared with random and capability-only baselines, but not with the obvious baseline of picking each benchmark's usual winner. Given the strong benchmark dependence, that baseline may account for much of the 87%. The stated Kendall τ of 0.89 for "architectural rankings stable across domains" sits uneasily with the domain-dependent winners reported in Section 4.2. If it was computed on the architecture-level constants, it is stable by construction.

Several numbers do not reconcile:
- Hybrid on WorkBench is -1.2% relative to SAS (about 0.62) in Section 4.2 but "0.21 on workbench" in the overhead discussion.
- Decentralized on PlanCraft is 0.332 in Section 4.2 but "0.282" in the redundancy paragraph; 0.282 is Centralized's number.
- The Figure 4 caption says Anthropic low-orchestrator, high-sub-agent teams (0.42) beat the homogeneous strong team (0.32) by 31%. Table 12 puts the same configuration at 0.42 with a delta of -0.01, implying a homogeneous baseline of 0.43.
- The "95% CI" of -58.7% to +77.2% for the mean MAS change is the spread of configurations, not an interval for the mean.

Several constructs are under-specified. "Trace-level error amplification" (the 17.2x headline) is "estimated from execution-trace token analysis" with no procedure. Information gain is described as posterior variance reduction of a binary success variable yet reported in bits (0.8 to 2.1). Who predicts the success probabilities is not stated. BERTScore below 0.3 is used to detect "semantic opposition," but negated statements typically stay lexically similar. None of these invalidate the descriptive results. They do mean the mechanism story (verification bottlenecks contain errors, information flow explains Finance) rests on metrics a reader cannot audit from the text. The regression itself finds the error-amplification terms non-significant.

What remains supported: on six interactive benchmarks, multi-agent topologies at their own natural token spend help substantially on decomposable research tasks, and hurt substantially on short sequential planning and on tasks where single agents already do well. Communication-free parallel agents are worse than one agent. Coordination multiplies turns and token cost several-fold. A static intelligence index transfers poorly across vendors for agent work. The claims that capability saturation is statistically robust, that coordination metrics add predictive power, and that the framework transfers to unseen models are not established by this text.

## Relevance to us

This paper supports our instinct to establish a strong single-agent baseline before building an agent team (B01). It also shows how easily "compute-matched" slides into matched caps with unmatched spend. Budget-fair comparisons in our own harness should log tokens and turns actually consumed, and should include a single agent given the team's spend, not just the team's cap. That matches what 2604.02460 found under thinking-token caps.

For the "complex vs deliberately simple agents" tension, the decomposability finding is the part worth carrying. Parallel literature or data checks look like Finance Agent. A tightly sequential derivation looks like PlanCraft, where decomposition only added overhead. That is a hypothesis to test task class by task class, not a result for physics, which is absent here. I would not adopt "check whether the single agent exceeds 45%" as a screening rule on this evidence. The threshold is confounded with regression to the mean and ceiling effects, and its supporting interaction is not robust.

On verification (B12, B13), the claim that orchestrator cross-checking contains errors rests on same-model LLM checking and an unaudited trace metric that the paper's own regression does not find significant. It is weak support for putting an LLM orchestrator-verifier in the loop as a reliability mechanism. The benchmarks where external checks exist (tests on SWE-bench, environment success on PlanCraft) show no multi-agent gain. The question we care about, whether an external verifier changes the single-versus-team picture, is not addressed.
