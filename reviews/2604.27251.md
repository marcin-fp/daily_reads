Citation: Tan, X., Valentino, M., Akhter, M. E., Zhou, Y., Liakata, M., and Aletras, N. Compliance vs. Sensibility: On the Reasoning Controllability in Large Language Models. Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2026.

## What this paper is about

When a large language model is asked to reason step by step, it normally chooses on its own whether to reason deductively, inductively, or abductively — the user never explicitly picks. This paper asks what happens when a user does pick, and picks wrong: if you explicitly instruct a model to use, say, inductive reasoning on a problem that actually calls for deduction, does the model obey the (bad) instruction, or does it quietly do what the problem actually needs? The authors call this tension a "reasoning conflict" between instruction compliance and logical sensibility, and run the first systematic study of it across open and proprietary LLMs, four benchmarks each isolating one reasoning type, and several converging methods (behavioral scoring, confidence estimation, activation probing, representation-similarity analysis, and activation steering). Their central finding is that models overwhelmingly favor being sensible over being compliant, that this preference for sensibility is also what predicts getting the right answer, and that instructed reasoning types remain almost perfectly detectable inside a model's internal activations even when its actual behavior ignores them — a gap between "the model knows what was asked" and "the model does what was asked" that the authors show can be partially, and unevenly, closed with a lightweight activation-steering intervention.

## Extended summary

The setup formalizes a question q with a known task-appropriate reasoning type t (deductive, inductive, or abductive), paired with an explicit instruction gt′ that mandates a possibly different type t′. A model is "compliant" (C) if its generated chain-of-thought's inferred reasoning type matches the instructed t′, and "sensible" (S) if it matches the task-appropriate t instead; every response is bucketed into one of four categories (sensible-and-compliant, sensible-but-not-compliant, compliant-but-not-sensible, or neither). Reasoning type is inferred automatically from generated text via an LLM-as-judge (GPT-5.1, or GEMINI3-FLASH when judging GPT-5.1 itself), validated against a proper human gold standard: two independent human annotators label a balanced 360-response subset spanning three model families and three reasoning types, with a third annotator resolving disagreements, yielding Cohen's κ of 0.84 (GPT-5.1) and 0.83 (GEMINI3-FLASH) against the human gold label, with full confusion matrices reported. The authors explicitly identify and report a specific judge vulnerability — sensitivity to "lexical mimicking," where a model's chain-of-thought can echo an instructed reasoning type's characteristic vocabulary without performing the corresponding logical operation — and note that giving the judge explicit type definitions and multiple-choice options, rather than an open zero-shot query, actually reduced agreement with humans.

Experiments cover two frontier proprietary models (GPT-5.1, GEMINI3-FLASH) and three open-weight families (OLMO3, QWEN3, LLAMA3) at multiple scales (7-8B and 32-70B) and configurations (instruct vs. thinking mode), evaluated on four datasets each targeting one reasoning type in relative isolation: FOLIO (deduction, first-order logic premises and claims), SPR (induction, predicting the next element of a numerical sequence), αNLI (abduction, selecting the most plausible explanation connecting a story's beginning and end), and RECV (a mix of deduction and abduction drawn from real-world Wikipedia/Twitter claim verification, with human-annotated reasoning types per question). Test sets are modest in size (100 questions for αNLI and SPR, 99 for RECV, FOLIO's full validation split used since it has no separate test split), with three random seeds per model/dataset/instruction combination.

Aggregated across all models, datasets, instructed types, and seeds, models are sensible-but-non-compliant in 43.5% of cases versus compliant-but-non-sensible in only 18.6%, with responses that are neither sensible nor compliant rare (5.7%). Sensibility rises with model scale in most but not all cases (GPT-5.1 highest at 88.1%; LLAMA3.1-8B-IT lowest at 58.2%; GEMINI3-FLASH notably underperforms its parameter class, trailing smaller and mid-sized models), while compliance is described as largely family-dependent and scale-agnostic: LLAMA3.1-8B-IT is far more compliant (65.1%) than the much larger LLAMA3.3-70B-IT, an inverse-scaling pattern the authors attribute to post-training recipe rather than parameter count, while QWEN3 and OLMO3 show the more conventional pattern of larger variants being more compliant than their smaller siblings. Enabling built-in "thinking" mode in QWEN and OLMO models tends to raise sensibility and lower compliance simultaneously. Sensible responses are also the accuracy-determining factor: regardless of whether the sensible response happened to be compliant, sensible responses substantially outperform compliant-but-non-sensible ones, most sharply on FOLIO (a 20-40 percentage-point accuracy gap) and least on RECV (all category differences under 20 points); GEMINI3-FLASH is a partial exception, showing relatively uniform accuracy across categories except a clear drop on FOLIO's compliant-but-non-sensible cohort, which the authors interpret as evidence that its chain-of-thought may function more as post-hoc justification than as functional reasoning.

Beyond generated text, the paper measures internal confidence via p(True) (asking the model, in the same conversation, whether its own answer is true or false, and reading off the probability of "True"), marginalizing correctness by comparing compliant against non-compliant responses separately within correct and incorrect answer cohorts across six open-weight instruction models. Compliant responses show significantly higher confidence than non-compliant ones in both correctness cohorts (p < 0.05 in both), but the effect size is small (Cohen's d = 0.09 for correct, 0.12 for incorrect answers), which the authors read as a "subtle frictional drag" rather than a catastrophic confidence collapse, still indicating the model retains some internal sensitivity to the instruction it is not following.

Mechanistic analysis applies linear probing to residual-stream activations (mean-pooled per layer over the question span) of the three open-weight families, training separate probes to predict the instructed reasoning type, the judge-inferred (actual) reasoning type, and binary compliance. The instructed type is almost perfectly linearly decodable across all three families, including in non-compliant responses, while compliance itself is a weaker, less linearly accessible property, with decoding accuracy typically peaking in middle-to-late layers; the gap between instruction-decodability and judge-inferred-type-decodability in non-compliant cases is largest for LLaMA, smaller for OLMo, and smallest for Qwen (whose probes are strong on both signals). A complementary representation-similarity analysis (linear CKA, corrected against a permutation baseline over question, instruction, and full-prompt token spans) finds LLaMA shows the largest instruction-driven geometric differences (particularly for deduction vs. the other two types), OLMo mainly distinguishes deduction, and Qwen retains high alignment overall except for a transient dip at a specific block (16) that recovers by later layers; the authors are explicit that neither probing decodability nor CKA similarity, alone or together, establishes a causal account of compliance or chain-of-thought faithfulness, since a condition-specific offset removed by CKA centering can coexist with near-perfect instruction decodability, and stable geometry (as in LLaMA) can still coexist with steering-manipulable behavioral compliance.

Finally, the paper applies Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) — computing a steering vector as the mean difference in residual-stream activations between contrastive compliant/non-compliant response pairs (matched for final-answer correctness and instructed type to isolate the compliance signal specifically) — to layers 14-17 of OLMO3-7B-IT (chosen as the least compliant model tested) and, in the appendix, LLAMA3.1-8B-IT. Increasing the positive steering multiplier substantially raises compliance (up to 29% in the paper's headline framing), most clearly overriding the model's natural abductive inclination on αNLI when instructed toward induction or deduction instead; a human evaluation on a subset of these steered outputs (compliance rate 24.2% at multiplier -1.0, 81.8% at multiplier 0, 87.9% at multiplier +1.0) independently confirms the LLM judge's read on the steering effect. However, positive steering's effect on task accuracy is mixed and instruction-dependent: for abductive instructions accuracy drops as the multiplier increases, attributed to a "task-neglect" side effect where the model becomes so focused on the steered reasoning style that it fails to follow output-formatting constraints; for inductive and deductive instructions accuracy follows a non-monotonic dip-then-rise pattern. Negative steering (pushing away from the instructed type, toward the model's natural sensible tendency) consistently improves accuracy across all instruction types and increases the rate at which the model simply answers directly rather than working through a full chain of thought. FOLIO shows a broadly similar pattern to αNLI, with deductive-instruction compliance rising substantially and accuracy across all instructed types improving with larger positive multipliers on that specific dataset.

## Learnings

When explicitly instructed to use a reasoning schema that does not fit the task, models across families default to task-appropriate ("sensible") reasoning far more often (43.5% of cases) than they comply with the mismatched instruction (18.6%), and this preference for sensibility is also what predicts getting the right final answer. This is a concrete, quantified demonstration that, for this specific kind of conflicting-schema instruction, a model's "disobedience" functions as a reliability feature rather than a pure compliance defect, which reframes what "instruction following" should even mean when the instruction itself is logically inappropriate for the task.

A model's chain-of-thought can perform sophisticated "lexical mirroring": echoing an instructed reasoning type's characteristic vocabulary (such as "most probable" and "simplest hypothesis" for abduction) while its actual underlying inference pattern is a different type entirely, as the paper's case study shows concretely for a model that narrates abductive-sounding language while its answer is actually reached through deductive entailment from an explicit premise. This is a specific, reusable pattern to check for whenever grading chain-of-thought faithfulness or reasoning-type compliance from surface text alone, since text-level self-description can diverge sharply from the operation the model is actually performing, and the paper's own judge had to be specifically hardened against exactly this vulnerability.

Instructed information can be almost perfectly linearly decodable from a model's internal activations even in precisely the cases where the model's final behavior does not comply with that instruction, showing that a failure to follow an instruction is not, in general, a failure to represent or notice the instruction internally. The size of the gap between how decodable the instructed type is versus how decodable the model's actual behavioral output type is, measured from the same layer, is itself a family-dependent signature (largest for LLaMA, smallest for Qwen) worth treating as a standard mechanistic diagnostic for studying instruction-following gaps generally, not only for reasoning-type instructions specifically.

## Verification

This paper's own evaluation infrastructure is a careful instance of verification design worth describing directly. The automated reasoning-type classifier is validated against a properly constructed human gold standard: a balanced 360-response subset, independently labeled by two human annotators with a third reconciling disagreements into a gold label, reporting Cohen's κ of 0.83-0.84 for the two LLM judges used, well above conventional thresholds for substantial agreement, together with full confusion matrices (Appendix F) showing exactly where each judge tends to err (GEMINI3-FLASH conflates abduction with induction most often; GPT-5.1 occasionally misclassifies deduction as an unrelated category). The paper explicitly identifies and reports a specific judge failure mode it had to guard against — sensitivity to lexical mimicking, where surface vocabulary matching the instructed type can fool a naive judge — and reports, as a useful negative result, that an alternative judge-prompting strategy giving explicit type definitions and options actually reduced agreement with humans rather than improving it. The steering intervention is independently validated with a second layer of human evaluation beyond the same automated judge used to build the steering vectors and score their effect (Table 5, confirming compliance rises with steering multiplier on a human-annotated subset of steered outputs), avoiding a self-confirming loop where the same judge both trains and grades the intervention. The paper is also explicitly careful, in its mechanistic-analysis discussion, to deny that any of its converging signals (linear probing, CKA) establishes a causal account of compliance or chain-of-thought faithfulness on their own, treating decodability and behavioral compliance as related but logically separate properties that must each be measured rather than inferred from the other — a disciplined, self-limiting epistemic stance.

## Field context

The paper extends recent work documenting that LLMs struggle with fundamental logical reasoning paradigms (prior work finding models frequently fail to identify the correct reasoning direction, particularly in abductive and mixed-form tasks; a related finding that similar gaps persist even in models explicitly trained for extended reasoning such as DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3; and the RECV dataset's own source study finding frontier LLMs fail on abductive reasoning in real-world claim verification, which this paper reuses directly) by asking a different, controllability-oriented question those works do not address: not whether a model can perform a given reasoning type correctly, but whether it obeys or resists an explicit instruction to use the wrong one, and what that reveals about the underlying mechanism. It engages directly with the chain-of-thought faithfulness literature (studies of unfaithfulness induced by biasing cues, and studies perturbing chain-of-thought to test causal dependence) but explicitly pivots the framing from a binary faithful/unfaithful judgment toward "reasoning compliance" as a distinct, graded axis. On the mechanistic-interpretability side, it builds on prior findings that instruction-following and task-specific logic localize in middle-to-late residual-stream activations, and extends recent activation-steering work (Contrastive Activation Addition, and a cited prior use of Conditional Activation Steering to mitigate content effects in reasoning) from typically binary steering targets to the more abstract target of reasoning-type compliance specifically. None of these specific precedents (the fundamental-reasoning-paradigm evaluation studies, the RECV claim-verification dataset, the cited chain-of-thought faithfulness and activation-steering papers) are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first paper in the corpus combining behavioral compliance measurement, linear probing, representation-similarity analysis, and activation steering as converging methods aimed at one shared research question, rather than using any single one of these methods in isolation.

## Critical discussion

The paper's own results contain a real tension that its framing does not fully foreground. The abstract and Section 5.4's title present steering as "enhancing compliance," with the headline figure being a 29% increase in instruction-following. But the same section's own accuracy results show that positive steering toward compliance actively hurts task accuracy for abductive instructions (via a documented "task-neglect" side effect) and only helps accuracy non-monotonically for inductive and deductive instructions, while negative steering — pushing the model away from the instructed type and toward its natural, task-appropriate tendency — "consistently improves accuracy across all instruction types." Read together with the paper's own headline behavioral finding that sensibility, not compliance, is what predicts correct answers, this suggests that from a pure task-performance standpoint, the more genuinely useful direction to steer a model is toward sensibility, not toward compliance with an arbitrary or wrong instruction — a nuance the paper's title and abstract frame as "a path toward improved controllability" without stating plainly that its own data shows compliance and accuracy pulling in opposite directions on the tasks tested.

Several of the paper's benchmarks are quite small (100 test questions for αNLI and SPR, 99 for RECV), a reasonable and disclosed trade-off given how expensive the full experimental design is (multiple models, datasets, instructed types, and random seeds, layered with activation-level probing and CKA analysis), but the paper does not report confidence intervals or variance estimates on its main aggregated behavioral percentages (the 43.5%/18.6%/5.7% category breakdown in Figure 2, or the per-model sensibility and compliance rates in Figure 3), even though it does report both statistical significance and effect size specifically for the confidence-score comparison (Table 2). This is an inconsistency in how rigorously different results within the same paper are quantified, and it leaves the precision of the paper's most central, most-cited numbers less clearly bounded than its secondary confidence-estimation result.

The steering experiments are conducted on a narrow slice of the tested model matrix — CAA is applied to OLMO3-7B-IT (chosen specifically as the least compliant model) and, in the appendix, LLAMA3.1-8B-IT, but not to any of the larger 32B/70B open-weight models or to the frontier proprietary models, which is an expected limitation of white-box activation steering (it requires model weights the proprietary models don't expose) but does mean the "steering works" claim is demonstrated on two small open-weight models specifically, without evidence in this paper of whether the same intervention scales or generalizes to larger models within the same families already shown to behave differently (for instance, LLAMA3.3-70B-IT's markedly lower compliance than its 8B sibling, which the steering experiments do not test directly).

What remains supported after these caveats: the central behavioral finding (models default to sensibility over compliance, and sensibility tracks accuracy) rests on a broad and carefully validated evaluation across five model families and four purpose-built datasets, with an unusually well-documented and human-verified automated judge; the mechanistic claim that instructed information remains decodable even in non-compliant responses is directly supported by layer-wise probing results consistent across all three open-weight families tested; and the steering results, while showing a real and human-verified compliance effect, are honestly reported alongside their accuracy trade-offs and the anti-steering finding, rather than presented as an unqualified improvement.

## Relevance to us

This paper connects directly to our verification and reasoning-faithfulness interests. The central finding — that models given a task-inappropriate reasoning instruction default to task-appropriate reasoning most of the time, and that this substitution is exactly what correlates with getting the right answer — is directly relevant to any physics-reasoning evaluation we build that specifies a particular solution method (for instance, "solve via dimensional analysis" or "apply perturbation theory") and needs to know whether a model faithfully follows the specified method or quietly substitutes its own judgment; this paper's evidence suggests a model may substitute a more appropriate method even when explicitly told not to, which is worth checking directly with the same sensible/compliant categorization scheme whenever we evaluate method-specificity rather than only final-answer correctness. The "lexical mirroring" case study is a concrete, reusable failure pattern for our own chain-of-thought faithfulness audits: a model's stated rationale can use the vocabulary of a requested approach while its actual working follows a different approach entirely, meaning any text-only faithfulness check that pattern-matches for the right vocabulary rather than verifying the actual logical structure of the steps can be fooled in exactly the way this paper's own judge initially was. The paper's disciplined separation of decodability (is information present in the representations) from causal behavioral control (does an intervention actually change output, and does the information present match the behavior observed) is a directly transferable methodological template for any interpretability work we do on our own physics-reasoning models, particularly its explicit refusal to treat probing accuracy or representational similarity alone as evidence of causal mechanism.
