## What this paper is about

Quadratic attention's ever-growing KV cache is a well-known memory/speed bottleneck for LLMs, and one popular fix is "linearizing" an existing pretrained model — retrofitting it with linear attention via post-training, sometimes with as little as 40M tokens (LoLCATs). This paper points out that essentially every linearization paper compares itself only to sink-free Sliding Window Attention (SWA), which is known to fail catastrophically once the window slides past the first few tokens — an unfair comparison, since a simple fix (also attending to a handful of fixed "sink" tokens) is already known to prevent that failure. Once SWA-with-sinks is used as the baseline instead, the authors find it matches or beats post-trained linear attention models on standard benchmarks, and dramatically outperforms them on long-context tasks — all with zero post-training, higher speed, and lower memory.

## Extended summary

**The missing comparison.** SWA restricts attention to the previous w tokens; because each layer's effective receptive field grows with depth, an l-layer stack still sees roughly lw tokens back, similar to a CNN's growing receptive field. Sink-free SWA degrades severely once the window moves past the first few tokens (LLMs are known to dump disproportionate attention onto early tokens regardless of their semantic content). Attending additionally to a small fixed number of "sink" tokens (s=4, as established in prior streaming-LLM work) fixes this collapse. The paper's core observation is that linear-attention retrofitting papers (SUPRA, Hedgehog, LoLCATs, Liger-GLA, MOHAWK, Mamba-in-Llama, DiJiang, ARWKV, Llamba, QLinAtt, QRWKV6/7) compare against sink-free SWA or don't compare against SWA at all, never against the sink-augmented version that is already known to be a much stronger training-free baseline.

**Short-context knowledge/reasoning.** Across 11 base models (1.3B-70B parameters: Phi-1.5, Mistral-7B, Llama 2/3 family, Qwen2.5 family, QwQ-32B) evaluated on MMLU-5shot, ARC-C/E, HellaSwag, PIQA, and WinoGrande, training-free SWA(64,4) achieves the best average downstream performance in 9 of 11 cases, losing only marginally to LoLCATs on Phi-1.5 (62.5 vs. 62.4) and to QRWKV6 on Qwen2.5-32B (77.3 vs. 76.6). SWA recovers 93.2% of teacher MMLU performance and 99.0% of the 6-benchmark average with zero post-training tokens, essentially matching LoLCATs (97.5% average recovery, 40M tokens) and QRWKV6 (99.1%, 350-700M tokens). A fully self-run, matched-condition follow-up (Appendix A.2: same base models — Qwen3-8B, Phi4-mini-reasoning, Phi4-reasoning-plus — same 0.1B-token post-training budget, three linear variants GLA/Gated DeltaNet/QRWKV6 all trained by the authors) shows an even larger gap: linear variants collapse to 45.7-56.1% average score versus teacher, while SWA stays at 60.9-73.9%, essentially matching the teacher.

**Long-context.** On Single Needle-in-a-Haystack up to 4K context (Llama 3.1 8B base), SWA matches or beats LoLCATs and Liger-GLA at every window size and context length tested, with the gap widening sharply as context grows: at 4K, SWA recovers 17.2-23% of full-attention accuracy versus at most 5.8% (LoLCATs) and 0.8% (Liger-GLA). On BABILong (5-task average, same base model, window=256), SWA is slightly behind LoLCATs at 0K/1K context (55% vs. 56%, 20% vs. 22%) but clearly ahead at 2K/4K (19% vs. 10%, 15% vs. 3%) — SWA recovers 25% of baseline performance at 4K versus LoLCATs' 5%.

**Speed and memory.** On a synthetic 4-layer transformer benchmark (embedding size 1024, 16 heads) run on a single GPU, full attention's decode throughput degrades past 1K context while SWA, linear attention, and LoLCATs all stay roughly flat; SWA is the fastest of all methods at every context length tested (up to 256K), and SWA at window-size 64 has the lowest memory footprint of any method, though SWA at window-size 512 uses more memory than linear attention alone.

**Self-stated limitations.** The paper explicitly excludes hybrid architectures that mix full-attention layers with SWA or linear attention, tests only training-free SWA (not post-trained SWA, which prior work shows can improve further), and does not extend the analysis to extremely large models or agentic/complex downstream tasks; it also notes that video-diffusion literature already shows training-free SWA to be a strong baseline there too (citing Sliding Tile Attention recovering 97% of baseline VBench score at 3.5x speed).

## Learnings

The paper's central methodological point — that an entire subfield's comparison baseline (sink-free SWA) was already known by a separate line of work to fail catastrophically, and no one had checked the obvious stronger training-free alternative (sink-augmented SWA) before publishing post-training methods that "beat" it — is a specific, well-documented instance of a missing-baseline problem worth generalizing: when a fast-moving subfield's papers converge on comparing against the same baseline configuration, it is worth checking whether that configuration is actually the strongest cheap alternative before crediting a more complex method with an improvement over it.

The corroborating appendix experiment (Table 5), where the authors post-train their own linear-attention variants on the same base models and token budget used for the SWA comparison, is a good practical pattern for defusing the natural objection to a critical/negative result built substantially on other papers' self-reported numbers (Table 2): running at least one fully matched, self-controlled version of the comparison and finding the same qualitative pattern strengthens the paper's claim considerably beyond what the literature-sourced table alone would support.

## Verification

Central and reasonably strong for the paper's most consequential claim: the primary short-context comparison (Table 2) sources most non-SWA numbers from each linear-attention method's own original publication (differing base models, post-training token budgets, and training stages across rows), while the SWA and teacher numbers are computed directly by the authors — a genuine mixed-provenance limitation for a head-to-head comparison table. This is meaningfully mitigated by Appendix A.2's fully self-run, matched-condition comparison (same three base models, same 0.1B-token budget, three linear variants and SWA all trained/evaluated by the authors under identical conditions), which reproduces the same qualitative pattern with an even larger gap — a genuine, if partial, independent check on the main table's literature-sourced conclusions. The long-context and speed/memory results are fully self-run and matched across methods.

## Field context

This paper is a direct, adversarial-style comparison paper against an active subfield: LLM linearization / attention-retrofitting methods (Hedgehog, LoLCATs, SUPRA, Liger-GLA, MOHAWK, Mamba-in-Llama, DiJiang, ARWKV, Llamba, QLinAtt, the QRWKV family), and against the broader linear-attention literature (Katharopoulos et al.'s original linear attention, Mamba/state-space models, RWKV). It builds directly on prior findings that (a) LLMs disproportionately attend to early tokens regardless of semantics (attention sinks; Xiao et al. 2024) and (b) sink-augmented SWA already recovers most of a model's performance and improves long-term memorization (Cabannes et al. 2026) — the paper's contribution is combining these two established facts into a direct, previously-missing comparison against the linearization literature specifically. No other paper in this corpus addresses efficient-attention or KV-cache-reduction methods directly, so this is a novel connection point rather than a corroborating or conflicting one within the corpus so far.

## Critical discussion

The paper's core comparative claim is well-supported where it matters most: the long-context results (Needle-in-a-Haystack, BABILong) and the speed/memory measurements are fully self-run under matched conditions across all compared methods, and the appendix's fully controlled short-context re-run (Table 5) corroborates the literature-sourced main table's conclusion with an even larger effect size, which is a meaningful form of internal replication for a paper whose headline table otherwise mixes self-run and other-papers'-self-reported numbers.

Two things temper how far the recommendation ("we strongly recommend practitioners to use SWA with attention sinks") should be read as settled. First, the paper explicitly excludes hybrid architectures that interleave full-attention layers with SWA or linear-attention layers — a design point widely used in practice precisely to combine efficiency with better long-range recall — so the comparison is between two pure-strategy alternatives rather than against the mixed strategies that many deployed efficient-attention systems actually use, even though the paper's practical recommendation is stated without this qualification in the main text (the exclusion appears only in the Limitations section) (`narrow-evaluation`). Second, the downstream long-context accuracy evaluation (S-NIAH, BABILong) stops at 4K context, while the paper's own speed/memory argument for why efficient attention matters at all is made at context lengths up to 256K — exactly the regime where the memory and speed savings from either SWA or linear attention would be most consequential in practice remains untested for downstream task accuracy, so the paper demonstrates SWA's long-context advantage only within a fairly short "long-context" range relative to its own motivating use case (`narrow-evaluation`). Separately, the primary Table 1/2 comparison's reliance on literature-reported numbers for the linear-attention side (`missing-control`) is a real methodological gap on its own, even though the appendix's matched re-run substantially mitigates the concern for the specific models it covers (Qwen3-8B, Phi4-mini-reasoning, Phi4-reasoning-plus) rather than for the full 11-model main table.

What remains well supported after these caveats: sink-augmented SWA is a materially stronger, zero-training-cost baseline than the sink-free SWA that linearization papers have compared against, and the paper's own fully matched experiments (long-context, speed/memory, and the Appendix A.2 short-context re-run) independently confirm that current post-training linearization methods do not clearly beat this stronger baseline at comparable or greater training cost. The narrower, appropriately scoped conclusion — that post-training linearization, as currently practiced, has not been shown to beat a simple, well-known training-free alternative — is well earned; the broader claim that linear attention itself is inferior to sliding-window attention in every deployment configuration (including hybrids, from-scratch training, and true long-context regimes beyond 4K) is not directly tested here and the paper is explicit that this remains for future work.
