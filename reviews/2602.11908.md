Citation: Goren, S., Galil, I., and El-Yaniv, R. When Should LLMs Be Less Specific? Selective Abstraction for Reliable Long-Form Text Generation. arXiv preprint arXiv:2602.11908, 2026.

## What this paper is about

Uncertainty-aware LLMs are often built to abstain entirely when they are not confident enough in an answer, which works reasonably well for short factual questions but is a poor fit for long-form generation (a biography, an explanation spanning many sentences), where a single response typically bundles many separate factual claims of varying certainty. Discarding the whole response, or even a whole sentence, because of one uncertain detail throws away everything else that was reliable. This paper proposes Selective Abstraction: instead of an all-or-nothing accept/abstain decision, a model can retreat from an uncertain specific claim to a less specific but more defensible version of the same claim (e.g., "born in Chicago" → "born in Illinois" → "born in the Midwestern US" → "born in the US"), trading away detail only where and to the extent needed to gain reliability. The paper formalizes this framework, instantiates it at the level of individual atomic claims within a response, builds an evaluation pipeline that can actually measure both the resulting factual risk and how much information was preserved, and shows across six open-source LLMs and two long-form factuality benchmarks that this claim-level approach beats simpler alternatives (deleting uncertain claims outright, or two prompting-based baselines) at trading reliability for detail.

## Extended summary

Selective Abstraction is first defined generally: given a model M's original response T to a prompt p, a selective abstraction procedure produces a modified response T' that should have higher accuracy than T, at the possible cost of conveying less information (formalized via a text information measure I(·) introduced later), optionally controlled by a confidence function κ and a threshold θ. The paper's concrete instantiation, atom-wise selective abstraction, operates in four stages. First, the model generates an initial response. Second, the response is decomposed (via few-shot prompting of the same generating model) into atomic claims — short, self-contained, single-fact statements, following established practice from factuality-evaluation and summarization literature. Third, for each atom the model elicits a verbalized confidence score; for every atom below the chosen threshold θ, the model is prompted to produce a sequence of progressively less specific abstractions (ending, if needed, in full abstention), each of which also receives a confidence score conditioned on the original atom's confidence; the most specific abstraction in that sequence whose confidence clears θ is selected (or full abstention, if none does). Fourth, the selected atoms/abstractions are reconstructed by the model into a single coherent response.

Evaluating this framework requires operationalizing both risk and the amount of information retained, which the paper does concretely for the factual-generation setting it studies (while noting the framework itself is not restricted to factual generation). Risk is defined via an agentic fact-checking pipeline: each atomic claim (or abstraction) is checked against Wikipedia by an LLM-driven search agent (using the free Wikipedia API and gpt-oss-120b as the agent backbone, rather than a paid search API), labeled correct or incorrect, and risk is the fraction of incorrect atoms in the final response. This pipeline is itself validated against 102 manually-labeled claims sampled across models, confidence levels, and correctness outcomes, reporting an F1 of 0.93 against human judgment. Information is defined through an entity-narrowing argument: a claim like "Alan Turing was born in London" is treated as a subject-independent predicate ("born in London") that narrows a reference set of entities (all people) down to a smaller compatible subset (people born in London); the claim's information content is the relative reduction in entropy this narrowing induces, estimated in practice by querying entity-set sizes from Wikidata via an existing natural-language-to-SPARQL agent (SPINACH). Coverage is then defined as the fraction of the original response's total information retained after abstraction. Sweeping the confidence threshold θ traces a risk-coverage (RC) curve, summarized by the area under it (AURC); lower AURC means a better overall trade-off.

Experiments compare atom-wise selective abstraction against three baselines — selective redaction (delete low-confidence atoms outright, with no abstraction), inline abstraction (a single prompt asking the model to adjust granularity according to its own uncertainty during generation, adapted from prior QA-granularity work), and self-revision (generate normally, then separately prompt the model to replace or remove uncertain claims) — on six open-source models (gpt-oss-120b/20b, Qwen3-235B/30B-Thinking, Qwen3-30B-Instruct, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) and two long-form factuality benchmarks (FactScore biographies; LongFact-Objects open-ended questions across 38 subject areas), using fixed subsets of 36 and 76 prompts respectively. Because Inline and Self-Revision are single-shot prompting baselines that cannot cheaply be swept across a full range of θ, they are evaluated only at a single operating point per model/dataset and compared via risk-at-matched-coverage rather than full AURC; a follow-up experiment that does inject explicit θ values into these two baselines' prompts (Appendix G.1) found this did not produce a meaningful risk-coverage trade-off. Against selective redaction, atom-wise SA reduces AURC on every tested model and both datasets, by an average of 17.43% (FactScore) and 13.77% (LongFact), with the largest single improvement (27.73% on FactScore) for gpt-oss-120b. Against Inline and Self-Revision, atom-wise SA achieves lower risk at matched coverage in nearly every model/dataset combination, by an average of roughly 10 percentage points, with one exception (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct vs. Inline on FactScore, where the risk gap is slightly negative, i.e., Inline achieves marginally lower risk at that specific matched coverage point).

A further contribution addresses deployment: since practical use requires fixing one threshold rather than presenting a full curve, the paper proposes a calibration procedure, grounded in split conformal prediction, that takes a target risk level α and a calibration set of prompts and returns a threshold guaranteed (under an exchangeability assumption, with a formal proof given in the appendix) to keep true risk within α ± ε with probability at least 1 − δ. Empirically, repeating this calibration 100 times per model/dataset combination (600 runs per dataset in total, using 30% of each benchmark as calibration data) shows the resulting empirical risk tracks the target risk closely across a range of target values, with visible but expected variability given the modest calibration-set sizes involved. A separate appendix compares the verbalized confidence score used throughout the main experiments against several logprob-based scores and against a binary "P(True)" self-judgment score, using both AURC and an independent ranking-quality metric (AUROC, i.e., how well a score separates correct from incorrect atoms); verbalized confidence wins on both, with the weaker performance of P(True) traced specifically to near-random ranking quality at the atom level, causing its apparent gains to saturate quickly rather than extend across the coverage range.

## Learnings

Selective prediction's binary "answer or abstain" framing generalizes cleanly to a continuous "how much detail to include" framing for long-form generation, by decomposing a response into atomic claims and giving each one its own ladder of progressively less specific rewordings ending in full abstention — this lets a single response mix fully-specific, partially-abstracted, and abstained content depending on per-claim confidence, rather than forcing one all-or-nothing decision for an entire multi-sentence output.

Treating a claim as a predicate that narrows a reference set of entities, and measuring its information content as the relative entropy reduction this narrowing induces (estimated via a knowledge base's entity counts), gives a genuinely comparable informativeness score across claims about entirely different topics and granularities, without requiring that the claims be alternative phrasings of the same underlying question — a specific, reusable improvement over prior informativeness measures defined only relative to a fixed set of alternative-granularity answers to one question.

When choosing among candidate confidence-scoring functions for a selective mechanism, ranking quality (how well the score separates correct from incorrect content, measured via AUROC) is the property that most directly predicts whether the resulting system will spend its information budget wisely, more so than whether the score "looks reasonable" in isolation — this paper's own comparison shows a score with near-random ranking (P(True)) produces a selective mechanism whose gains saturate quickly and do not extend across the coverage range, even though the same score is not obviously broken by other measures.

## Verification

This paper is unusually disciplined about verifying its own evaluation pipeline rather than assuming its components are trustworthy. The fact-checking agent that labels ground-truth correctness (an agentic Wikipedia-search pipeline, independent of whichever model generated the text being checked) is itself validated against 102 manually-labeled claims sampled across models, confidence levels, and correctness outcomes, reporting a concrete F1 of 0.93 against human judgment — a genuine accuracy check on the paper's own oracle rather than an assumed-reliable LLM judge. The choice of confidence-scoring function is similarly not simply asserted: verbalized confidence is compared against a family of logprob-based scores and a binary self-judgment score using both the paper's own end-to-end metric (AURC) and an independent ranking-quality metric (AUROC), with the losing alternatives' weaknesses traced to a specific, measurable cause (poor ranking) rather than left unexplained. What is comparatively under-verified is the other half of the evaluation pipeline: the entity-count estimates underlying the information/coverage metric depend on a separate natural-language-to-SPARQL agent (SPINACH) querying Wikidata, and this agent's accuracy is not independently spot-checked anywhere in the paper the way the correctness-labeling agent's is, even though both are equally load-bearing for the reported risk-coverage trade-off — a comparable manual validation (for example, checking a sample of SPARQL-derived entity counts against known reference figures) would extend the same verification discipline already applied to the risk side of the pipeline to its coverage side.

## Field context

The paper builds directly on classical selective prediction and its risk-coverage formalization (El-Yaniv and Wiener, 2010) and its NLP extension as "selective generation" for short outputs (Ren et al., 2023), and is an explicit continuation of the same authors' own prior work on hierarchical selective classification (Goren, Galil, and El-Yaniv, 2025), which let a classifier retreat to a less specific ancestor label under uncertainty rather than abstaining outright; this paper generalizes that same "retreat to less specificity" idea from a fixed label hierarchy in classification to open-ended, claim-level text generation. Its factuality-evaluation pipeline is positioned against FactScore's original static-retrieval, entity-level approach and against SAFE's costlier, rigid, fixed-query-count Google-Search-API pipeline, offering a free, Wikipedia-based, per-claim independently-verified alternative; it also explicitly contrasts its per-claim verification against a more recent OpenAI approach that batches multiple claims into a single verification call to reduce cost, at the acknowledged expense of per-claim focus. On the specificity side, it directly extends and critiques Yona et al.'s (2024) work on multi-granularity question answering, adopting the core intuition that correct-but-less-specific answers deserve credit, while arguing its own claim-level information measure is more broadly applicable because it is defined independently per claim (via the entity-narrowing construction) rather than only relative to a fixed set of alternative-granularity answers to the same question. None of these specific precedents (El-Yaniv and Wiener; the authors' own hierarchical selective classification paper; FactScore; SAFE; Yona et al.) are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first selective-prediction/hallucination-mitigation paper reviewed here.

## Critical discussion

The comparison against Inline and Self-Revision is less complete than the comparison against Redaction, and the paper is transparent about why: because these two baselines are single-shot prompting strategies, sweeping them across a full range of θ was judged infeasible at the paper's evaluation scale, so they are represented as single operating points and compared via risk-at-matched-coverage rather than a full AURC curve, and a follow-up attempt to inject explicit θ values into their prompts (Appendix G.1) is reported not to produce a meaningful trade-off. This honesty is a genuine strength, but it does mean the headline "27.73%" AURC improvement figure in the Abstract is computed only against Redaction, the simplest of the three baselines, while the comparison against the two more sophisticated prompting-based baselines rests on a narrower, single-point form of evidence; a reader relying on the Abstract alone could reasonably assume the strongest reported number characterizes the comparison against all baselines equally, when it in fact characterizes only the comparison against the weakest one.

Similarly, the Results section states "atom-wise SA consistently achieves markedly lower risk at matched coverage" against Inline and Self-Revision, but Table 2's own reported values include one exception: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct on FactScore shows a slightly negative risk gap against Inline (-0.70), meaning Inline achieves marginally lower risk at that specific matched coverage point. This is a minor, isolated exception rather than a pattern, and the paper's own table reports it plainly, but the surrounding "consistently" language does not flag it, echoing a pattern of slightly overstated blanket claims against tables that are themselves fully transparent.

The paper's two central agentic sub-pipelines receive asymmetric verification attention: the correctness-labeling agent (central to the risk axis) is validated against 102 human-labeled examples with a reported F1, while the entity-counting agent (central to the coverage/information axis, via Wikidata and SPINACH) receives no comparable independent accuracy check anywhere in the paper, despite the authors themselves noting that Wikidata "is sparse and does not cover all entities." Since both risk and coverage are equally necessary to interpret every reported AURC number, this asymmetry means one half of the paper's central evaluation metric is verified considerably more carefully than the other.

Finally, the main results (Table 1's AURC figures, and Table 2's risk-gap figures) are reported as single point estimates with no variance, confidence interval, or repeated-run information, in contrast to the threshold-selection experiment in Section 7, which is explicitly repeated 100 times per configuration and reports standard-deviation bands. Given that evaluation sets are modest (36 and 76 prompts), and some reported gaps are fairly small (for instance, a 5.41% AURC improvement on Qwen3-30B-Instruct/LongFact, or a 6.48% improvement on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct/FactScore), it is not possible from the reported numbers alone to judge how much of the smaller margins would survive a formal significance check, even though the paper demonstrates elsewhere (Section 7) that it has the methodological tools to report such variability when it chooses to.

## Relevance to us

This is one of the more directly relevant papers in the corpus to our verification and uncertainty-quantification interests, despite its domain being open-ended biographical and encyclopedic text rather than physics. The central move — treating reliability in long-form output as a per-claim trade-off between specificity and correctness, and letting a system retreat to a less specific but still-true statement rather than either asserting a risky precise claim or discarding it entirely — maps directly onto a scenario we care about: a physics-reasoning system confident about a qualitative conclusion or an order of magnitude but not about a precise numerical value, unit, or citation could apply this same logic to report the qualitative or coarser-grained claim instead of a risky precise number or a blank abstention, which is a materially more useful failure mode than plain non-response. The conformal-calibration-based threshold-selection procedure, with its formal marginal-coverage guarantee, is a directly reusable pattern for turning a per-claim confidence score into a deployable operating point that meets a stated target risk with a stated probability, rather than picking a threshold ad hoc, which is squarely in the spirit of this corpus's general preference for calibrated, checkable claims over an unexamined single confidence number. The paper's own most transparent limitation — that the entire mechanism is bottlenecked by the quality of the underlying confidence-scoring function, and that even the best-performing option (verbalized confidence, AUROC 0.74) is explicitly called "imperfect" — is a caution directly applicable to any confidence-based mechanism we might build on an LLM's self-reported certainty in any domain: the mechanism's usefulness is capped by a measured ranking-quality number, not by an assumption of good self-knowledge, and that number should always be checked rather than presumed.
