Citation: Xu, J., Ju, D., Lane, J., Komeili, M., Smith, E. M., Ung, M., Behrooz, M., Ngan, W., Moritz, R., Sukhbaatar, S., Boureau, Y-L., Weston, J., Shuster, K. Improving Open Language Models by Learning from Organic Interactions. Meta AI, 2023. arXiv:2306.04707.

## What this paper is about

When a conversational AI model is deployed publicly, the real users who talk to it are a far richer and more relevant source of training signal than any crowdsourced dataset, but they are also a messier one: some genuinely try to help the model improve, and others deliberately try to provoke bad or toxic behavior. This paper describes BlenderBot 3x, an update to Meta's BlenderBot 3 chatbot trained on over 350,000 real conversations and 155,000 pieces of user feedback (thumbs up/down) collected from its public deployment, and studies how to learn from that "organic" data, extracting genuine signal about what makes a response good or safe while not simply imitating the adversarial or toxic behavior a meaningful fraction of real users exhibit. The resulting model is both preferred by evaluators over the original BlenderBot 3 and measurably safer in adversarial conversations, and the authors release the de-identified interaction and feedback data publicly for the research community.

## Extended summary

The deployment dataset (collected 2022-08-05 to 2022-11-17, the frozen subset used for experiments) contains over 5.2 million utterances across 227,637 conversations, with 19% of conversations containing at least one thumbs up/down reaction. The authors split conversations into "standard" versus "adversarial" groups based on what fraction of messages the deployed safety classifier flagged (main threshold: 5%), yielding roughly 167k standard and 60k adversarial conversations. Independent crowdworker quality judgments (three workers per example, majority vote) reveal a striking asymmetry: human conversationalists are rated "good" only 66.3% of the time overall (75.4% in standard conversations, but just 45.5% in adversarial ones), while the bot itself is rated good far more consistently (83.1% overall, 85.5% standard, 77.2% adversarial), i.e., the model is comparatively robust to conversation type while human behavior varies drastically. Breaking down failure reasons, humans in adversarial conversations are judged inappropriate 23.0% of the time versus 4.8% in standard ones, while the bot's dominant failure modes are being nonsensical, off-topic, or ignoring the last turn rather than being unsafe (though all bot failure types, including inappropriateness, are somewhat elevated in adversarial conversations too). A separate analysis compares organic user feedback (their own thumbs up/down) against independent crowdworker judgments of the same utterances: organically-liked messages are rated good far more often than unliked or unlabeled ones, and organically-disliked messages are rated good much less often, confirming the feedback carries real signal, though the paper also reports "relatively high disagreement rates" across dislike categories without quantifying inter-annotator agreement as a single statistic.

A reward model (binary good/bad classifier) is trained on combinations of raw organic feedback, "denoised" organic feedback (using a prior user-level denoising technique from Ju et al. 2022 that discounts users whose labels systematically disagree with the model), crowdworker annotations, and existing safety datasets (WikiToxic, BBFI, BAD, and Anthropic's harmless/red-team data). Organic feedback alone yields 80.7% reward-accuracy, improving to 82.0% with denoising; crowdworker annotations alone reach 88.8%; combining crowdworker and denoised-organic data gives the best result, 89.2%. Adding safety datasets to the mix pushes safety-classification accuracy from roughly 80-90% up to over 98%, and a larger T5-3B reward model performs marginally better than the 400M RoBERTa-based one the authors ultimately adopt for speed.

Using this reward model, the paper applies the Cringe loss (a prior technique that penalizes negative-example tokens by contrasting them against a positive token sampled from the model itself, requiring no architecture change) to fine-tune a 3B-parameter BlenderBot 3 model, comparing several ablations. Two negative results stand out: adding Cringe-loss training on public non-deployment dialogue datasets (including FITS, ImageChat, DECODE, and Anthropic's hh-rlhf helpful data) produces no gains on deployment validation metrics, attributed to distribution mismatch; and simply adding all deployment turns to training with a standard language-modeling loss, without any quality filtering, also fails to help, with raw human turns actively hurting bot performance (consistent with the earlier finding that many human turns are themselves low-quality or adversarial). What does work is applying Cringe loss to deployment bot turns specifically labeled by the reward model as good or bad: this improves the reward-model score from 83.3% to 90.4% and Bot Gold F1 from 16.52 to 17.48, with a second iteration (generating from the improved model, re-labeling with the reward model, and training again) pushing these further to 94.5% and 17.76 respectively, the best 3B-scale result, demonstrating that adapting the training signal to the model's own current output distribution matters.

Safety is treated as a distinct problem requiring more than just the general reward-model-plus-Cringe approach, since a model already trained with that data mixture (reward-model score 90.4%) shows no safety improvement over the untuned baseline in stratified adversarial-level testing (Table 7), which the authors attribute to the reward signal conflating many quality factors beyond safety, and to unsafe examples potentially still slipping through as reward-model-labeled "positive." Several dedicated safety interventions are compared at 3B scale: adding identified-unsafe bot utterances as Cringe-loss negatives alone ("safety negative") backfires, producing incoherent outputs because the model is told what not to say without being shown what to say instead; replacing unsafe responses with a single fixed canned safe message as a positive training target ("baked-in single safe message") achieves near-perfect safety accuracy (99-100% across adversarial levels) but severely overproduces the canned response even in safe contexts, tanking the reward-model and F1 scores; using entity-templated, context-varying canned safe messages instead of one fixed message ("baked-in variable safe message") helps somewhat but still overproduces; and the best-performing approach, "baked-in variable safety pos/neg," simultaneously trains the model to produce a safe, context-appropriate canned response in unsafe contexts as a positive target and to avoid that same canned response in safe contexts as a negative Cringe-loss target, achieving 99.4-100% safety accuracy across all four adversarial-severity levels while largely preserving reward-model score (90.4%) and Bot Gold F1 (17.18-17.35, further improved by additionally including plain safety-negative examples).

The full-scale experiment repeats this recipe on the original 175B-parameter OPT-based BlenderBot 3, running three iterations of Cringe-loss training on deployment data labeled by the reward model plus an independently-updated safety classifier (discarding "liked" examples from users whose conversations elsewhere contained unsafe content). Crowdworker human evaluation (213 held-out, safe-context deployment conversations) shows steady improvement across iterations: 85.3% (baseline) to 88.7% (iteration 1) to 90.9% (iteration 2) to 94.4% (iteration 3) responses rated good. A separate, much sparser evaluation using organic user feedback collected after deploying the final BB3x model (957 conversations) corroborates the direction of the result (3.47% thumbs-up vs. 2.12% for baseline; 1.40% thumbs-down vs. 1.63%), though the paper is explicit these organic-feedback rates are low and the samples small. Fine-tuning Llama-30B and Llama-65B on the same BB3 task mixture, as a check of whether the base pretrained model matters, produces markedly worse results (72.3% and 75.8% good respectively) than the OPT-based models, which the authors attribute tentatively to OPT's pretraining including Reddit-derived dialogue-like data that Llama's corpus lacks.

## Learnings

The paper's clearest and most transferable lesson is a controlled demonstration that feedback-training data must be matched to the deployment distribution to be useful: Cringe-loss training on high-quality, publicly-released human-feedback datasets from other projects, explicitly including Anthropic's hh-rlhf helpful-preference data, produced no measurable gain on the BlenderBot deployment's own validation metrics, while the same technique applied to the deployment's own (reward-model-labeled) data produced large, iteratively compounding gains. Good feedback data from a different distribution is not a substitute for feedback data on the actual target distribution, even when both are nominally about the same underlying task (helpful dialogue).

A second, more specific and reusable safety-training recipe is the "positive and negative baked-in" pattern: the paper shows in a clean, ablated sequence that a model must be taught both what to say when the situation calls for a safe refusal (baked-in positive) and, separately, when not to reach for that same safe-refusal response (baked-in negative), and that context-varying (entity-templated) refusal targets outperform a single fixed canned message. Doing only one half of this (safety-negative penalties alone, or a single fixed positive target alone) each fails in a specific, identifiable way, incoherent output in the first case, over-triggering of the safe response even in benign contexts in the second, and the paper's ablation sequence isolates exactly which combination fixes each failure mode rather than presenting only the final best recipe.

A third, more cautionary learning concerns naive imitation of organic conversational data: simply adding unfiltered human-side utterances from real deployment conversations as a training target for the bot to imitate actively hurt performance, a direct empirical consequence of a meaningful fraction (roughly a quarter to a half, depending on conversation type) of real human conversationalists themselves producing low-quality, off-topic, or inappropriate messages. Any project considering using logged user utterances as direct imitation targets should treat this as a concrete warning that unfiltered organic human behavior is not automatically a good training signal, even when the underlying task is nominally learning to converse "like a human."

## Verification

The paper builds several distinct verification layers rather than trusting any single signal. Organic user self-reported feedback (thumbs up/down) is independently cross-checked against blinded crowdworker judgments of the identical utterances (Table 3), providing a genuine calibration check on whether real users' own feedback tracks an independent quality judgment, which it does directionally (liked messages score higher, disliked messages score lower) even though the paper reports non-trivial disagreement rates without a single summary agreement statistic. A user-level denoising procedure (from prior work, Ju et al. 2022) is applied specifically to detect and downweight organic feedback-givers whose labels systematically disagree with the trained model's own predictions under cross-validation, functioning as an automated defense against adversarial or unreliable feedback sources rather than trusting all organic feedback equally. The reward model itself is validated against held-out crowdworker-annotated data (not just self-consistency), and separately validated for safety-classification accuracy against four distinct external safety benchmarks (BAD, WikiToxic, BBFI Standard, BBFI Adversarial) with different data distributions, which is a meaningfully more rigorous check than validating only on in-distribution deployment data. The safety-focused model variants are stress-tested specifically under four levels of increasing conversational adversarialness constructed directly from the deployment data's own safety-classifier flags, rather than only under average-case conditions, which directly targets the paper's own stated concern (models behaving well on average while still failing in adversarial situations). One verification gap: the primary 175B headline human-evaluation result (85.3% to 94.4%) is computed only on a sample explicitly filtered to safe-context conversations, so the full-scale model's behavior under the stratified adversarial-level testing performed at 3B scale (Table 7) is not directly confirmed at the actual 175B production scale in the results presented.

## Field context

This paper sits within Meta's own multi-year BlenderBot research program (Roller et al. 2020's BlenderBot, Chen et al. 2021's BlenderBot 2 with internet retrieval, Shuster et al. 2022's BlenderBot 3, the base model this paper improves) and explicitly engages the broader "self-feeding chatbot" and learning-from-deployment literature (Hancock et al. 2019's self-feeding chatbot, the LIGHT dialogue system's finding that framing/UI design strongly affects how adversarial real users are, and Ju et al. 2022's user-level denoising technique used directly here). Its most concrete point of contact with this corpus is direct and testable: the paper explicitly incorporates Anthropic's released hh-rlhf helpful-preference dataset (from [2204.05862], already reviewed) as one input to its Cringe-loss ablation and reports it produced no measurable improvement on the BlenderBot deployment distribution, an empirical instance of exactly the kind of narrow, population-specific alignment-data limitation that 2204.05862's own Section 7.2 anticipates when it argues for alignment data as a shared "public good" precisely because different projects' human-feedback distributions do not automatically transfer to one another. The paper's own iterative "generate, relabel with the reward model, retrain" loop (2 iterations at 3B scale, 3 at 175B scale) is also methodologically convergent with 2204.05862's "iterated online RLHF" (retraining preference models and policies on a rolling cadence to keep pace with an improving policy's output distribution), arrived at independently in a non-RL, Cringe-loss-based framework rather than PPO, which is a useful cross-validation that the general idea (stale feedback signal degrades as the policy improves, so the labeling model must be refreshed against the current policy's outputs) generalizes across quite different training algorithms.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central ablation sequence, showing precisely which combinations of positive/negative training signal fix which specific failure mode (incoherence from safety-negative alone; over-triggering from a single fixed positive target; both resolved by combining variable positive and negative targets), is unusually well-isolated for an applied systems paper, and the explicit negative results (public datasets and unfiltered human turns both failing to help) are reported plainly rather than omitted, which strengthens rather than undermines the paper's credibility.

The clearest limitation is that the paper's central quality claims rest on crowdworker judgment as ground truth without ever reporting a quantitative inter-annotator agreement statistic (a Fleiss' kappa, percentage agreement, or similar), despite the paper itself noting "relatively high disagreement rates" between organic-feedback categories and crowdworker labels, and despite the entire reward-model and evaluation pipeline being built on the assumption that three-crowdworker majority vote constitutes a reliable ground truth; a reader cannot currently assess how much of the reported gains reflect genuine model improvement versus the specific, unquantified subjectivity of this particular judging population. A second, more specific gap concerns scope: the headline 175B result (94.4% good, up from 85.3%) is computed only on a sample deliberately restricted to safe-context conversations, while the safety-specific stratified adversarial-level evaluation demonstrating the value of the "baked-in variable safety pos/neg" technique is conducted only at 3B scale; the paper does not report whether the actual deployed 175B BlenderBot 3x model incorporates that specific best safety technique or, if it does, how it performs across the same four adversarial severity levels at full scale, leaving the paper's two strongest individual results (best quality improvement, best safety improvement) somewhat disconnected from each other in terms of exactly what final production configuration achieves both simultaneously. A third, smaller point, one the paper's own Limitations section discusses candidly, is that the observed rate and nature of adversarial user behavior is likely substantially shaped by the specific UI, launch publicity, and social-media attention the deployment received (the authors note a pre-launch pilot saw far less adversarial behavior than the public post-launch period), meaning the specific standard-versus-adversarial proportions reported here (roughly 70/30) should not be read as a general property of chatbot deployments but as an artifact of this particular deployment's circumstances, a limitation the paper itself flags rather than one this review is raising independently.

What remains well supported: feedback-training data must be matched to the target deployment distribution rather than substituted with feedback data from a different (even if high-quality) source; naive imitation of unfiltered organic human conversational data actively degrades model quality; a reward-model-labeled, iteratively-refreshed Cringe-loss training signal produces real, human-evaluator-confirmed quality gains that grow with additional iterations; and effective safety training specifically requires teaching a model both when and when not to produce a safe refusal, with context-appropriate rather than fixed refusal targets, a specific and actionable design pattern validated through a genuinely isolating ablation sequence.
