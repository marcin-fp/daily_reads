Citation: Michael Noukhovitch, Hamish Ivison, Nathan Lambert, and Aaron Courville. Learning to Solve Hard Problems in RL for LLMs by Never Giving Up. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13443v1

## What this paper is about

Reinforcement learning is used to make LLMs better at reasoning, but this paper shows that RL training does not lift performance uniformly: it improves problems a model already handles reasonably well much more than it improves the problems the model was worst at, a pattern the authors name the Matthew Effect after the economics/network-science "rich get richer" phenomenon. They diagnose why standard GRPO-style training produces this bias (not primarily because hard problems are undersampled, but because compute is wasted repeatedly resampling problems the model has already solved) and introduce Never Give Up (NGU), a simple adaptive-sampling rule that keeps resampling a problem until it gets a correct answer, which reallocates training compute toward harder problems using existing asynchronous RL infrastructure.

## Extended summary

**Demonstrating the Matthew Effect.** Using three independently trained, open-source RL models from three domains (Olmo 3.1 RL-Zero on math/AIME, DeepCoder on code/LCBv5, DeepSWE-Preview on agentic coding/SWEBenchVerified), the authors bucket evaluation problems by initial-model difficulty and compare RL-trained accuracy to the base model's. Across all three domains and model families, RL gains are consistently proportional to the base model's initial competence: easy-bucket problems improve the most, hard/extra-hard buckets improve the least (Figure 1).

**Testing the signal-loss explanation.** The standard account is that GRPO's group-relative advantage gives zero gradient whenever all K sampled completions for a prompt share the same reward (all-correct or all-incorrect), so hard problems, which are disproportionately all-incorrect, contribute no learning signal, and the standard fix is to increase K. Training Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on GSM8k-Platinum with GRPO across K in {4, 8, 16, 32} (holding total batch size N*K constant), the authors find the Matthew Effect persists at every K, and counterintuitively the smallest setting (K=4) performs best, especially on the hardest problems (Figure 2). The mechanism (Figure 3): larger K only requires a single incorrect completion among many to keep an otherwise easy, mostly-solved prompt in the training batch (since it isn't literally all-correct), so larger K dilutes the training batch with easy prompts rather than concentrating compute on genuinely hard ones; smaller K filters easy prompts out of the batch more aggressively once the model can solve them consistently.

**Signal efficiency and Never Give Up.** The paper reframes the problem as signal efficiency rather than signal loss: the issue is not that hard problems are never sampled correctly, but that fixed-K sampling wastes compute repeatedly resampling problems that are already solved. NGU samples a small K (e.g. 4) per prompt; if all completions are correct, the prompt is filtered as too easy; if all are incorrect, rather than giving up, NGU resamples K more completions with probability p_NGU (continuing with a geometric distribution of total samples, giving up entirely with probability 1-p_NGU to avoid infinite compute on truly unsolvable prompts). This requires asynchronous RL infrastructure to work efficiently, since easy prompts that resolve quickly are replaced by harder ones still being resampled. On the GSM8k-Platinum testbed, NGU (K=4, p_NGU=0.95) outperforms all fixed-K baselines, with gains concentrated on the extra-hard subset (Figure 4), and achieves near-Pareto-optimal training-batch composition, matching the hard-problem representation of large-K settings early in training and the easy-problem filtering of small-K settings later (Figure 5).

**Design-choice ablations.** Maintaining previous (stale) completions once a prompt is eventually solved gives a richer GRPO group and larger effective advantages for rare correct answers, but including completions more than T=4 training steps stale degrades performance (T in {1,4,8,16} tested; performance rises from T=1 to T=4, then falls). To still use information from filtered stale completions without including them directly in the gradient, the authors propose "anchoring the positives," a specific advantage-rescaling rule that keeps positive-completion advantages fixed and evenly rescales negative-completion advantages to preserve a zero-sum group baseline; this outperforms both an unrescaled (de-centered) baseline and a prior downsampling approach (Xiong et al., 2025) that equalizes positive/negative counts, with downsampling shown to be particularly harmful (Figure 6).

**Scaling to a larger math task.** Training Qwen3-4B-base on a 10k-example Deepscaler subset, evaluated on AIME 2025 and BRUMO Nov 2025 with three seeds and compute-matched budgets (~120 H100 hours), varying K again trades off easy-vs-hard performance, but varying p_NGU trades off the same axis more favorably (improving hard-problem performance with a smaller cost to easy-problem performance, Figure 7). NGU also outperforms a static curriculum-learning baseline that sets a fixed, larger K per prompt based on the model's initial difficulty estimate: the fixed curriculum matches NGU on the hardest subset but degrades easy-problem performance, because difficulty is not static and shifts as the model improves during training, while NGU's difficulty assessment is implicitly dynamic.

**A harness-adjusted definition, and a coding demonstration.** On Manufactoria, a novel coding benchmark with an unfamiliar, long-format prompt, an initial attempt to observe the Matthew Effect (bucketing difficulty by the model's very first pass rate) found no clear effect; the authors attribute this to early RL steps being dominated by adapting to the unfamiliar prompt/harness rather than genuine task-skill improvement, and after re-bucketing difficulty using the model's performance after roughly 100 adaptation steps, a clear Matthew Effect reappears (Figure 8), leading to a refined "harness-aware" statement of the effect. Manufactoria problems contain 14-30 per-problem test cases of varying difficulty; standard GRPO with a per-test reward plateaus around solving 80% of individual tests but almost never fully solves any single problem (all tests correct), because most of the reward signal comes from inconsistently-solved medium-difficulty tests rather than the hardest ones. NGU (p_NGU=0.95) continues improving specifically on the hardest tests and eventually solves complete problems where standard GRPO stagnates (Figure 9). Compared against the only previously known fix for this task (Sun et al., 2025's two-stage recipe: train with a per-test reward until it plateaus, then switch to an all-tests-pass reward, since the model can never pass all tests from a cold start), NGU with a per-test reward alone matches the two-stage recipe's compute efficiency without requiring an explicit reward-switching curriculum (Figure 10). The authors additionally note that a model trained 3000 steps under the weaker per-test objective, then given NGU, recovers to match the two-stage approach, which they contrast with the classical RL primacy-bias literature (where bad early training permanently damages plasticity), arguing the Matthew Effect is a distinct phenomenon from plasticity loss.

## Learnings

The central methodological finding, that increasing completions-per-prompt K does not straightforwardly fix undersampling of hard problems because it simultaneously lets more nearly-solved easy prompts survive the all-correct/all-incorrect filter, is a specific, counterintuitive result (smaller K outperforming larger K on the hardest subset) worth remembering whenever tuning group-relative RL hyperparameters: the standard intuition ("more samples per prompt helps hard problems") can be wrong precisely because of the same filtering mechanism meant to remove uninformative groups.

Re-deriving a difficulty-stratified evaluation using the model's performance after a short adaptation period, rather than its literal initial performance, is a specific and transferable methodological fix whenever a novel prompt format or harness could itself be a source of early-training difficulty independent of the underlying task skill being measured; the paper's own before/after comparison (Figure 8) is a clean demonstration of a confound the authors caught and corrected in their own analysis rather than one a later reviewer had to point out.

The "anchoring the positives" advantage-rescaling rule (fix the advantage of positive completions, rescale negative completions' advantages to preserve a zero-sum baseline while excluding only the negatives too stale to use directly) is a specific, reusable technique for recovering partial value from off-policy or stale rollouts in asynchronous RL without directly training on them, distinct from simply discarding stale data or downsampling to balance positive/negative counts, and the paper's direct comparison shows the naive downsampling alternative is measurably worse.

## Verification

The paper's central claims are checked with a deliberate progression from observational demonstration (three independently trained, open-source models across three different task domains showing the same difficulty-proportional bias) to controlled causal testing (a small-scale, three-seed testbed that manipulates K directly and tracks batch composition over training, Figures 2-3) to a proposed mechanism and fix (NGU) validated with its own ablations (staleness threshold T, advantage-rescaling method) before being scaled up and re-tested against both a fixed-K sweep and an explicit curriculum-learning baseline at a larger scale (Figure 7). The GSM8k-Platinum and Deepscaler experiments both report mean and standard deviation across three seeds, and the harness-aware Matthew Effect refinement is itself a form of verification: the authors test whether their own effect might be an artifact of prompt-adaptation confounding difficulty measurement, find evidence it can be, and correct their protocol rather than reporting the confounded version. What is not similarly verified: the Manufactoria coding experiments (Section 6, Figures 8-10), which carry some of the paper's most striking qualitative claims (standard GRPO "remains unable to pass all tests on even a single example," NGU eventually solves them), do not state a number of seeds or report any run-to-run variance, unlike the GSM8k and Deepscaler experiments in the same paper.

## Field context

This paper sits in the GRPO-based RL-for-LLM-reasoning line of work (Shao et al.'s original GRPO, its dynamic-sampling and length-penalty refinements) and explicitly positions NGU against several adjacent lines it argues are orthogonal or complementary: methods that inject external information to help solve hard problems, methods that modify the GRPO loss itself, test-time search methods, and explicit curriculum-learning approaches (removing solved-out easy problems, curating harder datasets, or reweighting the sampled data distribution), arguing NGU differs by implicitly adapting sampling for compute efficiency rather than explicitly reshaping the training distribution. Its closest prior-work comparison is Reinforce-AdaSeq-Positive (Xiong et al., 2025), which targets the same undersampling problem for hard prompts but under synchronous RL, losing the compute-efficiency benefit NGU derives specifically from asynchronous infrastructure; the paper directly compares against this prior work's downsampling-based advantage adjustment and reports it performs worse than their own "anchoring the positives" rule. Within this corpus, "[Single-Rollout Asynchronous Optimization for Agentic Reinforcement Learning](2607.07508.md)" (SAO, reviewed in an earlier batch) addresses a different but related pain point in the same asynchronous-GRPO substrate: SAO tackles "policy lag" (tracking an exact old-policy probability across an asynchronously-updated model) and "group-wise sampling mismatch" (GRPO's requirement to wait for an entire group of rollouts before computing an advantage) by dropping group-relative comparison entirely in favor of a single-rollout, value-model-baselined approach; NGU instead keeps group-relative GRPO intact and uses asynchronous infrastructure to dynamically resample within a group until a solution is found. The two papers are not in direct competition (they solve different problems within the same broader "make async GRPO-style RL for LLMs more efficient" agenda) but both illustrate that asynchronous rollout infrastructure, not just the loss function, is now a first-class design surface for LLM RL efficiency work.

## Critical discussion

The paper's causal chain is unusually carefully built for a paper proposing a new training trick: rather than jumping straight to a fix, it first establishes the phenomenon across three independent, realistically-scaled open-source models and domains, then designs a dedicated, controlled small-scale testbed specifically to distinguish between two competing explanations (signal loss vs. signal efficiency), and the counterintuitive result of that testbed (smaller K outperforming larger K on hard problems) is exactly the kind of specific, falsifiable prediction that a purely correlational analysis could not have produced. The harness-aware refinement of the Matthew Effect, where the authors identify and correct a confound in their own definition when applied to a novel-prompt coding task, is a genuine strength: it would have been easy to report the initial null result on Manufactoria as a boundary case of the effect rather than investigating why it appeared absent.

Two points temper how far the results should be generalized. First, the phenomenon is demonstrated on three real, independently-trained models (up to 32B parameters, in agentic coding), but the causal analysis and the NGU method itself are validated only on smaller or narrower setups (a 0.5B model on GSM8k-Platinum for the mechanism study, a 4B model on a 10k-example Deepscaler subset for the "at scale" validation, and Manufactoria for coding); NGU is never applied back to the original three larger demonstration models and tasks (Olmo 3.1 RL-Zero, DeepCoder, DeepSWE) to close the loop end to end, so the paper's evidence that NGU specifically fixes the originally-observed Matthew Effect (rather than a similar but distinct effect at smaller scale) is indirect (`narrow-evaluation`). Second, while the GSM8k and Deepscaler experiments report three-seed means and standard deviations, the Manufactoria coding results, arguably the paper's most vivid qualitative finding (GRPO plateaus at partial test-passing while NGU eventually solves full problems), are reported without any stated seed count or variance, so the reliability of that specific comparison across independent training runs is not established the way the paper's other two experimental settings are (`missing-uncertainty`). The comparison to the curriculum-learning baseline and to Sun et al.'s two-stage reward-switching recipe are both genuine, previously-published or previously-proposed alternatives rather than weak strawmen, which strengthens the paper's competitive claims where they are made.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the existence of a difficulty-proportional RL improvement bias across three independent, realistically-trained models and domains; the specific mechanistic finding that larger K dilutes training batches with near-solved easy prompts rather than reliably increasing hard-problem signal; and NGU's improvement over fixed-K baselines and a static curriculum on the GSM8k and Deepscaler testbeds, each backed by a matched-compute, multi-seed comparison rather than a single anecdotal run.

## Relevance to us

The paper's headline observation, that a single aggregate accuracy number can hide a systematic bias where a training method is quietly failing on exactly the hardest, most informative cases while looking like it is improving overall, is a direct instance of a principle we already hold (a single error number is not enough; multidimensional, non-saturating evaluation that reports what failed, not just one score, is necessary to see this kind of effect at all) and this paper is a concrete, quantified demonstration of that principle specifically for RL post-training rather than for benchmark scoring. If we ever RL-train or fine-tune a physics-attuned backbone against verifiable rewards (relevant to B02 and B21), NGU's core mechanism, dynamically reallocating sampling compute toward problems the current policy has not yet solved rather than fixing a uniform sample budget per problem, is a directly transferable, low-overhead technique worth considering, and the paper's specific warning (a curriculum based on a model's initial difficulty estimate can degrade as the model improves, because difficulty is not static) is a concrete caution against building a fixed physics-problem difficulty curriculum without a mechanism to keep it current during training.
