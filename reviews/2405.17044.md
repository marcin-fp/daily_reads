Citation: Gu, X. and Krenn, M. Interesting Scientific Idea Generation using Knowledge Graphs and LLMs: Evaluations with 100 Research Group Leaders. arXiv:2405.17044 (2024).

## What this paper is about

Coming up with a genuinely interesting research idea, especially one that bridges two different fields, is hard, and the sheer volume of scientific literature makes it harder every year. This paper introduces SciMuse, a system that builds a knowledge graph from tens of millions of papers, uses it to identify a scientist's research interests and find promising conceptual bridges to another scientist's interests, and then has GPT-4 turn a chosen pair of concepts into a concrete, personalized research-collaboration proposal. The novel part is the evaluation: rather than a handful of graduate students judging a small batch of generic ideas (the norm in prior work), the authors got more than 100 senior research group leaders across the Max Planck Society — spanning physics, biology, and the humanities — to rate over 4,400 ideas personalized to their own actual research programs, and then used that large dataset to ask what makes an idea seem interesting and whether "interestingness" can be predicted cheaply, without needing a human in the loop every time.

## Extended summary

The knowledge graph is built from about 2.44 million paper titles/abstracts (arXiv, bioRxiv, chemRxiv, medRxiv, cutoff February 2023), from which the RAKE keyword-extraction algorithm plus GPT-3.5-based filtering and a Wikipedia-based recovery step for mistakenly-dropped terms yields a final vocabulary of 123,128 scientific concepts. Edges between concepts are drawn from co-occurrence in titles/abstracts across roughly 58 million papers from OpenAlex, weighted with citation information as a proxy for impact; this graph representation and its accompanying impact-prediction model were introduced in the authors' own prior work and are reused here largely as-is, applied to a new, larger graph.

To generate a personalized suggestion for a pair of researchers, the system extracts each researcher's own concepts from their last two years of publications, builds a personalized subgraph for each, and selects a concept pair in one of three ways: randomly from the two subgraphs, the pair predicted (via the authors' prior impact-forecasting method) to have the highest future impact, or no specific pair at all (GPT-4 works only from the researchers' recent paper titles). GPT-4 is then prompted, using a self-refine-style procedure (generate three candidate framings, critique them, refine twice, then commit to a final project), to produce a titled project description combining the two researchers' expertise.

The main human evaluation involved 110 Max Planck group leaders (104 from natural-science/technology institutes, 6 from social-science/humanities institutes; average 59.7 papers published, 3,760 citations, evaluated as of January 2024) from 54 institutes, each rating up to 48 personalized suggestions on a 1 ("not interesting") to 5 ("very interesting") scale, yielding 4,451 total ratings; each rated suggestion was evaluated by only the one researcher (Researcher A) being proposed as one half of the collaboration, not independently cross-checked by a second rater. About 25% of suggestions (1,107 of 4,451) were rated 4 or 5, with 394 rated the maximum. A first headline finding is a null result the authors report plainly: suggestions generated using a random concept pair, a predicted-high-impact concept pair, or no concept pair at all received statistically indistinguishable interest ratings on average, which the authors use as a "sanity check" enabling a cleaner analysis of which network features correlate with interest, since concept-pair type itself doesn't appear to drive the outcome. Analyzing 2,996 of the ratings (those generated using an actual concept pair) against eight knowledge-graph-derived features, binned into 50 groups each, the paper reports two consistent trends: higher network degree, PageRank, and citation counts for a concept are negatively correlated with the interest rating of ideas built around it (more "central"/heavily-cited concepts make for less interesting suggestions), and greater semantic distance between the two researchers' fields is also negatively correlated with interest (same-institute, topically closer pairings tend to be rated as more interesting than pairings from more distant fields), consistent with a separate observation that same-institute collaborations were rated somewhat higher than cross-institute ones.

Two methods are used to predict, ahead of time, whether a suggestion will be rated highly interesting (4-5) versus not (below 4), treated as a binary classification task. A small, data-efficient neural network (25 selected knowledge-graph features, one 50-neuron hidden layer, trained with dropout and evaluated via Monte Carlo cross-validation run until the standard deviation of mean AUC fell below 10^-3, reached after 130 iterations) achieves an average AUC of 0.673 and, more practically, a top-3 precision of 66.4% (i.e., two-thirds of its top-3 recommended suggestions per researcher were rated highly interesting, versus a 23% base rate) — the network's performance is reported as robust across a hyperparameter sweep (feature count, layer width, learning rate, dropout), and a decision-tree baseline could not match it. Separately, and without using any human evaluation data at all, GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o-mini were used to rank all 2,996 suggestions via pairwise ELO-tournament comparisons (22,000-45,000 comparisons per model, each researcher-specific suggestion starting at ELO 1400); GPT-4o's zero-shot ranking reached an AUC of 0.673 (essentially matching the supervised neural network) with top-3 precision of 45.0% and top-1 precision of 51.0% (versus the neural network's 70% top-1 precision), and ranking quality improved with newer models (GPT-4o outperforming GPT-3.5). Supplementary small-scale tests (3 evaluators, 180 pairs comparing GPT-4 versus GPT-4o outputs from the same prompt; 1 evaluator, 11 pairs comparing GPT-4o versus GPT-o1) found progressively newer models' ideas preferred more often (60.6% for GPT-4o over GPT-4; all 11 of 11 for GPT-o1 over GPT-4o), explicitly flagged by the authors as small-scale, suggestive results rather than a controlled claim.

Data and code are released (Zenodo for the knowledge-graph data, GitHub for code and evaluation data), and the study was reviewed and approved by the Max Planck Society's Ethics Council. The Discussion frames the two prediction methods as complementary: the supervised network is useful for selecting promising abstract topics before generating specific text, while the zero-shot LLM ranking is valuable precisely when expensive human-evaluation data isn't available, and speculates toward a longer-term vision of automated idea generation feeding into automated experimentation.

## Learnings

Whether a suggested idea was built from a random concept pair, a pair predicted to have high future impact, or no concept pair at all made no measurable difference to how interesting expert evaluators rated it. This is a genuinely useful, honestly-reported null result: it suggests that, in this system, the elaborate knowledge-graph impact-prediction machinery may not be what actually drives a proposal's perceived appeal, and that GPT-4's own generative and self-refinement process, working from little more than a researcher's recent paper titles, may be carrying most of the load — worth checking directly (with a simpler ablation) before assuming a sophisticated retrieval or ranking layer is earning its complexity in any similar idea-generation system.

Two cheap, content-free proxies for "will an expert find this interesting" — a small neural network trained only on graph-structural features (never seeing the generated text) and an LLM performing zero-shot pairwise ELO-tournament comparisons (never seeing any human feedback) — achieved essentially the same discrimination (AUC around 0.67) as each other, and both meaningfully beat random selection at surfacing the few best ideas among hundreds (precision at the top few recommendations). Structural, text-free signal alone carries real information about perceived interestingness, and an LLM can rank for subjective appeal about as well as a model purpose-trained on human ratings, without ever seeing those ratings.

Two specific, consistently-observed correlations push against an intuitive assumption that "more surprising and more distant" is what makes a cross-disciplinary idea compelling: concepts that are more central and more heavily cited in the literature produced less interesting suggestions, and researcher pairs whose fields were semantically closer (not farther) were rated as more interesting collaborations than more distantly related pairs. Familiarity and centrality, not novelty and distance, appear to correlate negatively and positively (respectively) with perceived interest in this dataset.

## Verification

This paper is not about verifying the scientific content of the generated ideas — no check is made on whether a proposed project is technically feasible, correctly reasoned, or genuinely novel relative to the literature beyond the concept-pair's own graph-derived "impact" score; "interest," the only outcome measured, is a subjective rating of appeal, not a check on validity. What the paper does verify carefully is its own predictive claims about that subjective rating: the supervised neural network's performance estimate uses Monte Carlo cross-validation run to a pre-specified statistical stopping criterion (standard deviation of mean AUC below 10^-3), and its robustness to hyperparameter choice is explicitly checked via a sweep (Fig. S2) rather than assumed; the zero-shot LLM ranking's alignment with human ratings is tracked as an AUC computed against the held-out human-eval labels over the course of the ELO tournament (Fig. S4), which is direct, if modest-scale, external validation of a ranking procedure that itself never saw the human data. Both of these are appropriate, standard machine-learning validation practices for the claims being made (predicting a rating), distinct from and not a substitute for checking the substantive quality of an idea, which is out of scope for this paper and should not be read into it.

## Field context

The paper positions itself explicitly against a small set of prior LLM-based research-idea-generation systems (SciMon; a hypothesis-discovery system evaluated by social-science PhD students; ResearchAgent), each criticized by name for evaluating with only a handful (6, 3, or 10) of graduate-student raters rather than experienced group leaders who "define and evaluate research projects through grant applications" — the paper's central methodological claim to novelty is scale and evaluator seniority, not the underlying idea-generation mechanism itself, which the authors present as a natural extension of their own group's earlier work on knowledge-graph-based prediction of research trends and future high-impact topics (cited repeatedly as the source of both the graph-construction methodology and the impact-prediction features reused here). None of these specific precedents are yet reviewed in this corpus.

## Critical discussion

The scale of this evaluation (110 senior scientists, 4,451 ratings, real personalized proposals rather than generic prompts) is a genuine methodological advance over the small graduate-student evaluations the paper explicitly contrasts itself with, and the honest reporting of a null result (concept-pair selection method has no measurable effect on interest) rather than only favorable findings is to the paper's credit.

The most significant unaddressed gap is that each rated suggestion received exactly one human rating, from the one researcher (Researcher A) it was framed around, with no second rater and no reported measure of inter-rater agreement or rating stability anywhere in the paper. This matters specifically for the correlational claims in the "Properties of interesting research suggestions" analysis (concept centrality, semantic distance, etc.): with a single, unreplicated rating per item, there is no way to know how much of the reported interest score reflects a property of the idea itself versus that one evaluator's personal taste, mood, or familiarity with the topic on the day they rated it, and the paper does not discuss this limitation or attempt to bound it (for example, by having a subset of ideas double-rated). Given that a large, well-resourced, ethics-board-approved study of this kind could have collected duplicate ratings on at least a subsample, this is a real, addressable gap rather than an unavoidable one.

The framing of the prediction results also runs slightly ahead of the numbers reported: the text describes both the neural network and GPT-4o's zero-shot ranking as achieving "high prediction accuracy," but an AUC of about 0.65-0.67 on a binary task is a real, non-trivial, and (per the top-N precision results) practically useful signal, not a strong one in absolute terms — "high" is an optimistic characterization of an effect that is genuinely useful for the specific application shown (surfacing a handful of good ideas from hundreds) but modest as a general classifier. This is a minor but specific instance of language outrunning the evidence, worth noting because the same numbers, described more calibratedly ("a moderate but practically useful signal"), would support exactly the same practical conclusions the paper draws without the overstatement.

A further, related interpretive tension: the null result on concept-pair selection method sits somewhat uneasily next to the paper's broader framing of the knowledge graph as the mechanism that "facilitates the discovery of interesting research collaborations... that might otherwise remain undiscovered." If suggestions generated with no concept pair at all (i.e., GPT-4 working only from recent paper titles) are rated just as interesting as those using the impact-predicting knowledge-graph machinery, the knowledge graph's specific contribution to perceived idea quality — as opposed to its role in personalizing which two researchers get paired at all — is not clearly established by the evidence presented, a point the paper's Discussion does not directly reconcile.

What remains well supported: expert-rated interest in AI-generated, personalized research ideas is measurable at meaningful scale from senior scientists across disciplines, roughly a quarter of generated suggestions were rated highly interesting, and both a cheap, content-free neural-network predictor and a zero-shot LLM ranking procedure can identify the most promising few suggestions out of many substantially better than chance. What is less clearly established is exactly what role the knowledge-graph engineering plays versus the underlying LLM's own capability, and how much the correlational "what makes an idea interesting" findings would replicate under a design with multiple independent raters per idea.

## Relevance to us

This is a large-scale, directly relevant prior-art data point for our own B17 interest (a problem-formulation engine that turns a hunch into a testable program) and for Tension 3 (solving vs. problem formulation): SciMuse is a working, seriously-evaluated instance of exactly this class of system, and its most actionable lesson is the null result on concept-pair selection — before investing in a bespoke physics-concept knowledge graph or impact-prediction layer to drive an idea-generation feature (adjacent to our B04 interest in universal physics-concept embeddings), it is worth testing, as this paper incidentally did, whether that machinery actually changes how interesting the resulting ideas seem relative to a much simpler baseline (an LLM working from a researcher's own recent papers alone), rather than assuming the engineering effort is what's earning any observed quality.

The single-rater-per-item evaluation design is a concrete methodological gap worth avoiding if we ever run an analogous study asking whether physicists find Theo's proposed research directions compelling: collecting at least some duplicate ratings per idea would let us distinguish genuine idea quality from one evaluator's personal taste, something this otherwise large and well-run study did not do. The correlational finding that topically closer researcher pairs were rated as more interesting collaborations than more distant ones is also a useful, if modest, counterweight to an assumption embedded in our own framing of cross-disciplinary idea generation (that maximal disciplinary distance is what makes a proposed connection exciting) — worth treating as a real, if unreplicated, empirical data point rather than dismissing.

Finally, this paper's zero-shot GPT-Elo-tournament ranking mechanism is the same general method (LLM-judged pairwise comparisons accumulated into an ELO score) used by Co-Scientist's hypothesis-ranking tournament, reviewed elsewhere in this corpus ([26cosci](26cosci.md)), applied here to a different target (subjective interest rather than hypothesis quality). Co-Scientist's Elo tournament was flagged there for judge circularity when used to claim superiority over competing systems; SciMuse's use of the identical mechanism for a genuinely different, more modest claim (predicting subjective appeal, validated post hoc against real independent human ratings rather than used to declare victory over rival models) is a fairer and better-validated use of the same underlying tool, worth remembering as the difference between the mechanism itself and how rigorously its output is checked against independent ground truth.
