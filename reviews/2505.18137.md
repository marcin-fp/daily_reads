Citation: Kundu, A. K., Patil, V. S., and Jaja, J. Boosting Open Set Recognition Performance through Modulated Representation Learning. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), 2026.

## What this paper is about

Open set recognition (OSR) asks a classifier to correctly label test samples from its known training classes while also flagging samples from classes it has never seen. Many recent OSR methods rely on a "temperature" hyperparameter that scales similarity scores or logits before a loss function is applied, and this temperature is normally held fixed throughout training. This paper observes that a low temperature and a high temperature push a model's learned representations toward two different, useful properties (fine-grained, instance-specific separation versus coarse, class-level compactness), and that holding temperature constant forces a compromise between them rather than getting both. The authors propose scheduling the temperature over training instead of fixing it — specifically, starting low and moving to a higher value using a new "negative cosine" schedule — and show this drop-in change (no extra computation, no extra data, no auxiliary model) improves both open-set and closed-set accuracy across several standard OSR loss functions and benchmarks, most consistently on the harder, larger-scale "semantic shift benchmarks" that much recent OSR work does not evaluate on at all.

## Extended summary

The paper starts from an analysis of why temperature matters in the two loss functions most commonly used for OSR representation learning: cross-entropy (CE) and supervised contrastive loss (SupCon). In SupCon, a lower temperature amplifies the differences in scaled similarity between an anchor and its negatives, so the loss pushes hardest against the nearest negatives, producing instance-specific, spread-out representations with a sharp decision boundary; open-set samples stay distant from known-class clusters because of the heavy penalty for even slight similarity. A higher temperature spreads the repulsive gradient across more negatives, producing group-wise, semantically compact clusters with a smoother boundary, but open-set samples that are semantically close to a known class now land inside its cluster. The same low/high dichotomy (sharper vs. smoother output distributions) holds for CE loss. Since a fixed temperature can only sit at one point on this spectrum, the authors propose scheduling it across training instead.

Their central proposal is a generalized cosine schedule with a phase parameter that, at one extreme (phase 0), reduces to a previously proposed cosine schedule from the self-supervised long-tailed-classification literature (Kukleva et al., 2023) that starts high and decreases, and at the other extreme (phase 1, their new "negative cosine schedule," NegCosSch) starts low and increases instead. Their argument for reversing the direction is procedural: starting with a low temperature first lets the model form a coarse, well-separated decision boundary while open-set-like regions stay pushed away, and only then raising the temperature refines within-class compactness without disturbing that separation; doing it in the opposite order (start compact, then sharpen) does not preserve the same benefit, per their ablation results. They also propose simpler monotonic variants (a single half-cycle increase, called M-NegCosSch, plus plain linear, exponential, and logarithmic increasing schedules) and note that the schedule can be folded into any existing temperature-dependent OSR loss (CE, SupCon, or the ARPL loss of Chen et al., 2021) as a pure hyperparameter change, with no added compute, data, or model.

Experiments are run on TinyImageNet and on the harder semantic shift benchmarks (SSBs: CUB-200, FGVC-Aircraft, and Stanford Cars, each with "Easy" and "Hard" open-set splits, following Vaze et al., 2022), plus additional CIFAR-derived benchmarks reported in the appendix. Models are VGG32 (regular benchmarks) or ResNet-50 pretrained on Places365 (SSBs), each trained for 600 epochs with 5 random seeds, reporting closed-set accuracy, open-set AUROC, and the combined open-set classification rate (OSCR). On CE loss, an ablation across many candidate schedules (random, linear decrease, several increasing schedules, periodic and monotonic versions of the cosine and negative-cosine schedules) shows the proposed increasing schedules, especially the periodic negative-cosine schedule (P-NegCosSch), winning the single best result in 8 of 18 reported metric/dataset columns, with M-NegCosSch and a plain linear increase each winning 4. Extended to four loss functions (CE, CE with label smoothing, SupCon, SupCon with label smoothing, and ARPL), NegCosSch improves over the constant-temperature baseline in nearly all reported cases (up to 1.87 points of accuracy, up to 3.3/3.1 points of AUROC on Easy/Hard splits, and up to 4.4/3.96 points of OSCR), with two explicitly acknowledged exceptions where it does not beat the baseline (Aircraft under SupCon with label smoothing, and TinyImageNet under CE with label smoothing). A further experiment varying the fraction of available training classes (20% to 100%) on the SSBs shows the improvement from NegCosSch over the best constant-temperature baseline tends to grow as the task gets harder (more training classes), most visibly on CUB and least on FGVC-Aircraft. Appendix results extend the finding to a Vision Transformer backbone on CUB and report per-condition standard deviations across the 5 seeds for both the ablation table and the four-loss-function table.

## Learnings

Temperature in a similarity- or logit-scaled loss is not just a calibration knob; it trades off two genuinely different representation-learning objectives (instance-level separation versus class-level compactness), and a paper that needs both properties at different points in training can potentially get both by scheduling the temperature rather than picking a single compromise value — a reusable idea that likely generalizes to any temperature-scaled contrastive or cross-entropy objective, not just OSR specifically.

The direction of a temperature schedule matters, not just its range: this paper's own ablation shows that starting low and increasing (their negative cosine schedule) outperforms starting high and decreasing (the pre-existing cosine schedule it is built from), attributed to which representational property gets "locked in" first (coarse separation before within-class compactness) rather than the reverse order. This is a concrete, checkable claim (not merely an intuition) that a future paper using a similar schedule could directly test in its own setting.

A drop-in hyperparameter-schedule change (an epoch-dependent temperature, with zero added compute, data, or parameters) produced measurable gains across four different loss functions (CE, SupCon, and ARPL, with and without label smoothing) and multiple benchmarks in this paper's tests, which is a useful demonstration that method-agnostic "how a hyperparameter is set over training" changes can be a comparably cheap lever to more heavily engineered interventions (auxiliary generative models, mix-up, secondary objectives) that the same subfield has otherwise pursued.

## Verification

This paper's checks are the standard supervised-learning kind: closed-set accuracy against ground-truth labels, and open-set detection performance (AUROC, OSCR) against a ground-truth known/unknown label per test sample, all computed directly from the labeled evaluation splits rather than through any model-based judge. The one verification-adjacent design choice worth noting is honesty about negative results: the authors explicitly name the two cases (Aircraft/SupCon+LS, TinyImageNet/CE+LS) where their proposed schedule does not beat the constant-temperature baseline, rather than omitting or downplaying them, and they report per-seed standard deviations for both of their two main results tables (Tables 3 and 4 in the appendix) instead of only point estimates. What is not present is any formal statistical test (e.g., a paired test across the 5 seeds) establishing which of the reported improvements exceed what the reported seed-to-seed variance would produce by chance; see Critical discussion.

## Field context

The paper builds directly on the "good closed-set classifier" baseline and the semantic shift benchmarks introduced by Vaze et al. (2022), which the authors credit with re-triggering OSR research toward better representation learning rather than toward generative or secondary-objective approaches. It positions itself against three broad prior strategies it argues are all computationally heavier: generating synthetic open-set-like samples or using mix-up (Chen et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2023; among others), training a secondary model with an auxiliary objective (e.g., VAEs with a reconstruction loss), and adding regularization terms that carve out empty regions of representation space for unknown classes to (hopefully) occupy (Chen et al. 2020a; 2021; Zhou et al. 2021). Its more specific technical precedent is a cosine temperature schedule proposed by Kukleva et al. (2023) for self-supervised learning on long-tailed closed-set data; this paper's central technical move is to test the reverse direction of that same family of schedules and to apply it to the open-set setting instead of the closed-set, long-tailed setting Kukleva et al. studied. None of these precedents are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first open-set-recognition paper in the corpus.

## Critical discussion

The paper is transparent in ways that build some trust: it names its own two failure cases rather than omitting them, and it reports per-seed standard deviations for its main comparisons instead of only point estimates. Reading those standard deviations against the reported improvements, however, surfaces a real gap: several of the reported gains are comparable in size to (or smaller than) the reported per-seed standard deviations. For example, in Table 2, the CE+label-smoothing row on TinyImageNet shows the constant baseline at 84.55% accuracy against M-NegCosSch at 84.24% (i.e., NegCosSch is nominally worse there, one of the two acknowledged exceptions), while several of the SupCon-loss accuracy improvements on TinyImageNet and Stanford Cars are on the order of a few hundredths to a few tenths of a percentage point against reported standard deviations of a similar or larger magnitude (e.g., SupCon without label smoothing on TinyImageNet: 85.37% baseline vs. 85.40% for M-NegCosSch, against a baseline standard deviation of 0.18). No paired significance test across the 5 seeds is reported anywhere in the paper, so it is not possible, from the numbers given, to tell how many of the claimed improvements would survive such a test; the larger, more clearly separated gains (the CE-loss CUB and Aircraft results, and most of the CE-loss ablation table) look much more robust than the smaller SupCon-loss gains on some datasets, and the paper's own framing ("improves ... for all cases except for two") does not distinguish between a clearly significant win and a numerically small one within noise.

A second limitation, disclosed by the authors themselves in a footnote, is that the exact pretrained backbone used for SSB experiments in the original Vaze et al. (2022) paper could not be located, so a different, "completely unrelated" Places365-pretrained checkpoint was substituted. This is handled honestly and does not undermine the paper's internal comparisons (every schedule and loss function in this paper is evaluated on the same substituted backbone), but it does mean the paper's absolute SSB numbers are not directly comparable to the original Vaze et al. published numbers, which matters for any reader trying to place this paper's results on an external leaderboard rather than reading its own internal ablations.

A third, more minor point: SupCon training on the SSBs used a batch size of 12 (stated as a single-GPU memory constraint), which is unusually small for a contrastive loss that benefits from a larger pool of in-batch negatives; this could plausibly explain why the SupCon-loss improvements are generally smaller and less consistent across benchmarks than the CE-loss improvements in the same tables, though the paper does not discuss this connection itself.
