Citation: Khairi, A., D'souza, D., Fadaee, M., Kreutzer, J. Making, not Taking, the Best of N. arXiv:2510.00931, 2025.

## What this paper is about

When an LLM system generates several candidate answers to a prompt, the standard way to pick a final output is Best-of-N: score every candidate and keep only the single highest-scoring one, throwing the rest away. This paper argues that approach wastes information, since different candidates often get different parts of the answer right, and proposes instead using a second LLM (a "fusor") to read all the candidates together and write a new answer that combines their best pieces. The paper tests this idea, called Fusion-of-N (FusioN), as a drop-in replacement for Best-of-N in two settings people already use it for: picking a final answer at inference time, and picking which generated example to use for fine-tuning a smaller student model, across many languages and tasks.

## Extended summary

Best-of-N (BoN) selects the single candidate y* from a pool Y that maximizes a scoring function S(x, y), where S is typically a trained reward model or an LLM-based scorer; the paper notes BoN's quality is capped by the single best candidate in the pool and by how well the scoring function aligns with what is actually wanted, and that scaling either the reward model's accuracy or the sample count to fix this can be expensive (sometimes needing thousands of samples). FusioN instead has an LLM fusor F generate a new response y* conditioned on the full prompt and the entire candidate pool at once, so the final answer is not restricted to be one of the original candidates and can in principle exceed the best individual one; BoN is described as a degenerate special case of FusioN, since the fusor retains the option to simply copy the single best candidate wholesale if nothing else in the pool is useful. The FusioN prompt instructs the fusor to focus on both integrating the best parts and discarding the worst ones, and the authors report only minimal manual prompt tuning beyond that instruction.

Two experimental settings are tested, holding the underlying candidate pool identical between BoN and FusioN in each so that only the aggregation mechanism differs. In test-time scaling, N=5 samples (temperature 0.7) are drawn from either Aya Expanse 8B or Command A (111B), with BoN scored by an in-house multilingual reward model (ranked around 11th on English RewardBench 2 at submission time, and top of the multilingual RewardBench leaderboard) and FusioN using Command A as fusor. In synthetic data generation, five diverse open teacher models (Gemma3-27B-It, Kimi-K2-Instruct, Qwen3-235B, DeepSeek-V3, Command A) each produce one low-temperature completion per prompt, and either BoN (reward-model selection) or FusioN (Command A fusor) picks the training example used to fine-tune a 111B instruction-tuned student (with a 7B-base ablation reported separately), across a general instruction-following mix (UltraFeedback Binarized, machine-translated into 9 languages) and a reasoning mix (GeoFactX for geography-based factual reasoning, translated s1k prompts for math), with teachers prompted to produce full chains of thought for the reasoning data. Evaluation spans three task families: open-ended generation judged by GPT-4o-based win-rate against Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash on mArenaHard v2 (11 languages), machine translation scored by the XComet-XL metric on WMT24++ (English into 10 languages), and closed-ended reasoning accuracy on GeoFactX and MGSM.

In test-time scaling, FusioN improves win-rate against Gemini 2.5 Pro over BoN for both model sizes across most languages (up to +10.8 points for Aya Expanse 8B in French), and in two cases (Command A in German and Spanish) FusioN with only 5 samples lets the smaller Command A model win outright against the larger Gemini 2.5 Pro; the paper notes that when the fusor and the sampled model are the same (Command A fusing its own samples), this amounts to a form of self-refinement. On WMT24++ translation, FusioN beats BoN by up to +11.4 XCometXL points in Korean, and in three languages (German, Russian, Chinese) FusioN's fused output actually scores higher than the "oracle" (the single sample that would have scored highest against the ground-truth reference had it been perfectly identified), demonstrating that selection alone cannot reach FusioN's ceiling even under a hypothetically perfect selector. For synthetic-data generation, fine-tuning on FusioN-selected versus BoN-selected data (all else held identical) yields consistent downstream gains for FusioN: +2.5 average win-rate points against Gemini 2.5 Flash on Arena and +0.8 average XCometXL on WMT (statistically significant per paired t-test/bootstrap in most languages), and on the reasoning mix, FusioN-trained models beat BoN-trained models in answer correctness and reasoning quality on GeoFactX in 4 of 5 languages (with the largest gains, +2.8 points, in lower-resource Swahili), and both FusioN- and BoN-trained models exceed not just the base model but the fusor model itself, which the authors interpret as evidence that synthesis genuinely distills collective knowledge across teachers rather than being capped by the fusor's own competence.

A dedicated analysis section investigates mechanism and limits. Fusor scale matters: a small (4B) fusor performs poorly at FusioN out of the box, and win-rate keeps improving as fusor scale increases up to 111B, the opposite pattern from using the same models as BoN scorers, where smaller models can score adequately but gains from added scale plateau; this mirrors the broader finding that even top generative models tend to lag purpose-trained reward-model classifiers on reward-scoring benchmarks specifically. FusioN is also more sample-efficient at low N (matching or beating BoN's gains at N=10 with only N=2 samples), though the two are not directly comparable in compute terms since FusioN's single fusor call encodes all N samples jointly in one long context rather than N independently-parallelizable generations. Ablating the teacher pool shows FusioN is comparatively robust to a weaker or smaller pool of contributing models (a weaker single substituted teacher, or dropping one of five teachers, hurts FusioN less than it helps BoN relatively), though sampling repeatedly from only a single teacher substantially hurts both methods, underscoring that pool diversity itself, not just method choice, drives quality. Surface-level attribution analysis (tracking which words in a fused output trace back to which teacher) shows FusioN blends contributions from multiple teachers rather than simply reproducing one, including from lower-preference teachers and from teachers in languages the fusor model does not officially support. On the GeoFactX data specifically, FusioN achieves the highest answer-correctness (58.6%) and the highest internal reward score, even though the fusor model itself (Command A) individually scores the lowest correctness among the five teachers, which the authors take as evidence FusioN's quality ceiling is set by the diversity of the pool rather than by the fusor's own standalone competence.

The paper also reports a specific, self-disclosed exception to its overall pattern: on the closed-ended MGSM math benchmark, BoN retains a small but consistent edge over FusioN in test-time scaling across nearly all languages tested, and in the reasoning-data fine-tuning setting, the FusioN-trained student underperforms both the BoN-trained student (-1.2%) and the fusor's own judge-time accuracy (-1.5%) on MGSM specifically, even though FusioN still improves over the base model and clearly wins on the GeoFactX factual-reasoning task in the same experiment. The authors speculate this may reflect either that closed-ended, single-correct-answer math tasks are poorly suited to generative ensembling (versus the specialized training a reward model typically receives for such domains), or a possible contamination confound (the popular s1k math dataset may already have been seen during training by both the fusor and the baseline model), a caveat they flag explicitly rather than omit. Separately, a bias analysis addressing potential LLM-judge confounds finds no evidence of self-preference bias (the fusor does not systematically favor its own generations when they are in the candidate pool) but does find a slight position bias, where the fusor tends to favor candidates presented earlier in its context over otherwise-similar candidates presented later.

## Learnings

The paper's most broadly transferable finding is a clean, repeated demonstration across two genuinely distinct use cases (test-time output selection and synthetic training-data selection) and multiple tasks that treating aggregation as synthesis rather than selection can exceed the ceiling that pure selection is mathematically bound by, most concretely shown by FusioN beating the oracle-selected sample in three translation languages, a specific, quantified counter-example to the assumption that a selection method's best-case performance is capped by the best individual sample in the pool.

A second, more mechanistic learning concerns when synthesis-based aggregation actually works: the paper's finding that a fusor needs to cross a certain scale threshold to perform effective synthesis out-of-the-box (unlike scalar scoring, where smaller models can already do a passable job) is a concrete, actionable design constraint for anyone considering this approach, as is the complementary finding that once past that threshold, the specific choice of which capable fusor model to use matters less than the diversity and quality of the underlying candidate pool.

A third, genuinely important cautionary learning is the paper's own reported exception on closed-ended math reasoning (MGSM), where BoN retains an edge over FusioN despite FusioN's advantages elsewhere; this is a specific, non-trivial boundary condition on when generative synthesis is the right tool, suggesting that domains with a single, precisely verifiable correct answer and well-developed specialized reward models may not benefit from, or may even be hurt by, an LLM-based synthesis step relative to a well-trained scalar scorer, a distinction worth checking for explicitly before adopting FusioN-style aggregation in a new domain.

## Verification

The paper takes several concrete steps to rule out specific alternative explanations for its results. The reward model used for BoN is independently benchmarked and shown to be strong rather than a weak strawman (ranked competitively on English RewardBench 2 and at the top of a multilingual RewardBench leaderboard at the time of submission), which strengthens the comparison since FusioN is shown to beat a genuinely capable, not merely convenient, selection baseline. The oracle comparison on WMT24++, using the actual ground-truth reference to identify the best-possible candidate in the pool, is a real, meaningful upper bound rather than an assumed one, and FusioN's result of exceeding this oracle in three languages is a directly falsifiable, specific claim rather than a vague assertion of superiority. The downstream fine-tuning comparisons hold every experimental factor constant (hyperparameters, prompts, teacher outputs) except the aggregation method, isolating the aggregation choice as the causal variable, and WMT results are checked for statistical significance via paired t-test and bootstrap resampling rather than reported as raw deltas only. The paper also runs a specific, targeted bias check for two plausible LLM-judge confounds (self-preference and position bias) rather than assuming the fusor's synthesis judgments are unbiased, finding no self-preference effect but a real, disclosed position bias.

Verification is comparatively thinner in two places. First, the central Arena win-rate metric across nearly all reported test-time-scaling and downstream-generation results depends on a single LLM judge (GPT-4o), and while the paper checks that judge for position and self-preference bias with respect to the fusor's own outputs specifically, it does not report a broader validation of that judge's reliability (e.g. against human preference agreement) the way some other benchmark papers in this corpus do for their LLM-judge components. Second, the paper's efficiency claims (FusioN as "the more efficient choice for low-budget scaling") are framed entirely in terms of sample count N, and the paper itself notes but does not quantify the asymmetry that FusioN requires one long-context call encoding all N samples through a large fusor model, while BoN requires N independently parallelizable generations plus a comparatively lightweight reward-model scoring pass; no wall-clock latency or total-FLOPs comparison is reported to substantiate a practical efficiency claim once this asymmetry in per-call cost is accounted for.

## Field context

The paper explicitly positions FusioN within a recent and active line of work on synthesis-based ensembling at inference, most directly Mixture-of-Agents (Wang et al. 2024) and LLM-Blender (Jiang et al. 2023), distinguishing itself by requiring no specialized training (unlike LLM-Blender's separate pairwise-ranking and fusion modules, or other contemporaneous aggregator-training approaches it cites) and by working effectively in a single aggregation step rather than the layered, iterative structure of Mixture-of-Agents, positioning simplicity and out-of-the-box applicability as its main practical advantage over that more widely-adopted-in-spirit but more complex framework. It also explicitly engages with, and reports results in some tension with, a specific prior claim (Li et al. 2025a) that Mixture-of-Agents-style aggregation may not be robust to lower-quality inputs and that single-teacher sampling is a safer alternative, reporting instead that FusioN remains fairly robust to weaker pools and that single-teacher sampling actually performs substantially worse in its own ablations, a direct, specific point of disagreement with that prior finding rather than an unexamined restatement of it. Within this corpus, the synthetic-data-generation half of this paper's results connects directly to [2509.14786] (Pre-training under infinite compute, reviewed earlier in this same batch), since both papers find that combining information from multiple independently-generated samples (via FusioN's fusion here, versus explicit ensembling and distillation there) before using the result for training produces a demonstrably better outcome than treating those samples as competitors and keeping only one, a shared, cross-validated theme (aggregation beats selection, or ensembling beats picking a single "winner") emerging independently from two different experimental traditions (multilingual synthetic instruction data versus pre-training scaling laws) within this same reading batch.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central comparative claim, that FusioN outperforms BoN across a genuinely broad sweep of models, languages, and both major use cases, is supported by a substantial and methodologically careful body of evidence: a strong (not strawman) reward-model baseline, an oracle upper-bound comparison that FusioN is shown to exceed in specific cases, controlled downstream fine-tuning experiments holding all other factors fixed, statistical significance testing on the translation results, and a targeted bias check for the most obvious LLM-judge confound specific to this setup (the fusor favoring its own prior outputs). The mechanism analysis (fusor-scale threshold, robustness to weaker pools, attribution tracing) goes beyond simply reporting that FusioN wins to investigating specifically why and under what conditions, a real strength relative to a paper that only reported aggregate win-rates.

The clearest limitation is that the paper's headline framing ("FusioN consistently outperforms BoN," repeated in the abstract and conclusion) is in tension with its own reported exception on closed-ended math reasoning, where BoN retains a small but consistent edge in test-time scaling on MGSM, and where FusioN-trained models actually underperform both BoN-trained models and the fusor's own standalone accuracy in the reasoning-fine-tuning setting on the same benchmark. The paper discloses this exception candidly in an appendix section and offers a plausible, appropriately hedged explanation (either that closed-ended verifiable-answer domains are poorly suited to generative synthesis, or a possible training-data contamination confound with the popular s1k dataset), which is good scientific practice, but the word "consistently" in the abstract's summary of results does not fully anticipate a reader who only sees the headline claim, and a more precise framing would scope the consistency claim to open-ended and translation tasks specifically rather than to aggregation broadly. A second, more technical limitation is that the paper's efficiency and practicality claims are argued primarily in terms of sample count rather than compute cost; since FusioN requires a single large-context call through a fusor model that must itself be large enough (per the paper's own scale-threshold finding) to work well, while BoN can pair many cheap parallel samples with a comparatively lightweight, purpose-trained scalar reward model, a full cost-matched comparison (total FLOPs, latency, or dollar cost per query) would be needed to fully support the "more efficient" framing as a practical deployment recommendation rather than only a sample-count comparison.

What remains well supported after these caveats: for open-ended generation and machine translation specifically, using an LLM to synthesize a final answer from multiple candidates reliably outperforms selecting a single best candidate via a strong reward model, across a wide range of languages and at both test-time and in synthetic-data-generation pipelines, and this advantage is large enough in some cases to let a smaller model's fused output beat single generations from a substantially larger, stronger model. Synthesis-based aggregation is demonstrably not a strict subset of what selection can achieve, since it is shown to exceed an oracle selector's own reachable ceiling in specific tested cases; and the technique's real, disclosed weak spot, closed-ended tasks with a single, precisely verifiable correct answer, is a genuine and specific boundary rather than a hypothetical one.
