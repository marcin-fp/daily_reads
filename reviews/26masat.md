## What this paper is about

Does adding more coordinating AI agents to a task actually help, and if so, when? This paper runs a genuinely controlled experiment to find out: holding task prompts, available tools, and compute budgets identical, and varying only the coordination architecture (a single agent versus four different multi-agent topologies) and the underlying model's capability, across 260 configurations spanning six realistic, interactive benchmarks and three major model families. Rather than concluding that multi-agent systems are generally better or generally worse than a single strong agent, both of which have been argued in prior work, the paper finds that the answer is strongly predictable from one simple, cheaply measured quantity: how well a single agent already does on the task. It packages this into a fitted, statistically stress-tested model for choosing when coordination is worth the overhead, rather than a universal scaling law.

## Extended summary

The paper first argues that much of the prior "more agents helps" evidence was demonstrated on static, non-agentic benchmarks (single-shot reasoning tasks like HumanEval, GSM8K, or MMLU) rather than on genuinely agentic tasks requiring sustained, multi-step interaction with an external environment under partial observability, building on and extending the "Agentic Benchmark Checklist" framework to make this distinction operational. It selects six benchmarks that satisfy this stricter, agentic definition: web browsing (BrowseComp-Plus), financial analysis (Finance Agent), Minecraft-style planning (PlanCraft), realistic workplace tasks (WorkBench), software engineering (SWE-bench Verified), and command-line tasks (Terminal-Bench). Five coordination architectures are compared as a deliberate structural ablation over two dimensions, orchestrator presence and peer communication: a single-agent system (SAS), and four multi-agent variants, Independent (parallel execution with no interagent communication, synthesized at the end), Decentralized (sequential peer debate with voting), Centralized (a hierarchical orchestrator), and Hybrid (orchestrator plus direct peer exchange), each formally characterized by asymptotic complexity in LLM calls, sequential depth, and communication overhead. These are tested across three LLM families (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic) at multiple capability tiers, yielding 260 controlled configurations with matched per-system compute ceilings.

The headline empirical result is enormous domain-dependence: aggregated across all six benchmarks and architectures, the mean multi-agent improvement over single-agent is 0.0% (95% CI −58.7% to +77.2%), reflecting extreme heterogeneity rather than a consistent effect in either direction. On Finance Agent, every multi-agent architecture substantially outperforms the single agent (up to +80.8% for centralized coordination), because the task naturally decomposes into independently researchable sub-questions (regulatory analysis, filings research, operational impact) that can be pursued in parallel and synthesized. On PlanCraft, every multi-agent architecture substantially underperforms (down to −70.0% for independent coordination), despite a nearly identical domain-complexity score to Finance Agent, because the task is a short, strictly sequential chain (look up a recipe, move an item, craft) that a multi-agent architecture artificially and wastefully decomposes into bureaucratic sub-steps, consuming token budget on coordination messages for a task requiring only a few genuinely necessary actions. This contrast leads to the paper's key mechanistic distinction: task complexity and task decomposability are different properties, and it is decomposability into independently pursuable sub-threads, not complexity per se, that determines whether coordination pays off. On SWE-bench Verified, where single-agent baselines already exceed roughly 45% for most models, all multi-agent architectures show mild degradation (−1% to −13%), consistent with a capability-saturation effect where high baseline performance leaves little room for coordination to add value. On Terminal-Bench, where baselines are below that level but the environment offers only two tools, results are mixed, with the paper attributing limited orchestration benefit to the environment's low tool diversity specifically.

The paper's core statistical contribution is a 20-parameter linear regression, built from theoretically motivated main effects and nine prespecified interaction terms (not an exhaustive search) relating performance to model capability, task properties (tool count, single-agent baseline), and empirically measured coordination metrics (efficiency, overhead, message density, redundancy, trace-level error amplification). This achieves cross-validated R² = 0.373 using a standard intelligence-index capability metric, improving to R² = 0.413 when replaced with a task-grounded "Agentic Capability Index" (each model's own mean single-agent performance across the six benchmarks), which the paper recommends as the primary capability metric going forward, noting the two metrics only moderately correlate (r = 0.45). Despite this middling absolute fit, the model selects the best-performing architecture in 87% of held-out within-domain configurations, substantially beating random choice (20%) or a capability-only baseline (54%). The paper is explicit and careful that this within-domain success does not extend to genuinely unseen task domains: a leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation yields a strongly negative R² (−2.09), meaning the model cannot predict absolute success rates for a new kind of task, even though relative architecture rankings show partial cross-domain stability (Kendall's τ = 0.89 in coordination-metric rankings across benchmarks).

Two robustness corrections are applied to distinguish confirmed effects from suggestive ones: cluster-robust standard errors (clustering at the level of the six benchmark "datasets," since several predictors such as tool count vary primarily at that level rather than being independently sampled 260 times) and Holm-Bonferroni correction across the 19 tested coefficients (correcting for testing many hypotheses simultaneously). Only the single-agent baseline coefficient survives both corrections simultaneously (Probust = 0.004; PHolm = 0.018), making it, in the paper's own words, "the only individual coefficient we describe as robustly supported." A second effect, baseline-scaled error amplification (architectures without centralized verification propagate errors more severely as task difficulty grows), survives cluster-robust correction alone (Probust = 0.030). A separately fitted baseline-by-team-size decision rule derives an empirical threshold near 45% single-agent success, beyond which additional agents are unlikely to help; this threshold correctly predicts the sign (positive or negative) of the multi-agent effect in 94% of 16 held-out SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench model-benchmark configurations (P < 0.001), but because the underlying interaction term does not itself survive cluster-robust correction, the paper explicitly presents it as "a validated selection rule rather than a coefficient-level scaling law." Several other directionally consistent patterns, a tool-coordination efficiency trade-off, a near-linear capability-performance relationship, and a baseline-by-team-size interaction, are reported as descriptive rather than confirmed once the same corrections are applied.

A detailed mechanistic analysis quantifies error dynamics per architecture from execution-trace token analysis: trace-level error amplification ranges from 4.4× (centralized) and 5.1× (hybrid) up to 7.8× (decentralized) and 17.2× (independent) relative to the single-agent baseline of 1.0×, showing that architectures with a genuine verification bottleneck (an orchestrator or peer-debate round that checks outputs before aggregation) contain errors far more effectively than an architecture with no cross-checking at all; centralized and hybrid architectures achieve 22.7% average error reduction versus the single-agent baseline, while independent multi-agent shows net error amplification (+4.6%) from having no correction mechanism whatsoever. A token-overlap analysis of agent rationales (classifying tokens as unique, shared, or semantically contradictory) finds high-performing runs show more shared-token entropy and much lower contradictory content (2.3% versus 8.1% in failing runs), though high redundancy (cosine similarity above 0.50) correlates negatively with success, suggesting a sweet spot (around 0.41) that balances shared grounding against genuine informational diversity rather than "more agreement is always better." Total reasoning turns scale as a clean power law in agent count (exponent 1.724, R² = 0.974), a super-linear rate the paper explicitly contrasts with the sub-linear exponent (about 0.76) describing how neural network performance scales with parameter count, naming this a distinct "agentic scaling regime"; extrapolating this relationship predicts communication cost reaching 9.5× to 21.8× the single-agent baseline by 6 to 10 agents, with per-agent reasoning capacity becoming "prohibitively thin" beyond roughly three to four agents under a fixed total compute budget. Token efficiency (successes per 1,000 tokens) drops sharply for every multi-agent variant relative to single-agent (67.7 for SAS versus 13.6 for hybrid, a 5× penalty), with real per-vendor dollar-cost figures reported (roughly $0.008 per percentage point of gain for OpenAI's most efficient configuration versus $0.024 for Anthropic's, a 3× spread).

A smaller, explicitly labeled preliminary investigation of 13 heterogeneous-capability configurations on one benchmark (BrowseComp-Plus) finds no evidence that mixing models of different capability tiers within a team bypasses the capability-saturation threshold: heterogeneous centralized configurations underperform their strongest-homogeneous-model counterpart by an average of 12.6 percentage points, while heterogeneous decentralized configurations show only marginal gains (+2.0 percentage points), attributable mainly to simply including the stronger constituent model rather than genuine complementary specialization.

The Discussion section is unusually extensive and self-critical, explicitly naming limitations including: scaling was tested only up to nine agents under a token-based communication scheme, leaving open whether larger collectives could show beneficial emergent self-organization or would simply hit communication bottlenecks; heterogeneity experiments varied only capability tier within one base architecture and family, not genuinely different model architectures or specialized fine-tuning; the tool-heavy-environment failure mode is directionally consistent but does not survive cluster-robust correction with only six benchmark clusters available; prompts were held identical across conditions for validity but were not optimized per model or family; SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench used only 20-instance subsets (versus 50-100 for the other four benchmarks) due to the cost of Docker-based evaluation, yielding wide (about ±20 percentage point) per-cell bootstrap confidence intervals, so individual comparisons there are underpowered even though the aggregate 94% threshold-validation trend across 16 configurations remains statistically robust; the economic viability of multi-agent scaling is fundamentally constrained by the current token-based natural-language communication paradigm; and the small number of benchmark clusters (six) makes the cluster-robust standard errors conservative by construction, with the negative leave-one-dataset-out R² directly confirming that absolute performance prediction on a genuinely unseen task domain is not currently feasible with this model.

## Learnings

Whether adding coordinating agents helps or hurts a task is well predicted by a single, cheaply measured quantity: how well a single strong agent already does on that task. An empirically derived threshold near 45% single-agent success rate correctly predicted the direction of the multi-agent effect in 94% of an independently held-out set of configurations, though the underlying statistical interaction behind this threshold does not itself survive the most conservative correction for non-independent observations, meaning it should be used as a validated practical screening rule rather than treated as a proven causal law. Before investing engineering effort in a multi-agent architecture for a new task, checking the single-agent baseline first is a cheap, high-value diagnostic.

Task complexity and task decomposability are different properties, and it is decomposability, not complexity, that determines whether coordination helps: two benchmarks with nearly identical domain-complexity scores showed opposite outcomes from adding agents (a roughly +80% gain versus a roughly −70% loss), because one task naturally splits into independently researchable sub-threads while the other is a short, strictly sequential chain that a multi-agent architecture can only pad with artificial, wasteful decomposition. A concrete, transferable diagnostic follows: does a task's optimal solution path actually branch into independently pursuable sub-threads, or is it a short, inherently serial chain that coordination structure can only add overhead to?

Architectures with a genuine verification bottleneck, an orchestrator or a peer-debate round that checks outputs before they are aggregated, contain and correct errors substantially better than an architecture that runs agents fully in parallel with no cross-checking step at all. Trace-level error amplification ranged from about 4× for orchestrator-checked architectures up to more than 17× for an architecture with no interagent verification whatsoever, and only the verification-containing architectures showed net error reduction relative to a single agent; a purely parallel ensemble with no aggregation-time check was measurably worse at containing errors than doing nothing extra at all, not merely unhelpful.

## Verification

This paper is one of the more rigorous instances in this corpus of explicitly separating what a statistical analysis found from how much that finding should be trusted. Two distinct corrections are applied to the same underlying regression for two distinct problems: cluster-robust standard errors address pseudoreplication (several predictors vary mainly at the level of only six benchmark clusters rather than being independently sampled across all 260 configurations), while Holm-Bonferroni correction addresses the multiplicity of testing 19 coefficients simultaneously. The paper reports naive, cluster-robust, and Holm-corrected results side by side throughout, and explicitly downgrades most of its own directionally suggestive findings (tool-count effects, intelligence scaling, the overhead-tool interaction, the redundancy-team-size interaction) to "descriptive directional patterns... not confirmed effects" once the appropriate correction is applied, rather than reporting only the more favorable naive-OLS numbers. It also runs a leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation specifically to test generalization to an unseen task domain, honestly reports that this fails (a strongly negative R²), and explicitly scopes the model's validated claim to within-domain architecture selection rather than cross-domain absolute-performance prediction. Separately, the ground-truth task-success labels underlying the entire analysis are themselves checked: domain-specific validators are compared against human raters via Cohen's κ (0.87 to 0.91 across four benchmarks, exceeding the conventional 0.80 threshold for strong inter-rater reliability), providing an independent reliability check on the labels rather than trusting an automated grader by assumption.

## Field context

This paper directly engages an active, contentious debate about multi-agent systems, positioning itself between claims that "more agents is all you need" and a proposed "collaborative scaling law" for multi-agent coordination on one side, and a concurrent body of work, including two named industry commentary pieces ("Don't sleep on single-agent systems" and "Don't Build Multi-agents"), arguing that single strong agents often match or beat multi-agent systems, on the other. Its central methodological contribution to this debate is the argument, and the demonstration, that much of the "more agents helps" evidence was generated on static, non-agentic benchmarks rather than on tasks requiring sustained environmental interaction, building on and extending the "Agentic Benchmark Checklist" framework to make this distinction operational for evaluation design. It also builds directly on a cited multi-agent failure-mode taxonomy (MAST, "Why do multi-agent LLM systems fail?"), extending that taxonomy from a qualitative catalog of failure types into a quantified, architecture-linked error-amplification analysis. Within this corpus, this is the most direct, large-scale, statistically rigorous empirical treatment yet reviewed of the "complex versus deliberately simple agents" tension, and its central mechanistic findings, that decomposability rather than complexity determines coordination payoff, and that verification bottlenecks rather than raw parallelism determine error containment, provide a specific, evidenced, transferable diagnostic framework rather than a general argument for either side of that debate.

## Critical discussion

The paper's statistical discipline is a genuine and unusually high standard for this literature, worth crediting directly: distinguishing pseudoreplication (addressed via cluster-robust standard errors) from multiplicity (addressed via Holm-Bonferroni correction) as two separate problems requiring two separate corrections, reporting naive, cluster-robust, and Holm-corrected results side by side rather than only the most favorable set, and explicitly running and reporting a leave-one-dataset-out validation that fails, together represent a level of self-scrutiny well beyond typical practice in empirical multi-agent-systems papers, and directly addresses long-standing concerns about missing controls and unexamined superiority claims in this literature.

At the same time, the paper's most heavily marketed number, the 94% sign-prediction accuracy for the capability-saturation threshold, rests on only 16 model-benchmark configurations drawn from two benchmarks that themselves use 20-instance evaluation subsets, versus 50 to 100 instances for the other four benchmarks, due to the cost of Docker-based evaluation, with individual per-cell bootstrap confidence intervals as wide as ±20 percentage points. The paper is explicit that "individual comparisons have wide bootstrap 95% CIs... so this rate reflects a robust aggregate trend rather than a per-cell test," which is an honest and correct characterization, but the evidentiary base for this specific headline claim is narrower than the overall "260 configurations" framing of the study might suggest to a reader who does not follow the analysis into its validation section (narrow-evaluation). Relatedly, the underlying statistical interaction behind the 45% threshold does not itself survive cluster-robust correction, and the paper is careful and consistent in calling the threshold "a validated selection rule rather than a coefficient-level scaling law" rather than a confirmed causal mechanism; this is a correctly drawn distinction, but it means the specific numeric threshold should be read as an estimate fit to these six benchmarks and three model families rather than a universal constant, consistent with the paper's own negative leave-one-dataset-out result. The heterogeneous-agent investigation, potentially one of the most practically consequential questions the paper raises, is explicitly labeled preliminary, covering only 13 configurations on a single benchmark; the paper is appropriately modest in presenting its finding (no evidence of bypassing the saturation threshold) as suggestive rather than conclusive, but this does mean one of the paper's most actionable-sounding conclusions for practitioners currently rests on the thinnest evidentiary base in the paper. Finally, the study was funded entirely by Google LLC, with a majority of the listed co-authors being Alphabet employees or stockholders at the time of the work; the paper discloses this transparently in its own Competing Interests statement, and its central finding does not obviously favor a self-serving narrative in either direction for a compute vendor, so this review notes the funding structure for transparency rather than as a specific basis for doubting the results, consistent with the paper's otherwise thorough statistical self-scrutiny.

What remains well supported: the finding that single-agent baseline performance is the most robust predictor of whether multi-agent coordination helps or hurts is the one individual result that survives both cluster-robust inference and multiple-comparison correction simultaneously, making it the paper's best-evidenced conclusion. The mechanistic account of why, task decomposability determining whether parallel exploration is productive rather than merely bureaucratic, and verification bottlenecks determining whether errors are contained or amplified, is directly supported by concrete execution-trace examples and a quantified, architecture-linked error-amplification analysis, not only by regression coefficients. The paper's explicit, repeated distinction between within-domain architecture-ranking validity (well supported, 87% correct selection) and cross-domain absolute-performance prediction (explicitly shown to fail) is a disciplined and correctly scoped claim that matches what the evidence actually supports.

## Relevance to us

This paper gives a concrete, quantified answer to a question our own framing already poses as open: whether simplicity or added structure wins depends on whether "the signal is tight," and this paper identifies exactly the cheap, measurable proxy for that, the single-agent baseline on the task itself, as the strongest available predictor of whether added coordination structure will earn its keep. This is a directly actionable design heuristic for any physics-reasoning system we build with multiple specialist components (for instance, separate derivation, verification, and literature-search agents): before investing engineering effort in a multi-agent architecture for a given class of physics problem, this paper's finding argues for first establishing how well a single strong agent already does on that problem class, since problems where a single agent already performs reasonably well are specifically the regime this paper found added coordination tends not to help and can actively hurt. The decomposability-versus-complexity distinction is a second, directly transferable diagnostic: physics tasks vary in whether they naturally split into independently pursuable sub-threads (checking several candidate mechanisms against data in parallel, for instance) versus being an inherently sequential derivation chain where each step depends tightly on the previous one, structurally similar to this paper's PlanCraft example; this paper's finding predicts multi-agent decomposition would help the former and actively hurt the latter, which is worth checking explicitly, task class by task class, before assuming a multi-agent physics-reasoning architecture is the right default. The error-taxonomy and amplification-factor analysis bears directly on our own verification lines: the finding that architectures with a genuine cross-check step contain errors far better than an architecture with no verification step at all, and that a purely parallel ensemble with zero cross-checking is measurably worse at error containment than a single agent, is a specific, quantified argument for always including some form of verification bottleneck in any multi-agent physics pipeline we build, rather than assuming parallel exploration alone improves reliability. Finally, this paper's own careful treatment of its heterogeneous-model experiments, finding on preliminary evidence that simply mixing capability tiers does not bypass the capability-saturation ceiling, is a useful caution against assuming that combining a frontier model with smaller specialist models will automatically outperform the frontier model alone; if we build a heterogeneous specialist federation, this paper's preliminary result suggests the benefit would need to come from genuine complementary specialization or architectural diversity, not merely from including a capability gradient across team members.
