## What this paper is about

There are dozens of ways to nudge an LLM's behavior — rewrite the prompt, fine-tune the weights, add a vector to its hidden activations, or reshape the decoding process — but each comes from a different research lineage with its own interface and assumptions, making it hard to compare them fairly or combine them. This paper introduces AI Steerability 360, an open-source Python library from IBM Research that puts all of these under one roof: a shared taxonomy of four "control surfaces" (input, structural, state, output), a common `SteeringPipeline` interface that lets multiple steering methods be composed into a single operation, and `UseCase`/`Benchmark` classes for evaluating and comparing steering methods on a task under both fixed and swept-parameter settings.

## Extended summary

The toolkit organizes steering methods by where in the model's computation they intervene. Input controls (`p_θ(σ(x))`) modify the prompt before it reaches an unmodified model. Structural controls modify the weights or architecture themselves (fine-tuning, adapters, weight merging), producing a new parameter set θ'. State controls modify hidden activations/attentions at inference time via hooks inserted into the forward pass, without persisting any change to the model. Output controls (`d(p_θ)(x)`) intervene during decoding — adjusting logits, constraining the output space, or using reward-guided search — leaving both weights and internal states untouched. Each category is a base class requiring the corresponding override (`get_prompt_adapter`, `steer`, `get_hooks`, `generate` respectively), and a `SteeringPipeline` object provides the common interface (`steer()` for any needed training/fitting, `generate()` for inference) through which one or several controls — potentially spanning different categories — act on a base Hugging Face model.

For state-based controls specifically (the paper's main worked examples), the toolkit factors activation steering into four reusable components: an estimator (learns a steering direction from data, e.g., mean-difference over contrastive pairs), a selector (chooses where to intervene — which layer/head, with what threshold), a transform (how the modification is applied to hidden states, e.g., additive), and a gate (a per-step decision about whether the transform fires at all). Three implemented methods — ActAdd, Inference-Time Intervention (ITI), and Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) — are shown to differ only in which estimator/selector/transform they plug into this shared pattern (e.g., CAA uses a mean-difference estimator over contrastive pairs plus an additive transform at a fixed layer, while ITI trains per-head probes and steers only the top-K most predictive heads). A single worked example demonstrates CAA reducing a documented sycophantic response (a Llama-2-7b-chat model agreeing with a fictitious "renowned researcher" author-persona's stated opinion) into a more balanced, non-committal response by subtracting a sycophancy-contrast direction at layer 15.

The `UseCase` and `Benchmark` classes support comparing steering pipelines on a task: a `UseCase` defines how evaluation data maps to generations and how those generations are scored (mixing "standard" metrics like perplexity with "LLM-as-a-judge" and custom task-specific metrics), while a `Benchmark` runs one or more named `SteeringPipeline`s against a `UseCase` — either with fixed control parameters, or with parameters swept via a `ControlSpec` object (supporting explicit lists, Cartesian grids, or functional/lambda-defined sweeps) and repeated `num_trials` generations per input to account for sampling stochasticity. The paper's one quantitative benchmark result (Figure 1) sweeps the steering-strength parameter α of PASTA (a state control that rescales attention weights toward emphasized prompt spans) on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct for an IFEval-based instruction-following task, plotting instruction-following accuracy against a separate reward-model quality score across α ∈ {5,10,15,20,25,30}; the resulting Pareto-frontier plot shows a "sweet spot" around α≈10-15 beyond which both instruction-following and response quality degrade further.

A final experiment, described in text but with its actual results deferred entirely to an accompanying notebook not included in the paper, composes a state control (PASTA) with an output control (DeAL, decoding-time lookahead search) on a TruthfulQA truthfulness task with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, reporting that the composite pipeline "can yield more favorable truthfulness-informativeness tradeoffs than steering under each control individually," with a proposed causal mechanism (PASTA diversifies the response pool by amplifying the truthfulness instruction in representations, giving DeAL's lookahead search higher-quality beams to choose from) offered as a hypothesis rather than something independently tested in this paper.

## Learnings

The most transferable idea is the four-component decomposition of activation steering methods (estimator, selector, transform, gate) as a shared design pattern: rather than treating ActAdd, ITI, and CAA as three unrelated algorithms, the paper shows they are three different instantiations of the same four-slot template, differing only in which specific estimator/selector/transform/gate each one plugs in (e.g., CAA's mean-difference estimator with an always-open gate and additive transform, versus ITI's per-head probe estimator with a top-K head selector and a head-specific additive transform). This is a genuinely useful organizing abstraction for anyone building or comparing new activation-steering methods, since it turns "is method X similar to method Y" into a concrete, checkable comparison of four named components rather than a qualitative judgment.

A second learning is the explicit framing of steering-parameter search as a Pareto tradeoff between the targeted behavior and unintended side effects, rather than a single-objective optimization: the PASTA α-sweep result frames "how strong should the steering intervention be" as a two-axis tradeoff curve (targeted metric vs. general response quality) with an empirically identifiable sweet spot rather than a single best value, which is a more honest and generally applicable framing for steering-strength selection than reporting only the value that maximizes the targeted metric.

## Verification

None in the sense of independently validated empirical claims about steering methods themselves — this is a system/tool paper, and its own worked examples (the CAA sycophancy anecdote, the PASTA α-sweep, the PASTA+DeAL composite claim) function as illustrations of the toolkit's capabilities rather than as claims the paper itself sets out to rigorously establish and defend. The one place the paper's own text distinguishes an illustrated result from a broader claim is the composite-steering finding, which is explicitly hedged as "Our hypothesis is that..." for the proposed causal mechanism (PASTA diversifying the response pool for DeAL's beam search) rather than presented as demonstrated — an appropriately cautious framing given that the underlying evidence for both the headline tradeoff-improvement claim and the mechanistic explanation for it live entirely in a supplementary notebook not included in or checkable from the paper's own text.

## Field context

The paper positions itself against a genuinely fragmented landscape of existing steering libraries, each covering only one control surface: state-based tools (TransformerLens, baukit, representation engineering, pyvene, EasySteer, EasyEdit2, DialZ, Neuronpedia's steer feature, and Chang et al. 2025's steerability tool) and weight-based tooling (Hugging Face's ecosystem, PEFT, TRL, Axolotl, LlamaFactory), explicitly noting that decoding-time/output control is "notably absent" from existing unified frameworks and that no prior tool spans all four control surfaces under one interface — a specific, checkable gap claim (rather than a vague "no one has done this") since it names the specific tools it compares against and the specific surface (output/decoding-time control) each one lacks. It also explicitly connects to a smaller, more recent literature on how composed steering interventions interact non-linearly (Scalena et al. 2024; Abreu et al. 2025; Han et al. 2026 on combining multiple steering vectors), positioning the toolkit's cross-category composition support (e.g., combining a state control with an output control, as in its PASTA+DeAL example) as extending that discussion beyond same-category (activation-vector-only) composition to composition across fundamentally different intervention mechanisms. Several of the paper's own authors have previously published individual steering methods now implemented in the toolkit (e.g., Lee et al. 2024's conditional activation steering, Miehling et al. 2025's prompt-steerability evaluation, Ko et al. 2024's self-detoxification), so the toolkit can also be read as an integration and standardization effort consolidating this research group's own prior methods alongside external ones under a common interface.

## Critical discussion

As a system/tool paper, the appropriate scrutiny concerns how well the paper's own illustrative results support its stated capabilities, rather than whether a scientific hypothesis is proven. On that standard, the paper's evidentiary base is thin relative to what its own framing implies the toolkit is for: the abstract and introduction position the toolkit as enabling "comprehensive evaluation and comparison of steering methods" and analysis of "steering trade-offs," yet the paper itself shows exactly one quantitative benchmark result (the PASTA α-sweep, a single model, single task, single steering method swept over one parameter) and one qualitative single-example anecdote (the CAA sycophancy case, explicitly noted as having "a wider set of prompts" tested only in an external notebook). The composite-steering claim — arguably the paper's most interesting result, since it demonstrates cross-control-surface composition working better than either control alone, which is the toolkit's most novel structural capability — is stated with a specific proposed mechanism but its supporting quantitative evidence is entirely offloaded to a notebook outside the paper, so a reader of the paper alone cannot verify the magnitude, statistical reliability, or robustness of this claim; it should be read as a pointer to where the evidence lives rather than as evidence presented and defended within the document being reviewed.

This limitation is consistent with the paper's own Limitations section, which is candid about a different but related scope constraint: the toolkit currently depends on Hugging Face `transformers` for the inference-time hooks state-control methods require, which the authors state is measurably slower than optimized inference runtimes like vLLM, constraining "the practicality of running larger scale experiments/comparisons" — an honest, specific explanation (rather than a vague caveat) for why the paper's own demonstrated benchmarks are necessarily small in scope, and one that plausibly accounts for why the shown results are single-model, single-task illustrations rather than the broad, standardized comparison across steering methods that the toolkit's stated purpose would otherwise invite. The Ethical Considerations section is similarly direct rather than perfunctory: it explicitly names the dual-use risk that steering tools can be used to make a model comply with harmful requests, states plainly that such mechanisms "are already being exploited in the wild," and flags an "unknown unknowns" risk specific to the toolkit's own design — that a user benchmarking one targeted behavior may miss unintended side effects along dimensions they didn't think to monitor — naming this as an open problem for future toolkit development rather than claiming the current `Benchmark` class's custom-metric support already solves it.

What remains well supported: the four-control-surface taxonomy and the shared estimator/selector/transform/gate decomposition for activation steering are conceptual/organizational contributions that can be evaluated on their own clarity and coverage rather than requiring extensive empirical validation, and the paper does substantiate the specific comparative claim that existing tools are fragmented by control surface with a concrete, named list of prior tools and the specific gap (unified cross-surface control, decoding-time steering) each one leaves open. The PASTA α-sweep result, while narrow in scope, is a genuine, shown-in-paper demonstration of the toolkit's variable-control benchmarking feature actually producing an interpretable tradeoff curve, which is sufficient to establish that this specific piece of toolkit functionality works as described, even though it does not establish anything general about PASTA or steering methods more broadly.
