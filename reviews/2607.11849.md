Citation: Kong, L., Wu, Z., Gu, Y., Zhao, H., Huang, W., Sun, S., Xiong, Z., Zhang, X., Zhao, S., Wang, Y., Xu, D., Zhang, W., and Chen, K. AdvancedMathBench: A Benchmark Suite for Advanced Mathematical Proof Generation and Verification. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11849

## What this paper is about

Most mathematics benchmarks for language models grade a final answer, and most of the proof benchmarks that exist are at competition level. This paper builds a benchmark for writing and for checking natural-language proofs at the level of undergraduate course exams and doctoral qualifying exams. It has two parts. ProverBench is 245 proof problems that models must prove. VerifierBench is 888 model-written proofs, each labelled by experts, that models must judge valid or invalid while explaining why.

Grading free-form proofs at scale is the hard part, so the authors train their own proof grader. It starts from an open 35B model and is trained with reinforcement learning on about 3,200 labelled proofs. A proof counts as correct only if eight independent grading passes all accept it. The headline claims: the best generator (GPT-5.5-xhigh) proves 64.5% of undergraduate and 48.9% of qualifying-exam problems. The best verifier (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) reaches only 65.1 balanced F1 once its explanation, not just its verdict, has to match the expert. Models mostly fail by accepting flawed proofs.

## Extended summary

Construction (Sec. 3.1). Problems come from four sources: undergraduate course exams at Fudan, Peking, and Shanghai Jiao Tong universities; doctoral qualifying exams from universities including Stanford, UCLA, Tsinghua, and Johns Hopkins; all tracks of the S.-T. Yau College Student Mathematics Contest; and textbook exercises. PDFs are parsed and answer-centric formats (true/false, multiple choice, fill-in-the-blank) are removed. Difficulty is pre-screened with verifier uncertainty. Unnamed "top-tier LLMs" write several proofs per problem, Intern-S2-Preview-35B grades each proof several times, and uncertainty is the entropy of those repeated verdicts. Problems whose proofs are consistently judged correct are dropped as easy. At the proof level, trivially judged proofs are dropped and verifier-uncertain ones kept. Experts with PhD-level training then check the statements, verify or revise the reference solutions, and remove "ambiguous, contaminated, overly simple, noisy, or ill-posed" problems and malformed proofs. The method for detecting contamination is not described.

ProverBench (Sec. 3.2, App. A.1). The UG split has 200 problems from undergraduate exams and textbooks: probability 33, mathematical analysis 33, differential equations 32, advanced algebra 28, statistics 23, advanced analysis 16, geometry 13, discrete mathematics 9, numerical analysis 7, graph theory 3, operations research 3. The QE split has 45 problems from qualifying exams, graduate textbooks, and the Yau contest: graph theory 14, geometry and topology 12, algebra 9, analysis 5, applied and computational 5. Models write proofs as `<step>`-tagged sequences. The prompt (App. B.1), which tells the model that human judges will grade it, allows only well-known named theorems and asks the model to state uncertainty rather than make unsupported claims.

VerifierBench (Sec. 3.3, App. C). The 888 problem, proof, and ground-truth triples are labelled with a "full-chain" protocol rather than first-error-only labelling. The argument is that the first local slip is often not the error that decides validity. Experts separate fatal errors (the global argument breaks: invalid theorem use, one direction of an equivalence, a necessary condition treated as sufficient) from recoverable errors (notation slips, minor algebra, small omitted justifications, sign conventions). They record a reviewer comment, the first fatal step, recoverable steps before the first fatal step, and suggested corrections. Errors after the first fatal step are not required. A proof is labelled fully correct only when there is neither a fatal nor a recoverable error. The meta-verification prompt makes the binary ground truth "correct if and only if" both fields are empty (App. B.3). Annotators see the problem, an optional official solution, the proof, and several model-generated verifications "as references", and are told not to trust them. Uncertain cases go to adjudication. No annotator count or inter-annotator agreement is reported. The worked example (App. A.3) is an isoperimetric variational proof. It has a fatal error at step 9 (it assumes a minimizer exists) and a recoverable sign-convention slip at step 5.

Verification task and meta-verifier. The model under test outputs an assessment, a list of errors, and a first-error index. The index is discarded for meta-verification (App. B.2). Its verdict is read from whether the error list is empty. gpt-oss-120b, as meta-verifier, compares the output with the expert ground truth and assigns one of four levels. EXACT_MATCH means both say correct, or the model finds all relevant expert errors with aligned reasons and no material false positives. BASIC_MATCH means it finds the first fatal error but misses some recoverable ones or adds minor false positives. POOR_MATCH means the verdict is right but the key issue is missed or distorted. WRONG_POLARITY means the verdict is wrong. "Rough" scores use the verdict only. "Meta-Verification" scores also require rationale agreement. The paper does not say how the four levels are turned into Meta-Ver TPR and TNR. Balanced F1 is the harmonic mean of TPR and TNR; the reported values check out.

Automatic verifier used to grade ProverBench (Sec. 4). The authors generate extra proofs for each ProverBench problem with models such as GPT-5.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro and with an iterative-revision method from their own prior work (ref. [11]). They select informative proofs by verifier uncertainty and have experts annotate them. With the original benchmark labels this gives about 2k training examples. Because uncertainty-selected proofs skew invalid, they add about 1.2k positives through proof repair. Expert correction suggestions guide the original models to revise failed proofs, and repairs that DeepSeek-V4-Pro and GPT-5.5 judge correct become positive examples. The verifier is Intern-S2-Preview-35B trained with GRPO. Its reward is the meta-verifier's level: 1.0, 0.5, 0.25, or 0. At grading time it runs 8 parallel passes and accepts a proof only if all 8 accept it (pessimistic verification, ref. [18]). Unlike the models tested on VerifierBench, the automatic verifier also receives the reference solution (App. B.2).

Setup (Sec. 5.1). Eleven models are evaluated at temperature 1.0, with up to 64k output tokens and the highest available reasoning effort. The number of samples per problem is not stated.

The authors read this as follows. The rationale-aware reward improves error detection over a polarity reward. Extra uncertainty-selected annotation sharpens rejection but costs TPR because it skews negative. Positive augmentation restores TPR. Pessimism makes the judge more conservative. The class split of the 94 proofs, whether they are held out by problem or only by proof, and whether the frontier baselines received the reference solution are not stated.

Author caveats. The paper has no limitations section. The caveats it does state concern prior evaluation (LLM-judge bias, first-error-only labelling) and the negative skew of uncertainty-selected training data.

## Learnings

Selecting training cases by verifier uncertainty buys hard examples but skews them toward invalid proofs, and a verifier trained on them learns to reject. Here, extra uncertainty-selected annotation cut held-out TPR from 66.1 to 56.8, and adding about 1.2k repaired proofs as positives lifted it to 81.4. The positives were certified by two frontier LLM judges, not by experts. The balance was fixed, but part of the label source moved back to LLM judgment.

Scoring a verifier by its verdict alone gives an upper bound, not a measurement. When the explanation must also name the expert's error at the right step, frontier TNR falls by about 12 points on average, while TPR stays the same by construction. Any verifier leaderboard that reports only accuracy or F1 on valid/invalid labels is probably inflated on the negative class. The size of that inflation here rests on an LLM meta-judge that was not validated.

Requiring unanimity across 8 samples of the same verifier is a cheap precision knob. On the held-out set it raised TNR from 77.0 to 85.0 and cost 2 points of TPR. The set has only 94 proofs, so the exact trade-off is loose, but the direction matches the design argument for pessimistic aggregation in reviews/2606.13473.md.

Tables 1 and 2 show that generating proofs and checking them are not the same skill. Claude-Opus-4.8 is the second-best UG prover (59.0) but among the most accepting verifiers (Meta TNR 35.0), while DeepSeek-V4-Pro is a mid-table prover and the best verifier. A strong generator should not be assumed to be a strong grader of the same kind of proof.

## Verification

Verification is central here, at three levels.

Expert ground truth. PhD-trained annotators grade each VerifierBench proof over the whole chain up to the first fatal error. They mark fatal and recoverable steps, write a reviewer comment, and suggest corrections. Uncertain cases are adjudicated, and unjudgeable cases can be flagged. The binary label counts any recoverable error as "not fully correct". Annotators see model-generated verifications as optional references. No agreement statistics are reported.

LLM judges. Frontier models act as prompted verifiers on VerifierBench, with no reference solution. They must return a verdict with a rationale and cannot abstain. Under verdict-only scoring their TNR ranges from 37.9 to 73.8 and their TPR from 66.9 to 96.4. Under rationale-matched scoring, TNR ranges from 30.7 to 55.8.

Meta-judge and trained grader. gpt-oss-120b, given the expert ground truth, grades whether each rationale matches it at four levels. The same meta-judge is also the RL reward for the trained grader. The trained grader (Intern-S2-Preview-35B with GRPO, given the reference solution, 8 passes that must all accept) is what issues every ProverBench score. Its reported reliability is one held-out set of 94 proofs: TPR 79.4 and TNR 85.0 on verdicts, and 79.4 and 69.1 with rationale matching. On that set, about one valid proof in five is rejected and about 15% of invalid proofs are accepted. No human re-grading of ProverBench outputs is reported, and the grader's error rates are not broken down by generator model.

The paper treats formal proof assistants (Lean, Coq, HOL, Metamath) as complementary and uses none of them.

## Field context

The authors place the work at the meeting point of three lines. The first is answer-graded mathematics benchmarks that have moved from GSM8K and MATH through OlympiadBench and Omni-MATH to U-MATH, HARDMath, FrontierMath, and MathArena. The second is natural-language proof evaluation (NaturalProofs, the Open Proof Corpus, ProofRank, DeepTheorem, IMO-Bench), which established that a correct final answer does not imply a valid proof. The third is process verification (PRM800K, ProcessBench, OPV-Bench, Hard2Verify), usually framed as locating the first error. Their stated novelty is advanced-level natural-language proofs, a trained proof judge instead of human grading or a general LLM judge, and verifier evaluation that scores the rationale, not only the verdict. The uncertainty-based selection comes from OPV-Bench and the iterative-revision method from ref. [11]; both references share authors with this paper. The pessimistic rule comes from Huang et al. (ref. [18]).

Against reviewed work (our inference): reviews/2606.13473.md (MaxProof) uses pessimistic minimum aggregation over several judges as an RL reward and a structured assessment/errors verifier output. This paper uses the same ideas for evaluation rather than training, and its Table 3 gives a small held-out, expert-labelled measurement of what pessimism buys. That supports MaxProof's premise that false positives are the costly error for proof judges. reviews/2504.16828.md (ThinkPRM) reports that a generative verifier fine-tuned on a small amount of filtered data beats the same or larger models used as prompted judges. This paper points the same way at proof level and against stronger frontier judges, though it departs from ThinkPRM's first-error step labels. reviews/2604.06802.md (Riemann-Bench) sits above it in difficulty, with 25 private research problems, double-blind expert checks, and answer grading. AdvancedMathBench sits between competition and research level, draws on public exam and textbook material, and grades full proofs with a learned judge. Its contamination exposure is correspondingly higher. reviews/2606.26294.md anchors a learned proof grader to IMO-GradingBench human grades. VerifierBench offers a comparable human-anchored test for graders on harder, non-olympiad material. The low measured TNR bears on the verification stage of the pipeline in reviews/2608.16753.md. The grader-error lesson of reviews/2609.13009.md, where most "model errors" on physics benchmarks were grader or benchmark errors, also applies to ProverBench, whose own grader rejects about a fifth of expert-valid held-out proofs.

## Critical discussion

The construction is careful where it counts most for a proof benchmark. The fatal/recoverable protocol is explicit, the prompts and rubrics are printed in full, and the paper does something many benchmark papers skip: it trains a domain grader and tests it against frontier judges on held-out expert labels. Even before pessimistic aggregation, the trained single-pass grader beats GPT-5.5-xhigh on the verdict-only metric (79.1 against 70.6 balanced F1, Table 3). That is the part of the comparison that does not depend on the meta-judge. The claim that a domain-trained judge beats a general one is supported on this distribution.

The weakest link is the grader's validation, which the abstract calls "strong agreement with human experts". It rests on 94 proofs with no reported class split, no confidence intervals, and no agreement statistic beyond TPR and TNR. The text does not say the proofs are held out by problem. The training data are, by design, proofs of the same ProverBench problems (Sec. 4.1), so the held-out test measures generalization to new proofs of seen problems, not to new problems. The grader receives the reference solution (App. B.2). The paper does not say whether the two frontier baselines in Table 3 did, and their Table 3 rates differ noticeably from their Table 2 rates on the full VerifierBench (GPT-5.5-xhigh TPR 63.2 against 78.9). Taken at face value, the operating point (TPR 79.4, TNR 85.0) means ProverBench numbers are pass rates under a grader that rejects about a fifth of valid proofs and accepts about 15% of invalid ones. Those rates cannot be transferred uniformly to Table 1: gpt-oss-120b's QE score of 2.2 is below the held-out false-accept rate. The grader's error therefore depends on the distribution, and per-model error was not measured. No sample of ProverBench outputs was re-graded by humans. Absolute scores and close rankings are therefore less secure than the paper's framing suggests.

The rationale-matched ("Meta-Verification") numbers depend on gpt-oss-120b, whose agreement with humans on the match levels is never measured. The same model is also the RL reward for the trained grader (Sec. 4.3). In Table 3, the Meta-Ver columns score the grader with the judge it was optimized against, which is circular. The verdict-only columns avoid this. On those, Meta-Ver-RL beats Rough-RL by 2.6 F1 (66.4 against 69.0) on 94 proofs, a difference of a few proofs. It is suggestive, not established. The mapping from four match levels to Meta TPR and TNR is also not given.

Several sources of same-family judging are left unexamined. gpt-oss-120b meta-judges its own VerifierBench outputs; it ranks last, so no self-favouring is visible, but the setup is not independent. More importantly, the ProverBench grader was trained on many proofs written by GPT-5.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro. Its roughly 1.2k augmented positives were certified by those same two models (Sec. 4.2), which are also the top generator and the top verifier in the tables. The grader may be more familiar with, and possibly more lenient or stricter toward, those families' proof styles. The paper does not test for this. The grader's base model, Intern-S2-Preview-35B, is also a ranked generator (27.0 / 16.7) and was the entropy judge used to pre-screen problems.

The label semantics shape the headline "over-acceptance" finding. The binary ground truth counts a proof with only a recoverable error, such as the sign-convention slip in App. A.3, as incorrect. Yet App. C.5 says such errors "do not by themselves necessarily invalidate the global proof". Some of the low TNR in Table 2 is therefore models accepting proofs that are globally sound but locally imperfect. The paper does not split negatives into fatal and recoverable-only, so it does not show how much of the "critical error detection" bottleneck involves critical errors. The "full-chain" protocol is also truncated at the first fatal error, which makes it closer to first-fatal-plus-earlier-slips than to a whole-proof audit.

The difficulty claims lack a control. Problems whose proofs were consistently judged correct were removed, and VerifierBench proofs were kept precisely because they made Intern-S2 uncertain. Both sets are adversarially filtered by construction, which makes low scores partly a design choice. The contrast with HMMT and USAMO (Figure 1) compares MathArena's scores under its own grading with an all-or-nothing, 8-pass-unanimous, zero-recoverable-error criterion on filtered problems. No benchmark was graded by both pipelines, so the gap mixes difficulty with strictness. The UG-to-QE drop is more robust, since it holds for all 11 models under one grader. Still, QE has 45 problems, so one problem is 2.2 points, and ties or near-ties (Claude and DeepSeek at 40.0, GPT-5.5-high at 46.1) are within noise. Some Table 1 entries (UG 53.3; QE 46.1, 33.5, 16.7) do not correspond to a whole number of solved problems at one sample per problem, and the sampling protocol is not stated.

Contamination is a real risk that the paper asserts away rather than measures. Qualifying exams, the Yau contest, and textbook exercises are public material, often with published solutions. Experts removed "contaminated" problems by an unstated method, and without a release no one can audit this. VerifierBench minus the 94 held-out proofs went into the grader's training data. So the released grader, if released, could not be scored fairly on VerifierBench, and the paper correctly leaves it out of Table 2.

There are also reporting issues. Annotator count and agreement are missing, and annotators were shown model-generated verifications, so anchoring is possible and unmeasured. The arXiv listing abstract (296 problems; 75.8 and 66.1) disagrees with the PDF (245; 64.5 and 48.9). Sec. 4.4 calls 82.1 a "Rough F1" where Table 3 labels it balanced F1. The identical Rough rows for gpt-oss-120b and Intern-S2 in Table 2 may be a copying error; the text does not let us tell.

What remains supported after these caveats. Frontier models judging hard, plausible, model-written proofs at this level reject flawed ones poorly: no model exceeds 74 on verdict-only TNR or 56 on rationale-matched TNR, and this holds across 11 models. Verdict-only scoring overstates verifier quality, and the direction is guaranteed by construction, although the 9-point size depends on the unvalidated meta-judge. A domain-trained 35B grader beats prompted frontier judges on held-out expert verdicts. Every model does worse on qualifying-exam problems than on undergraduate ones. Pessimistic aggregation trades a little recall for specificity. The absolute pass rates, fine rankings, and the claim that advanced proof is harder than olympiad proof should be read as provisional.

## Relevance to us

The paper matters for B12 as the best-engineered counterexample to our rule that verdicts should not come from an LLM judge. The authors put in about 3.2k expert and repaired labels, RL with a rationale-aware reward, and 8-pass unanimity. The result is still a grader that, on hard held-out cases, passes about 15% of invalid proofs and fails about 21% of valid ones. It is not audited per generator. That supports our reading of LLM judges as complete but biased, and it gives a concrete number for how far serious effort moves them. It does not settle whether precise but partial beats noisy but complete, because nothing here is partial: the grader must rule on every proof.

Three mechanisms transfer. First, uncertainty-selected hard cases skew negative and bias a trained checker toward rejection. That is the same trap an error-injection label factory walks into, and the repair-to-positive fix is worth copying, provided the positives are certified by something stronger than an LLM judge. Second, scoring a checker on whether it names the right step and the right reason, not just the verdict, is the right evaluation for our neutral flags and for a grounded skeptic (B13). A flag that is right for the wrong reason is not a useful flag. Third, the fatal/recoverable split is close to our need to separate intentional approximations and convention changes from real errors. The paper's own example files a sign-convention mismatch as recoverable. Its binary label nevertheless counts that proof as wrong, which is exactly the hard-gate failure mode our presentation rule is meant to avoid.

The low TNR is a measured version of what physicists told us: models are too ready to accept plausible arguments, and validation and pushback are what they value. The content is pure mathematics; the isoperimetric example and the differential-equations share are the only overlap with mathematical physics. The paper says nothing about physics-native checks, units, or regimes.
