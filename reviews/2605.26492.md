Citation: Sil Hamilton and David Mimno. *Elias in the Lighthouse, Again? Diagnosing Low Diversity in LLM Stories*. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492v1

## What this paper is about

The authors document an unusually narrow set of names, places, and occupations in stories produced from minimal prompts by four current LLMs. They then compare those tokens with literature, web, and one model's disclosed post-training data, arguing that neither raw frequency in training text nor in post-training stories explains their dominance at inference.

## Extended summary

The study samples 20,000 stories, 1,000 completions for each of five near-equivalent generic story prompts from Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.4-Mini, and OLMo 7B Thinking. It uses GPT-5.4-nano to extract names, settings, and professions, verifies that extracted spans occur in each story, then selects common tokens and uses changepoint analysis to define an 11-word Core vocabulary. At least one Core token occurs in 88.3% of stories. “Lighthouse” appears in 51.2%, “keeper” in 48.1%, and the names Elias, Mara, and Elara in 26.5%, 16.7%, and 13.1%, respectively. A name-location-profession triple appears in 49.1% of stories.

The authors compare Core-token rates with 2,700 contemporary English novels, Reddit WritingPrompts stories, and OLMo 3 pre-training and post-training data. The words are far rarer in the human corpora and web data: for example, Elias is reported as about 900 times more frequent in generated stories than in the contemporary-literature corpus. They train a FastText fiction classifier from 200,000 GPT-OSS-20B-labelled OLMo passages, reporting F1 0.84, precision 0.75, and recall 0.98 on 400 held-out CONLIT examples, to separate story-like from non-fiction text.

In OLMo 3's disclosed post-training set, 3,053 of 78,958 identified stories, or 3.8%, contain a Core token. DPO and RL story subsets have higher Core rates than SFT, but those rates remain only 8.0% and 10.9%. Topic modelling shows that Core stories occur across topics rather than forming one dominant training cluster. The authors therefore conclude that a small subset of post-training examples can exert disproportionate influence, and speculate that alignment may prefer innocuous generic stories over copyrighted-character or adult-content stories. They explicitly leave that causal explanation for future work. The experiment is monolingual, which they name as a limitation.

## Learnings

Low-diversity output can be measured through repeated concrete motifs rather than only aggregate lexical-diversity scores. Sampling across model providers makes the observed effect harder to dismiss as one checkpoint's quirk, but the phenomenon here is tied to an intentionally weak prompt distribution.

Small fractions of a disclosed post-training corpus need not predict their influence on aligned behavior. That mismatch is a useful warning for data curation, although this paper does not identify the training mechanism that turns a rare motif into a preferred one.

## Verification

The extraction pipeline uses an LLM to label exact spans for names, settings, and professions; the authors verify that each retained extracted span is present in its source story. Their fiction/non-fiction filter is evaluated against 400 labelled CONLIT samples, with reported F1 0.84, precision 0.75, and recall 0.98. These checks validate parts of the measurement pipeline, not the proposed causal account of post-training influence.

## Field context

The paper extends work on mode collapse and collective diversity in generated text by measuring repeated concrete motifs rather than relying only on aggregate lexical-diversity scores. Its cross-provider sample makes the shared generic-story pattern a field-level observation, while the disclosed OLMo data provide an unusually direct, though incomplete, view of how post-training data may relate to that pattern. The main unresolved step for the field is causal: the study identifies a frequency mismatch between training stories and generated stories, but does not isolate which part of post-training or decoding creates the attractor.

## Critical discussion

The descriptive result is persuasive for the sampled setting: the corpus is large, the five prompts are stated, and the same broad motif vocabulary appears across four providers. The comparisons with published fiction and OLMo data also support the narrower conclusion that simple token prevalence is insufficient to explain the output frequencies.

The causal headline is substantially less established. Only OLMo's training data are inspected, whereas the cross-provider observation cannot show that the same post-training examples or alignment choices caused the behavior in proprietary models. Within OLMo, the 3.8% Core-story rate and topic analysis demonstrate a frequency mismatch, not that these examples were disproportionately learned or that safety filtering selected them. A controlled post-training ablation, such as removing, upweighting, or varying these stories while holding the alignment procedure fixed, would be needed to separate data effects from reward-model, decoding, model-family, and prompt-template effects.

The use of five near-paraphrases of “write a story” deliberately establishes a weak-prompt regime, but does not measure diversity under prompts that specify genre, setting, or character constraints. The authors say similar behavior occurs for more complex prompts without presenting those results. Thus the evidence supports a real generic-story mode concentration and a worthwhile training-data puzzle; it does not yet support a broad claim that all creative writing, or alignment generally, is dominated by a small shared dataset.

## Relevance to us

This is direct evidence for the concern behind B16: output diversity can collapse into a few safe, high-probability themes even across distinct model providers. Its most useful contribution is methodological: inspect mode occupancy and repeated motifs, not only a single quality score. It also warns against inferring behavioral influence from corpus frequency alone. For a scientific system, the analogous failure could be a plausible, generic hypothesis repeatedly displacing rarer but better-supported alternatives, so diversity evaluation should preserve the problem regime and report which regions lose occupancy.
