Citation: Yuhan Wang, Zhengxi Lu, Yuchen Yan, Kaitao Song, Wenqi Zhang, Weiming Lu, Jun Xiao, Yueting Zhuang, and Yongliang Shen. PaperGym: Rubric-Centered Evolution for Research-Plan Generation. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31119v1

## What this paper is about

A research plan has no automatically checkable answer, so RL-with-verifiable-rewards can't directly train a model to write good ones — you need something that acts like a critic. Prior work builds that critic by extracting a rubric from a paper's own content, but if the question and the grading criteria are pulled from overlapping text, a model can raise its score just by paraphrasing the question rather than actually planning well. PaperGym fixes this by drawing the question and the rubric from disjoint sections of the same paper (question from the goal and background; rubric from the method and experiments), then uses that same rubric twice during training — first as private context for a self-distillation teacher, then as the actual reward for reinforcement learning.

## Extended summary

**Anti-leakage dataset construction.** Each arXiv paper (LaTeX source, not rendered PDF) is decomposed via an LLM map-reduce pipeline into four stages: Research Goal, Background, Research Method, and Experimental Design (numerical results excluded to prevent memorization shortcuts). The training question is synthesized only from Goal+Background; the reference answer only from Method+Experimental Design — a structural constraint that, measured by asking an LLM whether each rubric criterion can be inferred from the question alone, cuts criterion leakage to 3.73% on the resulting PaperGym-20k corpus, versus 11.90% (HealthBench) to 34.10% (ResearchPlanGen-ArXiv) for existing comparable resources. Each instance carries 10 instance-specific criteria (63.8% Method, 36.2% Experiment) generated from two complementary sources — question-only rubrics and answer-grounded rubrics, merged, deduplicated, and ranked — plus 7 fixed general criteria (reused from prior rubric-based work, itself drawing on HealthBench) covering completeness, specificity, justification, efficiency, and ethics. The final corpus spans 20,000 instances across CS (~50%), physics (~25%), and economics (~25%).

**Two-stage rubric-centered training.** Stage 1 applies On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD): a rubric-conditioned teacher (the rubric enumerates evaluation principles rather than one target answer, so it preserves a broader set of valid continuations than answer-conditioned distillation) evaluates the student's own on-policy rollouts, and the student minimizes KL divergence to the teacher. Stage 2 applies GRPO with the rubric as the reward: a frozen copy of the base policy model self-grades each candidate response criterion-by-criterion in two passes (specialized rubric, general rubric) with strict binary verdicts and zero partial credit, combined as 0.7×specialized + 0.3×general (a ratio separately validated by a sensitivity sweep). For the 1.7B model, whose self-scoring is judged unreliable, the 4B model from the same family scores its rollouts instead.

**Main results.** Across Qwen3-1.7B/4B/8B, the OPSD-then-GRPO schedule beats the untrained base model, SFT on reference answers, either stage alone, and the reversed ordering (GRPO-then-OPSD) on every one of five benchmarks at every scale, improving five-benchmark averages by +5.56, +5.04, and +4.81 points respectively. SFT alone is actively unhelpful at the largest scale (-0.96 average for Qwen3-8B), attributed to collapsing onto a single reference plan and losing output diversity. The reversed ordering loses on every benchmark at every scale (largest single-benchmark gap on PaperGym-Innov, up to -2.97 points), which the authors attribute to GRPO's cold-start instability without a distillation warm-up, and support with an entropy-dynamics analysis: OPSD raises output entropy (injecting knowledge, broadening the prior) while GRPO subsequently narrows it much more sharply and productively when applied after this warm-up than when applied first. A separate 0.5k-15k data-scaling sweep on Qwen3-4B shows monotonic improvement with more training instances. With training recipe held fixed, a model trained on PaperGym-20k wins 58.1% of three-way LLM-judged comparisons against 28.2% for the same recipe trained on RubricHub Science and 13.7% for the untrained base — isolating the data-construction contribution from the training method. The trained Qwen3-8B reaches 73.48 on ResearchQA, narrowly ahead of the substantially larger Kimi K2.6 at 73.19, and also beats three specialized scientific-task baselines (Intern-S1-Mini, Rebicon-Preview, S1-VL-RL) on the in-domain PaperGym-Innov/Design benchmarks.

**Ablations.** Rubric-source ablation confirms both question-only and answer-grounded rubric sources are individually insufficient (dropping performance when either is removed) and that both the innovation and experimental-design dimensions are needed. A model-scale ablation on the rubric generator itself shows that weakening the rubric-generating model degrades downstream training more sharply than weakening the extraction pipeline, which the authors read as evidence that rubric quality, not pipeline mechanics, is the bottleneck.

**Evaluation protocol.** Every number in the main results table (Table 1) — every baseline, every proprietary reference model, every one of the paper's own trained models, across all five benchmarks — is scored by a single LLM judge (DeepSeek-V4-Flash) via a rubric-coverage-per-criterion protocol. A separate reliability check (Figure 2) measures self-consistency (five runs at temperature 0) and pairwise agreement among four candidate judge models on binary rubric verdicts, finding all four self-consistent and even the smallest model (Qwen3-8B) reaching roughly 80% agreement with the largest (Kimi K2.6).

## Learnings

The disjoint-section construction (question from Goal+Background, rubric from Method+Experimental Design) is a simple, directly falsifiable, and demonstrably effective anti-leakage device: rather than post-hoc filtering criteria that look question-inferable, it prevents the leakage at the source by construction, and the paper backs the claim with a concrete, comparable leakage measurement across six datasets (3.7% vs. 11.9-34.1%) rather than asserting the improvement qualitatively.

The stage-ordering finding — OPSD-then-GRPO beats GRPO-then-OPSD on every benchmark at every tested scale, with a specific entropy-based mechanism (broaden, then narrow) rather than a bare performance table — is a transferable design principle for any two-stage training pipeline that combines a distillation-style warm-up with an RL refinement stage: run the entropy-raising, prior-broadening stage first so the subsequent reward-optimization stage has a richer distribution to sharpen, rather than narrowing a cold-start policy that then has nothing left to broaden from.

The observation that SFT on a single reference answer actively hurts performance at larger scale, specifically because a research question admits many valid plans and SFT collapses the output distribution onto one of them, is a concrete, mechanistically explained instance of a broader caution for any open-ended generation task with multiple valid solutions: imitation-style fine-tuning on one reference answer is not a safe default baseline, and can be worse than not fine-tuning at all.

## Verification

Central to the paper's data-construction claims but thinner for its evaluation claims. The criterion-leakage measurement is a direct, comparable, quantified check applied consistently across the paper's own dataset and five external comparators, which is a genuine strength. The judge-reliability analysis in Figure 2 checks self-consistency (same judge, five runs) and inter-model agreement (four different LLM judges scoring the same items) for the rubric-verdict protocol used during dataset construction. However, at no point in the extracted text is any LLM judge — the GRPO training reward's self-grader, the dataset-construction rubric judges, or the single evaluation judge (DeepSeek-V4-Flash) that produces every number in the main results table — checked against human or expert judgment, despite the paper's own Introduction stating plainly that "judging [a research plan's] quality requires expert review." Inter-LLM agreement establishes that judges agree with each other, not that their shared verdict tracks genuine research-plan quality; if several LLM judges share a correlated bias (for example, rewarding verbose, jargon-dense, or template-shaped plans), high inter-model agreement would not detect it.

## Field context

PaperGym extends a specific, recent lineage of rubric-driven RL for research-plan generation (Goel et al. 2025's rubric-driven RL for AI co-scientists, DEEPINNOVATOR, EvoIdeator), all of which the paper argues rely on sequence-level supervision and are vulnerable to the same-content criterion leakage it measures directly. Its two-stage training recipe explicitly combines two lines this paper's own Related Work separates: On-Policy Self-Distillation as a denser, complementary alternative to GRPO's uniform sequence-level scalar treatment of tokens, and the Rubric-as-Rewards paradigm's decomposition of quality into interpretable, prompt-specific criteria (while noting RaR's own known bottlenecks — expensive multi-call judging and reward-hacking vulnerability). Within this batch, PaperGym sits in the same broad space as this batch's other AI-for-science self-improvement benchmarks (Faraday/Replica, [2608.13331.md](2608.13331.md); OmniScientist, [2608.13558.md](2608.13558.md)), but at a narrower and more specific point in the research pipeline — plan generation specifically, trained with real RL rather than evaluated as a frozen agent — and its central methodological device (a rubric decomposed to avoid leakage, then reused as both distillation signal and RL reward) has not been directly tested elsewhere in this corpus.

## Critical discussion

The paper's most distinctive and best-supported contribution — reducing criterion leakage from double digits to 3.7% via disjoint-section construction — is measured directly, comparably, and consistently, and the resulting win-rate study (58.1% vs. 28.2% vs. 13.7% under an identical training recipe) is a clean design that isolates the data-construction contribution from the training-method contribution, since only the training data varies across the three compared models.

Three things temper the strength of the paper's central capability claims. First, the reward used for the GRPO stage is produced by a frozen copy of the very model being trained (or, for the smallest model, a same-family sibling model), self-grading its own rollouts against the rubric with no independent or human-calibrated verifier anywhere in the described pipeline — a same-family-judge setup that creates a direct opportunity for the policy to be rewarded for satisfying its own family's stylistic or substantive blind spots rather than genuine plan quality, and the paper reports no check (human or otherwise) that would rule this out (`same-family-judge`). Second, and more broadly, every single number in the paper's main results table — every baseline, every proprietary model, every one of the paper's own trained checkpoints, across all five benchmarks — comes from one LLM judge (DeepSeek-V4-Flash), with the only reported reliability check being inter-LLM agreement rather than agreement with human or expert judgment, despite the paper's own opening claim that judging a research plan's quality requires expert review; the entire evaluation apparatus therefore never actually meets the standard the paper sets for itself in its first paragraph (`missing-control`). Third, the headline claim repeated across the abstract, results, and conclusion — that the trained 8B model "surpasses the far larger Kimi K2.6" on ResearchQA — rests on a 0.29-point gap (73.48 vs. 73.19) on a benchmark scored by the same single, human-unvalidated LLM judge, with no confidence interval, variance estimate, or significance test reported anywhere for this or any other Table 1 comparison; given the benchmark's own scoring protocol allows zero partial credit and strict binary criterion verdicts, a fraction-of-a-point gap is well within the range that could shift with a different judge, temperature, or sampling seed (`overclaim`).

What remains well supported after these caveats: the leakage-reduction result and the stage-ordering/entropy-dynamics finding are each backed by a specific, controlled, comparably-measured demonstration rather than an assertion, and are the parts of the paper's contribution least dependent on trusting the absolute correctness of any single LLM judge (leakage detection and entropy trajectories are structural/behavioral measurements, not judgments of plan quality per se). The specific claim that PaperGym-trained models produce research plans of genuinely higher scientific quality than competing systems — as opposed to plans that score higher under this paper's own self-consistent but human-unvalidated LLM-judging protocol — is not established to the same standard.
