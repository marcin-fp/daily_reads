Citation: Jaewon Chu, Jinwoo Seo, Jaewon Cho, Jeehye Na, Yunyang Xiong, Youngdae Kim, and Hyunwoo J. Kim. AgentGrad: Intervention-guided Prompt Optimization for Multi Agent Systems. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08572v1

## What this paper is about

Multi-agent LLM systems are only as good as each agent's prompt, and "textual gradient" methods (natural-language critiques that play the role of a gradient for prompt updates) have become a popular way to optimize those prompts automatically. This paper argues that existing textual-gradient methods have two specific weaknesses: they cannot tell which agent in a pipeline actually caused a given failure, so they either update every agent's prompt at once or pick one arbitrarily; and once they have many small per-example critiques, they group them randomly before summarizing, which mixes together critiques for unrelated failure modes into an incoherent update. The paper's method, AgentGrad, fixes both by deliberately intervening on one agent at a time to localize the failure, then clustering critiques by shared failure pattern before abstracting them into an update.

## Extended summary

**Setup.** A multi-agent system (MAS) is a chain of N LLM agents, each governed by its own prompt; given an input, the system's output is scored by a task-specific reward function, and prompt optimization searches for prompts maximizing expected reward within a fixed rollout budget. AgentGrad has two stages, addressing gradient extraction and gradient aggregation respectively.

**Sequential intervention (gradient extraction).** For each training-set failure (an example scoring below max reward), AgentGrad walks backward through the agent chain (reverse execution order, motivated by the observation that failures concentrate in later agents, which reduces the expected number of interventions needed), injecting a hint into one agent's prompt at a time. The hint is built from the ground-truth answer or output constraints, used only at training time (never at inference). If intervening on agent n resolves the failure, that agent is identified as the target for that failure and the failure is removed from the unresolved pool; failures still unresolved after all agents are tried are treated as hard cases and deferred to a later round rather than discarded, since the failure set is reconstructed from the training set every round under the (possibly improved) current prompt set. The intervention-corrected output serves as an agent-level pseudo-label: comparing it against the original failed output under the same input isolates the specific behavioral change needed, which an LLM gradient extractor turns into a sample-level textual gradient, without requiring an explicit loss computed from the system-level output.

**Semantic textual gradient abstraction (gradient aggregation).** Rather than randomly grouping sample-level gradients (which the paper argues mixes unrelated failure modes and produces incoherent updates), an aggregator LLM clusters each target agent's sample-level gradients by shared corrective pattern and abstracts each cluster into one generalized gradient, using a soft, cyclically-scheduled lower bound on cluster size (e.g. 5 to 3 to 1 to 5) to alternate between coarser and finer abstraction over the course of optimization. Prompts are then updated agent by agent, in decreasing order of the cluster size behind each candidate update; a candidate is accepted only if it first improves performance on its own semantic minibatch and then also improves performance on a separate held-out validation set, otherwise it is discarded.

**Main results.** Evaluated on five MAS benchmarks (HotpotQA multi-hop QA, HoVer claim verification, IFBench instruction following, PUPA privacy-conscious delegation, MATH) with pipelines, splits, and reward functions adopted from prior work (GEPA for the first four, a separate math multi-agent paper for MATH), against MIPROv2, TextGrad, and GEPA, using either GPT-5-mini or Qwen3-8B as both the task model and every method's optimizer component (mean plus-minus standard error over three seeds). With GPT-5-mini, AgentGrad outperforms every baseline on every one of the five benchmarks, averaging +11.76 points over the no-optimization baseline versus GEPA's +9.24. With Qwen3-8B, AgentGrad achieves the largest average improvement (+9.67 vs. GEPA's +7.62), though Table 2 shows TextGrad numerically ahead of AgentGrad on IFBench specifically (42.52 vs. 41.42).

**Ablation, speed, and transfer.** Progressively adding sequential-intervention target identification (TI), agent-level supervision (AS), and semantic abstraction (STGA) to a vanilla textual-gradient baseline improves both HotpotQA and PUPA at every step (Table 3), and the full combination is best. AgentGrad is the fastest method on all five GPT-5-mini benchmarks, averaging 136 minutes versus GEPA's 337 (2.5x) and TextGrad's 647 (4.7x); the paper attributes this to a higher minibatch-improvement ratio (fraction of candidate updates that improve their own semantic minibatch and thus trigger a validation check: 0.72 for AgentGrad vs. 0.44 TextGrad and 0.28 GEPA), and a component-level breakdown attributes most of that ratio gain to TI and AS while STGA trades a small minibatch-ratio reduction for a higher validation-improvement ratio (more accepted updates that actually generalize). A transferability check (Table 5) applies prompts optimized on each of the five source benchmarks to a different, related target benchmark (e.g. HotpotQA-optimized prompts on 2WikiMultiHopQA) with no further optimization; AgentGrad's prompts transfer best on all five target benchmarks.

## Learnings

Turning a failure-attribution technique (which agent is responsible for this failure) into a supervision signal is a specific, transferable move: rather than treating intervention purely as a diagnostic tool, AgentGrad uses the intervention-corrected output itself as an agent-level pseudo-label, converting attribution evidence directly into a localized training target without needing an explicit system-level loss function, which is a distinct technique from either purely-diagnostic failure attribution or purely score-driven textual-gradient extraction.

Clustering per-example feedback by shared corrective pattern before summarizing it, rather than summarizing over arbitrary or random groupings, is a general fix for a specific failure mode in any LLM-generated-feedback aggregation pipeline: mixing unrelated corrective signals into one summary can produce an update that is internally incoherent because it is trying to satisfy contradictory pressures at once, and the paper's ablation (STGA's contribution isolated from TI/AS in Table 3 and Figure 4) is a clean demonstration that the aggregation step, not just the extraction step, meaningfully affects the outcome.

The paper's account of why AgentGrad is also faster, not just more accurate, is a useful mechanistic explanation rather than an unexplained side benefit: a higher fraction of candidate updates pass the cheap minibatch check before the expensive validation-set rollout is spent on them, so better-targeted gradients translate directly into fewer wasted rollouts, tying the accuracy and efficiency gains to the same underlying mechanism instead of treating them as two independent claims.

## Verification

The paper enforces a genuine, non-LLM-judged acceptance gate for every candidate prompt update: a candidate is kept only if it first improves reward on the semantic minibatch it was derived from and then also improves reward on a held-out validation set, so no update reaches the deployed prompt set without demonstrating out-of-sample benefit, not just in-sample improvement on the failures that produced it. The transferability experiment (Table 5) is an additional, separate generalization check, applying optimized prompts to a related but unseen benchmark with no further tuning. All methods compared, including the three baselines, use the same backbone LLM for the task model and every optimizer component within a given experiment, which avoids one common confound (a stronger judge or gradient-extractor model favoring one method). What is not fully specified in the extracted text: the exact form of the reward function per benchmark (whether HotpotQA/HoVer/PUPA use exact-match-style metrics, an LLM judge, or a mix), since these are adopted from prior benchmark papers (GEPA, and a separate math-agent paper) rather than defined in this paper's own text.

## Field context

AgentGrad sits directly in the textual-gradient line of automatic prompt optimization it reviews in Section 2 (ProTeGi's original textual-gradient framing, TextGrad's extension to compound multi-component systems, GEPA's combination of trajectory-level reflection with evolutionary search, and MIPRO's Bayesian-optimization approach to joint instruction/demonstration search), and it explicitly connects to two further lines: intervention-based failure attribution and debugging for multi-agent systems (which it extends from diagnosis into an optimization signal) and self-generated supervision (STaR, Reflexion, Self-Refine), which it redirects from refining outputs or reasoning trajectories toward localized prompt updates for individual agents. Within this corpus, "[Meta-Harness](2603.28052.md)" (reviewed in an earlier batch) makes a related but structurally opposite argument about the same family of methods: Meta-Harness explicitly names GEPA and TextGrad as compressing feedback into scalar scores or short reflective summaries and argues this compression discards information needed for long-horizon credit assignment, proposing instead to give a search process full, uncompressed access to raw execution traces and source code. AgentGrad instead keeps the compression step but restructures it (agent-level attribution via intervention, then pattern-based clustering rather than random grouping), which is a genuine alternative resolution to the same diagnosed problem (naive feedback compression loses or garbles credit-assignment information) rather than Meta-Harness's more radical decompression approach; the two papers agree on the diagnosis and disagree on the fix. No paper currently in this corpus previously addressed textual-gradient prompt optimization specifically.

## Critical discussion

The core empirical claim, that sequential intervention plus semantic abstraction outperforms existing textual-gradient methods, is supported by a reasonably broad evaluation (five benchmarks spanning distinct task types, two backbone model families of different provenance and capability, a controlled ablation isolating each of the three components, an independent transferability check, and a same-backbone-for-all-methods design that removes one obvious confound), which is a more thorough evidentiary base than a single headline comparison table.

Two things temper the framing. First, the paper's own Section 5.2 states that "AgentGrad achieves state-of-the-art performance across five MAS benchmarks on both backbone settings," but Table 2 shows TextGrad, not AgentGrad, with the higher score on IFBench under the Qwen3-8B backbone (42.52 vs. 41.42); the paper's actual claim for Qwen3-8B, read carefully, is about the largest *average* improvement rather than winning on every individual benchmark, and it phrases the GPT-5-mini result more precisely ("outperforms all baselines on every benchmark") than the Qwen3-8B result, but the topic sentence introducing both results together elides this distinction, which is worth flagging as a case where the surrounding prose claims more uniform superiority than the paper's own table supports (`incomplete-reporting`). Second, every reported comparison (Tables 1, 2, 4, 5) is a point estimate plus standard error over three seeds with no significance test, so several of the closer margins, including the IFBench case above and some of the smaller per-benchmark gaps over GEPA, cannot be distinguished from noise using what the paper reports (`missing-uncertainty`); the evaluation is also limited to two model backbones and five source MAS pipelines borrowed from two prior papers' designs, so it is not established whether the gains hold for MAS architectures with different agent counts, roles, or coupling structures than the ones tested (`narrow-evaluation`). A further design question the paper does not directly address: hard-to-resolve failures are excluded from a training round once no single-agent intervention fixes them, which plausibly also raises the minibatch-improvement ratio the paper credits for the wall-clock speedup, independent of whether the retained gradients are qualitatively better attributed; the paper does not isolate how much of the ratio gain comes from this selection effect versus from more accurate target identification per se.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the ablation in Table 3 and Figure 4 gives each of the three components (target identification, agent-level supervision, semantic abstraction) an isolated, positive, and mechanistically distinct contribution rather than an unexplained aggregate gain; the double-gated acceptance criterion (minibatch improvement then held-out validation improvement) means every accepted prompt update has cleared a genuine out-of-sample bar; and the transferability results provide independent evidence that the optimized prompts are not merely overfit to their source benchmark's specific examples.
