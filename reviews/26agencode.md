Citation: Hitzig, Z., Massenkoff, M., Lyubich, E., Heller, R., and McCrory, P. Agentic coding and persistent returns to expertise. Anthropic Research, 2026.

## What this paper is about

As AI coding agents like Claude Code become widely used, a natural question is whether they mainly help people who already know how to code, or whether they let anyone with enough understanding of a problem get technical work done. This report, based on a privacy-preserving analysis of roughly 400,000 real Claude Code sessions from about 235,000 people over seven months, tries to answer that empirically rather than speculatively. It finds a clear and stable division of labor, people decide what to build and the agent decides how to build it, and a clear pattern in who succeeds: task-specific domain understanding, not general coding skill, is what predicts whether a session goes well, though the benefit of expertise levels off quickly once a person has a working grasp of the domain.

## Extended summary

The dataset covers interactive Claude Code sessions through the CLI, claude.ai, and the desktop app between October 2025 and April 2026, explicitly excluding headless/programmatic CLI usage and third-party IDE or SDK integrations, both because that usage is often non-conversational and because full session boundaries are harder to observe there. All classification in the report, work mode, decision attribution, expertise, occupation, and success or failure, is performed by having Claude Sonnet 4.6 read session transcripts, cross-checked where possible against independently recorded telemetry (for example, more than 90% agreement between sessions the classifier labeled as creating or modifying code and telemetry showing actual code changes) and, per the discussion section, against a separate "strong reference model," though the details of that comparison are not given in the main text.

Sessions are classified into nine work modes: four involve writing or maintaining code directly (building 25%, fixing 26%, testing and orchestrating 5% combined), one is operating software such as deployment and monitoring (17%), two are about deciding what to do (understanding an existing system, planning a change; 14% combined), and two involve work where code is incidental or absent (data analysis, prose or presentations; 13% combined). Most sessions are anchored to an existing codebase (48% modify existing code, 17% explore it, 14% create new code from scratch), with roughly a fifth touching no codebase at all.

To measure the division of labor, a separate decision-attribution classifier lists the meaningful decisions in a session, splits them into planning decisions (what to do, which approach, what counts as done) and execution decisions (which files to change, what code to write, which commands to run), and attributes each to the user or to Claude. On average, users make about 70% of planning decisions but only about 20% of execution decisions, meaning Claude makes roughly 80% of execution decisions. A structural analysis of session turns finds about 4 turns per session on average, with each user prompt setting off roughly 10 Claude actions on average (a long tail: about 2% of sessions average more than 100 actions per prompt) and about 2,400 words of output per turn; when users retain more execution control, Claude takes fewer actions per turn, and when Claude controls more of the planning, it takes the most actions per turn, so autonomy in planning and autonomy in execution activity move together.

A separate expertise classifier rates a user's apparent, task-specific expertise on a five-point novice-to-expert scale, based on how precisely directions are framed, what the user asks Claude to verify, and who tends to correct whom; the report stresses this measures task-specific competence rather than general skill or job title (a senior engineer's first Rust question is rated as novice-level, while a non-programmer accountant who specifies exact reconciliation rules and catches an edge case is rated expert at that task). Expert-rated sessions trigger more than twice as many actions per prompt (12 versus 5) and about five times the output (3,200 versus 600 words) as novice-rated sessions, a gap that holds within every work mode and task-value band and remains statistically significant (roughly +9% actions and +13% output per expertise level) in a regression controlling for work mode, task value, month, occupation, and model family, with standard errors clustered by user.

Occupation is inferred from session context (project files, referenced artifacts such as legal filings or clinical data, and vocabulary), explicitly instructed not to treat coding activity itself as evidence of a coding occupation (a lawyer's contract-flagging script is classified as a legal-occupation session even though the work is mostly code); about 70% of sessions receive an occupation label, with Computer and Mathematical Occupations the largest group, followed by Business and Financial Operations, Arts/Design/Media, Management, and Life/Physical/Social Sciences, with management, sales, and legal occupations the fastest-growing non-software groups.

Over the seven-month window, the composition of work shifted: the share of sessions fixing broken code fell from 33% to 19%, while operating software rose from 14% to 21% and writing and data-analysis sessions roughly doubled. Estimated per-session economic value, calibrated by comparing each session's work to freelance-marketplace job postings and explicitly treated as a relative rather than absolute measure, rose about 27% overall, with building, operating, and fixing tasks each growing roughly a third or more in estimated value.

Success is measured with two complementary transcript-based classifiers: a "judged success" classifier (succeeded, partially succeeded, failed, or no clear goal) and companion "success signal" and "failure signal" classifiers that score verifiable evidence (git commits and pull requests matching the work, passing test suites, explicit user affirmation for success; errors, failed tests, retries, or user pushback for failure) on a 1-5 scale. "Verified success" requires both a judged success and at least one hard verifiable success signal. Controlling for work mode, estimated task value, month, task subject, and occupation group, novice-rated sessions reach verified success 15% of the time and at least partial success 77% of the time, while intermediate-or-higher sessions reach verified success 28-33% of the time and partial success 91-92%, with most of the gain occurring between novice and intermediate rather than between intermediate and expert. Among sessions that hit trouble (a failure signal of 3 or higher), verified success rises from 4% for novice-rated sessions to 15% for expert-rated ones under the same controls, and novice-rated sessions are abandoned (judged failed with zero lines of code written) 19% of the time versus 5-7% for everyone else. A footnote flags a specific residual confound: experts hit trouble less often overall, but the troubled sessions they do have tend to be on harder problems (the average estimated value of a troubled session roughly doubles from the bottom to the top of the expertise scale), so part of the observed recovery-rate gap may reflect that novices get stuck on routine problems while experts get stuck on genuinely hard ones. Occupation matters far less than expertise: software/math-occupation users reach verified success in about 30% of sessions overall versus about 26% for other occupations (34% versus 29% restricted to code-producing sessions), a five-point gap that has neither widened nor narrowed over the study period, with every one of the ten largest occupation groups landing within seven points of software engineers on success in code-producing sessions; management occupations score slightly above software engineering, which the authors flag as possibly reflecting that managers are more likely to explicitly confirm success in a transcript (which is exactly what the verification signal looks for) rather than a genuine skill difference.

The report is explicit about its own limitations: it cannot observe real-world downstream outcomes (whether generated code is actually kept, used, or produces economic value); it excludes non-interactive and headless usage, described as "a substantial share of activity"; and all measurements depend on a model reading transcripts, which the authors state is difficult to validate at scale, especially since sessions can be too long and complex for human labels to serve as reliable ground truth. The discussion proposes specific future signals to watch, such as declining returns to expertise over time (which would suggest models are increasingly supplying the judgment users currently provide) or growing success rates among non-software occupations (which would suggest software production becoming ordinary work across fields).

## Learnings

In a large-scale, real-world sample of agentic coding sessions, the division of labor between human and agent settles along a specific, consistent axis: users retain roughly 70% of planning decisions (what to build, what counts as done) while ceding roughly 80% of execution decisions (how to build it) to the agent. This is a concrete empirical anchor for what "typical current-generation human-agent collaboration" looks like in practice, distinct from either full automation or fully manual work, and a useful baseline against which to compare collaboration patterns in other domains.

Task-specific domain expertise, rather than general technical skill, predicts success with an agentic coding tool, and the returns to that expertise are front-loaded: controlling for task type, value, occupation, and time, a session rated as having at least intermediate expertise reaches roughly twice the verified-success rate of a novice-rated session, but the further jump from intermediate to expert yields comparatively little additional benefit. A working grasp of a domain appears to capture most of the achievable value; deep specialization adds only a modest amount beyond that.

When a session hits trouble, the sharpest difference between novice and more experienced users is not the ability to eventually succeed but the willingness and ability to keep going: novice-rated users abandon a troubled session (giving up with no code written) at roughly three to four times the rate of everyone else. This suggests that a meaningful part of the practical value of domain expertise in agentic collaboration lies specifically in diagnosing what went wrong and redirecting the agent, not only in specifying the task well at the outset, which points to session recovery, not just initial task success, as a distinct thing worth designing and measuring for.

## Verification

The report's own measurement pipeline is worth describing as a verification exercise in its own right, even though its subject is usage analytics rather than scientific claims. Its central outcome measure, "verified success," is deliberately two-layered rather than relying on a single LLM judgment: a holistic classifier first judges whether the user's stated goal was met, and a second, more mechanical check then requires at least one hard, independently observable signal (a matching git commit or pull request, a passing test suite, or explicit user confirmation) before a session counts as verified rather than merely judged successful, an attempt to avoid treating an LLM's own narrative judgment as sufficient evidence on its own. The report also validates one of its classifiers against independently recorded telemetry (agreement between LLM-judged code changes and actual code-diff telemetry exceeding 90%) and mentions, without full detail, a comparison against a separate "strong reference model." The authors are explicit about the limits of this verification effort: every classifier in the report, including the ones that produce the verified-success statistic itself, runs on the same company's own model (Claude Sonnet 4.6), and the report states plainly that "classifiers remain challenging to validate at scale," with session length and complexity limiting how much human labeling could even serve as reliable ground truth. This is a case where the paper's own verification of its own measurement pipeline is real but incomplete, and the authors say so rather than asserting the classifiers are trustworthy by default.

## Field context

This report builds directly on the same authors' prior Anthropic work on autonomy in Claude Code sessions and on how Claude Code has changed work inside Anthropic itself, and it explicitly cites two independent, methodologically distinct studies of agentic coding usage (one analyzing Cursor IDE sessions, one using a separate public dataset of coding-agent sessions) as parallel lenses on the same emerging phenomenon, situating this report within a fast-growing empirical literature on real-world AI-coding-tool usage rather than presenting it as the first such study. More broadly, it is one data point in an active and unsettled debate about whether AI tools substitute for or complement domain expertise across knowledge work, and its central finding, that returns to task-specific understanding persist and may even be a precondition for extracting full value from an agentic tool, cuts against a simpler "AI levels the playing field" narrative. Within this corpus, its finding that users retain planning while ceding execution is a large-scale, quantitative, non-physics illustration of the same tension named in this project's own framing between "helper," "mentor," and "skeptic" roles for an AI collaborator, and of the interview-derived observation that scientists want a sparring partner whose decision-making stays with the person rather than an obedient intern; this paper offers outside evidence that a related pattern is already emerging in a large population of a different but adjacent kind of knowledge work.

## Critical discussion

The report earns real credit for actively working against the most obvious confound in its own observational design, that experts might simply choose easier or more familiar tasks, by comparing sessions matched on work mode, estimated task value, month, task subject, and occupation group throughout, and by naming a specific residual confound it could not fully control for: expert-rated users hit trouble less often overall, but when they do, it tends to be on harder problems (the average value of a troubled session roughly doubles from the bottom to the top of the expertise scale), so part of the observed "experts recover better" gap may partly reflect that novices get stuck on routine problems while experts get stuck on genuinely hard ones. Naming this specific, plausible alternative explanation, rather than only a generic correlation-is-not-causation disclaimer, is a level of self-scrutiny worth crediting directly.

At the same time, every statistic in the report rests on classifications produced by a single model, Claude Sonnet 4.6, made by the same company whose product is being studied and whose commercial interest aligns with a favorable "expertise amplifies use" narrative for that product. The authors disclose genuine validation efforts (telemetry agreement for the code-modification classifier, a comparison to an unspecified "strong reference model") and are candid that classifier validation at this scale remains genuinely hard, but there is no independent, non-Anthropic-model measurement of expertise, success, or occupation anywhere in the report to check the classifiers against, which is a same-family-judge situation running through the entire measurement pipeline rather than a single isolated step (same-family-judge). Separately, the evidence base is exclusively interactive usage of one company's one product over seven months, explicitly excluding headless and programmatic usage described as "a substantial share of activity" and all third-party integrations; the report's own framing repeatedly reaches beyond this scope ("what happens on Claude Code may be a preview of where knowledge work is headed," "if these patterns hold across the economy"), and while this framing is consistently phrased as a hypothesis to watch rather than an established conclusion, readers should weight the "preview of the whole labor market" language against the fact that the underlying evidence is a single vendor's usage data, not a cross-tool or cross-market sample (narrow-evaluation). The freelance-marketplace-calibrated value estimates are explicitly and appropriately flagged by the authors themselves as coarse and intended only for relative comparison, which is the right level of confidence for a fuzzy match between a session and a job posting, and is a well-scoped caveat rather than a hidden weakness.

What remains well supported: the core division-of-labor finding (planning retained by users, execution ceded to the agent) and the core expertise-success gradient (with diminishing returns above intermediate expertise, and a sharp expertise-abandonment relationship specifically) are demonstrated on a genuinely large sample with real, disclosed statistical controls and at least one specific, named residual confound rather than an unqualified causal story, and the paper's language ("these findings are preliminary," an explicit limitations section, a candid footnote about a possible measurement artifact inflating managers' apparent success) is consistently calibrated to the strength of the evidence it actually has.

## Relevance to us

This is not a physics paper and its findings should not be imported as established fact, but it is a useful, large-scale outside data point for the tensions in TAXONOMY.md between helper, mentor, and skeptic roles, and for Arc 6 on collaboration, where the goal is a sparring partner whose decision-making stays with the scientist rather than an obedient intern. A real population of hundreds of thousands of users, given a general-purpose agentic tool and free choice in how to use it, settled into exactly the division our own framing hopes for: humans keep "what to do," the agent handles "how to do it." That this pattern emerged from revealed usage rather than being designed in is a genuinely useful independent data point that our own aspiration matches at least one real-world equilibrium, not only our own preference. The specific finding that abandonment, not just lower peak success, is the sharpest gap between less and more expert users is a concrete, transferable design signal: for a physics-reasoning collaborator, the highest-value intervention for a less experienced user may not be raising their best-case success rate, but reducing the rate at which they give up entirely when a derivation or calculation goes wrong, arguing for investing specifically in recovery-from-trouble support (a clearer diagnosis of what failed, a suggested next step) rather than only in raw task success. At the same time, the paper's central limitation, that every measurement comes from the same company's own model judging its own product's usage with no independent external validation at the scale its headline claims are made, is a caution to apply symmetrically to ourselves: an internally generated, same-model-judged usage study of our own tools would be informative but should not be treated as an independently verified causal result without some form of external check, exactly the gap this report is honest about not having closed.
