Citation: Zong, Q., Liu, J., Shen, J., Tang, Z., Wu, L., Liu, Y., Wang, R., Wang, Z., Wang, W., Qian, C., Chen, X., Song, Y. Co-Evolution in Agentic Systems: Toward Self-Directed Evolution Beyond Human Design. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10299v1

## What this paper is about

A single agent that improves itself by learning from its own experience is still bounded by whatever fixed tasks, feedback, and counterparts it happens to be given: once it has extracted everything learnable from a static setting, further self-improvement plateaus. This survey organizes a fast-growing body of work on co-evolution instead, where an agent and at least one other adaptive component (a peer agent, an opponent, the task distribution, the feedback signal, or even the evolution process itself) mutually reshape each other over time, so that neither side settles into a static target the other can fully exploit and then stop improving against. The paper proposes a three-stage taxonomy tracking how much of the system is allowed to change, from agents co-adapting within a fixed environment, to agents and their environment jointly adapting, to a speculative frontier where the mechanism governing evolution itself becomes evolvable.

## Extended summary

The paper first gives co-evolution a specific technical definition to distinguish it from mere interaction or information exchange: at least two evolving units must jointly adapt such that each one's changes reshape the other's subsequent evolution, not simply exchange messages or share a static reward. It formalizes an agentic system as a pair of an agent collective and an environment, an agent as a model backbone plus a harness (memory, tools, skills, prompts, workflows), and an "evolution mechanism" that specifies what can evolve, when evolution triggers, how variants are produced, where evolution happens, and how evolution quality is judged. The three-stage taxonomy tracks the progressive removal of human-engineered constraints on this mechanism.

Stage 1, Agent-Agent Co-Evolution, covers mutual adaptation among agents within a fixed environment, organized into three patterns. Adversarial agents (tracing back to GANs and adversarial robust-control training, through competitive self-play in games, to LLM-specific attacker-defender safety training) co-evolve through opposing objectives, with the survey further distinguishing pairwise pressure from multi-source pressure (e.g., AlphaStar's league training against a diverse population of exploiters, or training a defender against a whole team of task agents rather than a single attacker). Collaborative agents co-evolve through shared goals, split into parallel collaboration (originating in multi-agent RL, extended to reward agents directly from their discussion turns, or to diffuse notes and skills through shared memory across independently-exploring agents) and role-differentiated collaboration (produce-and-revise loops between a solver and a verifier/critic agent, or capability-split teams like a researcher-and-engineer pair accumulating persistent shared experience). Evolving agent organizations extends this further to co-adapting role assignments and interaction structure itself, rather than only the agents' internal policies.

Stage 2, Agent-Environment Co-Evolution, extends adaptation to the environment. Task-space co-evolution covers both selecting which existing tasks an agent is exposed to (curriculum learning by estimated progress, regret-based selection targeting where the agent lags a stronger reference, or resampling tasks that match an agent's own recent failure modes) and generating entirely new tasks calibrated to the agent's current competence frontier (in tool use, search, GUI/computer-use, and software-engineering settings, including generating the verification functions or setup scripts a new task needs). Feedback-space co-evolution covers an evolving reward or evaluation signal, driven by preference comparisons (actively relabeling stored rollouts as a preference-learning reward model itself improves), by task outcomes (co-searching reward-function candidates and policy variants together, or evolving unit tests and surrogate verifiers from execution failures), or augmented by consistency constraints across critics or across time steps. Interaction-space co-evolution covers an environment that constructs an increasingly challenging world for the agent (executable-world generation targeting an agent's weaknesses, in gridworlds, robotics simulation, and open-ended games) or replaces the real environment with a co-evolving learned world model (e.g., a web world model trained jointly with the agent policy to support synthetic rollouts and look-ahead).

Stage 3, Meta Co-Evolution, is presented as an emerging and still largely aspirational frontier in which the evolution mechanism's own five adaptive decisions (what to evolve, when, how, where, and how to evaluate) become themselves subject to revision, driven by and reshaping a lower-level co-evolving system. The paper is explicit that most cited candidate work are single-entity "precursors" that make an evolution mechanism itself evolvable (e.g., evolving the mutation prompts that control how future prompts change, or evolving an agent's own self-modification machinery) but without coupling that change to a genuinely co-evolving lower-level system of multiple components; it identifies one paper (a framework that co-evolves task agents and their evaluators, with a meta-agent using joint feedback from both sides to guide subsequent evolution) as going beyond this precursor stage toward a fuller instance of the definition.

Beyond the qualitative taxonomy, Figure 4 presents a cross-paper quantitative synthesis constructed with an explicit methodology (detailed in Appendix C): rather than pooling raw scores across papers using different models, benchmarks, and metrics, the authors first perform all comparisons within each paper (only including a paper if it reports both a static/frozen counterpart and an evolving counterpart under the matched backbone and evaluation setting, deliberately selecting the controlled ablation variant rather than an arbitrary or strongest baseline when multiple options exist), then aggregate the resulting within-paper effects across papers. Panel A compares mean direction-aligned scores between static and evolving conditions across matched settings; Panel B shows the proportion of matched settings with a positive improvement (breadth) against the mean paired improvement magnitude (gain), colored by taxonomy stage; and Panel C normalizes reported performance trajectories across evolution rounds/epochs/checkpoints onto a common [0,1] progress axis (interpolating and, where necessary, digitizing values from published plots) and averages them across papers, with the paper explicitly noting the resulting shaded band reflects cross-paper heterogeneity rather than a statistical confidence interval. The stated finding from this synthesis is that Stage 1 and Stage 2 co-evolution methods improve performance across most of the sampled matched settings, but that the normalized-trajectory gains show diminishing returns and approach a plateau as evolution progresses, which the paper uses as its central motivating evidence that Stage 3 (allowing the evolution mechanism itself to change) may be needed to move past this observed bottleneck.

Section 6 discusses three open challenges. Dynamic evaluation argues existing benchmarks mostly measure final capability rather than whether co-evolving components' gains transfer to unseen partners and environments or how much each component contributes to joint progress, and specifically names evaluator exploitation, partner overfitting, and diversity collapse as failure modes that a high task-success score can mask, citing one benchmark (testing whether an agent can autonomously post-train a base LLM) as a step toward evolution-aware evaluation while arguing more is needed, such as pairing fixed benchmarks with historical cross-play, component ablations, and held-out evaluators. Scaling co-evolution argues current systems mostly study isolated local loops (a single attacker-defender pair, a single policy-reward loop) rather than many components changing together, framing the central open problem as deciding which components should change and how their updates should influence each other without becoming unstable or dominated by one component. Safety and governance argues that as co-evolution becomes more autonomous, evolving agents may develop attack strategies, tool-use patterns, or communication protocols that exceed human understanding and monitoring capacity, a risk the paper argues is sharper specifically in meta co-evolution because the system may also alter which behaviors are rewarded and preserved, and calls for governance mechanisms including sandboxed deployment, continuous monitoring, rollback to verified states, and human intervention points, while explicitly stating in its own Limitations section that it treats these only as desiderata rather than developing concrete protocols.

Appendix B provides a direct, specific comparison against nine adjacent surveys' own stated taxonomies, checking where (if at all) co-evolution appears in each: the paper reports that several treat it only as scattered examples with no dedicated section, others mention it only as a listed future direction or open challenge, one covers a related but distinct concept (model-harness co-evolution within a single agent, which this survey's own definition treats as evolution of one unit rather than co-evolution of two), and one (on self-evolving agents broadly) includes model-environment co-evolution as one of three top-level branches but does not cover agent-agent or meta co-evolution. Appendix A gives explicit definitional boundaries distinguishing co-evolution from adjacent concepts the paper wants to avoid conflating with it: self-evolution (only one adaptive unit, fixed learning conditions), meta-evolution (the evolution mechanism itself changes, but without a coupled lower-level co-evolving system), iterative/recursive self-improvement (a single system repeatedly applying or improving its own fixed procedure), and open-endedness (a possible long-term outcome of continuous novelty that co-evolution does not automatically guarantee).

## Learnings

The paper's core definitional move, requiring genuine co-evolution to involve at least two units whose changes reshape each other's subsequent evolution (rather than merely exchanging information, sharing a static reward, or one side unilaterally adapting to a fixed counterpart), is a transferable conceptual tool for evaluating any claimed "co-evolutionary" or "self-improving" system: it gives a specific, checkable test (does the counterpart's own evolutionary trajectory change as a result of the agent's adaptation, and vice versa) for distinguishing genuine mutual adaptation from a system that merely looks dynamic on the surface.

The explicit methodological practice used to build the cross-paper evidence figure, performing every comparison within a single paper's own matched static-versus-evolving conditions before aggregating across papers, deliberately selecting the controlled ablation rather than the strongest reported baseline, and clearly separating cross-paper heterogeneity (a standard-deviation band) from statistical confidence, is a reusable and methodologically sound template for anyone attempting a quantitative synthesis across a literature that uses incompatible models, benchmarks, and metrics, where naively pooling raw scores would produce a meaningless or misleading aggregate.

The three named failure modes specific to co-evolving (rather than singly self-evolving) systems, evaluator exploitation (a co-evolving evaluator being gamed rather than genuinely improving the thing it judges), partner overfitting (an agent specializing to its current, still-evolving counterpart in a way that does not transfer to a different partner), and diversity collapse (co-evolving pressure narrowing rather than broadening the range of strategies or behaviors explored), are a specific, transferable checklist for auditing any multi-component adaptive system, distinct from the failure modes usually discussed for single-agent self-improvement (e.g., reward hacking or catastrophic forgetting).

## Verification

As a survey, this paper's central "verification" is the internal consistency and specificity of its own taxonomy and its comparison against alternative organizing schemes, both of which are handled with concrete, checkable detail rather than only asserted: Appendix B names nine specific adjacent surveys and states, for each, precisely where (if anywhere) co-evolution appears in that survey's own stated taxonomy, rather than making a general, unfalsifiable claim that "no prior survey covers this." As a spot check for this review, one of the paper's own inline literature summaries (a task-space co-evolution method described as "resampl[ing] tasks matching the agent's own failure modes," Wang et al. 2026c) was checked against this corpus's own independent review of the same paper ([reviews/2606.10917.md](2606.10917.md), Role-Agent), and the characterization is accurate: that paper does have a model play an environment role that diagnoses an agent's failure patterns and specifically resamples training tasks sharing that pattern, matching the survey's brief description closely. The Figure 4 cross-paper meta-analysis is a genuine, if modest, empirical contribution beyond pure narrative synthesis, and its construction (detailed in Appendix C) follows sound comparative-methodology practice for a heterogeneous literature, most notably performing every comparison within a paper before any cross-paper aggregation and explicitly avoiding the appearance of a formal confidence interval where only descriptive cross-paper spread is actually being shown.

The main limitation, which the paper's own Limitations section states directly, is that Stage 3 (Meta Co-Evolution) is populated by very few genuine instances meeting the paper's own definition, so most of that section's discussion rests on single-entity "meta-evolution" precursors that the paper itself distinguishes from true meta co-evolution, making the third stage of the taxonomy considerably thinner in supporting evidence than the first two. The safety-and-governance discussion is explicitly acknowledged to remain at the level of named concerns and desiderata rather than concrete, evaluable protocols. As with any survey covering well over a hundred individual works, most literature descriptions necessarily compress each cited paper into one or two sentences; this review is not able to independently verify every such characterization, though the single characterization checked against an existing independent review in this corpus (above) held up accurately.

## Field context

The paper positions itself directly against a specific, named set of adjacent surveys (on LLM-based multi-agent systems, agent harness/scaffold design, environment scaling for interactive agents, and self-evolving agents broadly), arguing through Appendix B's comparison table that each treats co-evolution as, at most, a scattered example, a listed future direction, or one branch of a broader taxonomy rather than as the central organizing axis, which this survey claims as its specific point of differentiation. Within this corpus, two of the specific papers this survey cites and discusses are already independently reviewed. [reviews/2606.26294.md](2606.26294.md) (the Red Queen Gödel Machine) is the paper this survey identifies as the clearest existing instance going beyond single-entity meta-evolution "precursors" toward genuine Meta Co-Evolution, since it co-evolves task agents and their evaluators with a meta-level mechanism using joint feedback from both sides to guide subsequent evolution; this survey's own critical discussion (Sec. 5.2) is consistent with, and does not overstate, what that paper's own review in this corpus found, namely that RQGM's central finding is genuinely a working instance of co-evolving evaluators and agents together, though still explicitly described in that paper's own text as "a preliminary empirical investigation" over "short search horizons," a caveat this survey does not repeat when citing it as its leading Stage-3 example. [reviews/2606.10917.md](2606.10917.md) (Role-Agent) is cited under task-space co-evolution as an instance of an agent's own failure modes driving subsequent task resampling, a characterization independently confirmed accurate against this corpus's own review of that paper (see Verification above).

## Critical discussion

The paper's specific, falsifiable definitional criterion for genuine co-evolution (mutual, trajectory-reshaping adaptation, as opposed to mere interaction, information exchange, or one-sided adaptation to a fixed counterpart) is a real conceptual contribution that does useful work throughout the taxonomy, for instance in correctly distinguishing single-agent "model-harness co-evolution" (evolution of one unit's two components) from genuine multi-unit co-evolution, a distinction the paper applies consistently when critiquing an adjacent survey's terminology in Appendix B. The Figure 4 cross-paper synthesis, while modest in scope, is methodologically more careful than a typical narrative survey's supporting evidence, explicitly guarding against raw-score pooling across incompatible papers and against silently treating cross-paper variance as a formal confidence interval.

One point is worth naming directly: the paper's overall narrative arc, that evolutionary freedom progressively expands from Stage 1 through Stage 3 toward open-endedness, carries a degree of teleological framing (the field is heading toward, and should head toward, an increasingly autonomous endpoint) that is more confident than the actual evidentiary weight behind Stage 3 currently supports; the paper's own Limitations section concedes Stage 3 has "only limited work" meeting its definition, and the Figure 4 plateau finding used to motivate the need for meta co-evolution is itself drawn from a necessarily selective sample of papers that happened to report a matched static-versus-evolving comparison, rather than a representative or random sample of the broader self-evolving-agent literature; a reader should therefore treat the "expanding boundary of evolutionary freedom" framing as an organizing narrative device the authors find compelling and evidence-consistent, rather than as an empirically established trajectory the field is necessarily converging toward. This is a limitation of interpretive framing rather than of factual accuracy, since the paper is careful elsewhere (in its Limitations section specifically) to flag the thinness of Stage 3 evidence directly.

What remains well supported after this caveat: the specific, checkable claim that no prior survey uses co-evolution as its central organizing axis is directly substantiated by the paper's own systematic, named comparison against nine adjacent surveys' actual stated taxonomies (Appendix B), rather than asserted without evidence; the three-stage taxonomy itself, and the specific sub-categories within Stages 1 and 2, are well-populated with concrete, individually-checkable examples (as confirmed for at least one spot-checked example in this review); and the specific failure-mode vocabulary the paper introduces for co-evolving systems (evaluator exploitation, partner overfitting, diversity collapse) identifies genuinely distinct risks from those typically discussed for single-agent self-improvement, providing a useful and specific vocabulary for future empirical work to test against, independent of how quickly or whether the field actually progresses toward the paper's proposed Stage 3.
