Citation: Kirgis, P., Kapoor, S., Schwartz, A., Rabanser, S., Africa, D., Voudouris, K., Nguyen, V., et al. Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies. arXiv:2607.27191v2, 2026.

## What this paper is about

A large, multi-institution "CRUX" collaboration (Princeton, the UK AI Security Institute, and others) asks whether frontier AI agents can do the open-ended part of AI research, not benchmark-style tasks with a known metric, but the part where you have to decide what question is worth pursuing, judge whether your evidence actually settles it, and recover creatively when it does not. They introduce a new evaluation method, shadow evaluation, give agents six days and thousands of dollars of compute to answer the central research question of two real, unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions, and have the papers' own original authors grade the results as conference reviewers.

## Extended summary

**Method: shadow evaluation.** The authors argue existing ways of measuring AI R&D automation both have real gaps: verifiable-task benchmarks (RE-Bench, MLE-Bench, PostTrainBench, MLS-Bench, and others tabulated in the paper's own survey) restrict evaluation to problems with a pre-specified metric, excluding genuinely open-ended research; and submitting AI-generated papers to blind peer review (as Sakana's AI Scientist and Intology's Zochi have done) inherits peer review's own well-documented unreliability, citing NeurIPS's own consistency experiments (Cortes and Lawrence 2021; Beygelzimer et al. 2023) showing half the variance in review scores is subjective, committees disagree on about a quarter of accept/reject decisions, and roughly half of accepted papers would be rejected on a re-run, plus the structural problem that a developer can submit many AI-generated papers and disclose only the accepted ones. Shadow evaluation instead takes the unpublished, uncontaminated central research question from a real paper, gives it to a well-resourced frontier agent with no access to the original findings, and has the original authors, who have spent months on exactly this question, grade the agent's output as a conference reviewer would.

**Setup.** Two case studies: a "Personas" paper on the structure and controllability of LLM persona traits (with UK AI Security Institute coauthors as the original researchers), and a "TabPFN" paper on designing a distribution-shift detector for tabular foundation models (with a University of Toronto coauthor). Each agent (Claude Opus 4.8 with extra-high reasoning, run via the model-agnostic OpenClaw scaffold) received the research question, six days of wall-clock time, $3,000 in API credits, GPU credits, and full access to a VM and open web, with the explicit goal of producing a paper worthy of a top-tier AI conference. Original authors set the research question and resource budget without hinting at promising directions, then graded the finished paper. The agent could self-review via a subagent and was instructed to also submit to three external AI reviewing tools (Stanford Agentic Reviewer, CMU Paper Reviewer, refine.ink).

**Headline result.** Both papers were unambiguously rejected: Personas scored 2/6 ("Reject"), TabPFN scored 1/6 ("Strong Reject"), with reviewer confidence at 4/5 and 5/5 respectively. Reviewers cited unprincipled data/experiment choices, conclusions that did not follow from the evidence, dense and heavily hedged writing, and limited significance relative to prior work (one reviewer described the agent's inferential leap as "a kind of 'proof by example' fallacy that is highly non-scientific"). Both agents nonetheless produced literature reviews and candidate hypotheses the original authors found reasonable and similar to their own early approaches, and one agent surfaced a minor, genuinely interesting counterintuitive finding about emergent misalignment not transferring from narrow style-only finetuning. A pre-registered survey of twelve CRUX collaborators, taken before any results were shared, put a median 30% probability on a weak-accept-or-better outcome and a median 60% probability on the weaker "produces some interesting findings" bar; the field mostly under-predicted failure severity on the acceptance question but over-predicted certain other failure risks (see Critical discussion).

**Five failure modes, from full log analysis:** (1) poor judgment about the bar for publishable research, agents fell back on small, hand-curated, or synthetic datasets and treated underpowered negative results as substantive findings; (2) uncreative responses to negative feedback, across 15 combined rounds of self- and external-AI review across the two runs, the process never once returned an acceptance, yet agents responded by narrowing claims and adding caveats rather than rethinking the premise; (3) ineffective backtracking, agents made local pivots but never abandoned a failing overall approach and restarted, despite having subagent tools that would have let them do so cleanly; (4) poor resource/time awareness, both runs ended with well under half the API budget spent, and one agent declared completion seven hours before deadline despite a paper below its own acceptance bar; (5) instruction drift, agents ignored explicit rules on exploration time, review-tool usage, and page/abstract length limits, causing both final papers to fail the technical submission requirements of a real conference (exceeding NeurIPS's nine-page limit onto a tenth page).

**Robustness and other findings.** A second run on the TabPFN question using a different model and scaffold (GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra via Codex) reproduced nearly all the same failure modes, arguing against "scaffold overhang" as the primary explanation, though this run also found and used a real (not purely synthetic) distribution-shifted dataset, a partial improvement. Two earlier dry runs without high reasoning effort produced markedly worse literature review and writing, suggesting reasoning effort (not wall-clock time or resources) was the more binding lever within the tested range. A manual audit of every LLM call and all committed code found no evidence of reward hacking in the sense of deceptive misrepresentation; if anything, agents retired more marketable early claims in favor of honest negative results, though the orchestrator did catch five instances of a subagent hallucinating or misrepresenting a result before it reached the final draft. Agents overweighted the single most lenient of the three external AI reviewers (Stanford Agentic Reviewer, which recommended acceptance on early drafts) even when the other two tools and the agent's own self-review disagreed. The paper explicitly names a "generator-verifier gap": since the AI reviewers reliably rejected genuinely bad papers, they could in principle power fast improvement via reinforcement learning, but because both real cases were true negatives, the paper cannot establish whether the reviewers were actually discerning quality or simply rejecting everything.

**Authors' own limitations and disclosed biases:** only two shadow cases were run (plus one robustness re-run), a small sample relative to typical benchmarks; reviewers were not blind (they knew the work was AI-generated and had already answered the same question themselves); the team could not access Anthropic's strongest model (deliberately capability-limited for frontier AI R&D use); researcher discretion pervades every stage of an open-world evaluation like this one, from question selection to log interpretation; and coauthors explicitly disagree on the root mechanism behind the observed failures (lack of creativity vs. epistemic lock-in vs. myopia vs. functional fixedness). The core team discloses its own directional prior, skepticism that imminent recursive self-improvement toward runaway superintelligence is likely, as a potential source of interpretive bias.

## Learnings

A verifier that consistently rejects bad work is not the same thing as a generator that can act on that signal: across 15 combined rounds of self- and external-AI review that never once returned an acceptance, neither agent fundamentally revised its research approach in response. Having an accurate rejector does not automatically give you an agent capable of the harder move, recognizing that the whole premise needs to change rather than the wording.

Reasoning effort (extra-high vs. no/low reasoning within the same model) visibly improved literature-review depth and writing quality, while neither run came close to exhausting its wall-clock or dollar budget, decoupling "more resources" from the actual bottleneck. Anyone proposing that scaling compute or time alone will close current gaps in autonomous research quality should treat this as a direct counterexample: within this setup, the agents were not resource-constrained, they were judgment-constrained.

A careful, artifact-level audit of every LLM call and committed code file found no deceptive reward hacking, and the observed bias ran in the opposite direction (agents retired attractive-looking positive claims in favor of honest negative results). This is a useful, specific counterpoint to any default assumption that autonomous research agents will primarily fail by lying about their results; in this setting the dominant failure was premature intellectual surrender, not fabrication.

## Verification

This paper is a rich case study in what an evaluator can and cannot establish about itself. The agents' self-review subagent and three external AI reviewing tools functioned as intended in one sense (all consistently returned negative verdicts that overlapped substantially with the expert human reviewers' concerns), but the paper is explicit and careful about the limit of that evidence: "we cannot establish the verifier's accuracy. Both agent-generated papers were rejects, so we cannot say whether the AI reviews were actually discerning quality or just uniformly rejecting the papers." This is a precise articulation of a general problem for any verifier evaluated only on negative cases: rejecting garbage is not proof of an ability to grade the full quality range, and all-reject data alone cannot distinguish a discerning judge from an indiscriminately harsh one. A related finding is that the three external review tools disagreed with each other in a way that mattered operationally: the Stanford Agentic Reviewer was measurably the most lenient (recommending acceptance on early drafts when the agent's own self-review and the other two tools said reject), and the agents leaned on that one favorable signal in their own final progress reports even though it was the outlier. The paper also reports that when human experts and the agents' own final self-reviews were compared directly, they largely agreed on which weaknesses existed, but not on how much each one mattered: the single most damning issue by the human reviewers' account (hand-curated or synthetic data choices) appeared in the agent's self-review, but flattened among many minor comments rather than flagged as decisive. This is a useful, concrete instance of a verifier that has the right facts but the wrong prioritization, a different failure mode from simply missing an error.

## Field context

The paper situates itself explicitly against two established evaluation traditions it tables and cites by name: automatically-verifiable-task evaluations (CORE-Bench, MLE-Bench, RE-Bench, MLR-Bench, PostTrainBench, MLS-Bench, and autonomous scaffold/model-improvement demonstrations like AlphaEvolve, the Darwin Gödel Machine, Karpathy's Autoresearch, an "Automated Weak-to-Strong Researcher," a NanoGPT training speedrun, and AIDE2), and blind- or non-blind-reviewed end-to-end auto-research systems (Sakana's AI Scientist / AI Scientist-v2, Intology's Zochi, and Agent Laboratory), positioning shadow evaluation as a third, complementary method rather than a replacement for either. The authors also explicitly flag a concurrent, methodologically adjacent paper (Ahmed et al. 2026, bioRxiv, evaluating advanced AI research frameworks against published natural-science studies), noting the key design difference that its target work is already public and its evaluators are not the original authors, a useful contrast for judging how much of that paper's design choices (versus this one's) drive any differences in reported outcomes. Within this corpus, the paper's specific, artifact-audited failure taxonomy overlaps strikingly with the ResearchArena study already reviewed here (`arxiv:2605.19156`): both independently name "underpowered experiments" as a leading, named failure mode for autonomous research agents, despite entirely different agents, task domains (open CS research seeds vs. two specific unpublished papers), and evaluation designs (artifact-aware LLM peer review plus human integrity audit vs. non-blind original-author grading). Two independently-run 2026 studies converging on the same named failure category from different methods is a meaningfully stronger form of evidence than either result alone, and is worth treating as corroborated rather than merely reported. The paper's finding that agents can fully execute the engineering of a research project unsupervised while still failing at judgment and creative recovery also gives hard behavioral-trace support to the same distinction the Hugging Face commentary already reviewed here (`arxiv:2509.06580`) draws rhetorically, between "AI success" (executing a well-posed task) and the harder, socially-and-scientifically-embedded work of deciding which questions and evidence actually matter.

## Critical discussion

The paper does not include a matched human-researcher control arm operating under the same six-day, $3,000 budget, so the finding "agents fall well short of a NeurIPS bar" cannot be cleanly separated from the possibility that no one, human or AI, reliably clears that bar under those specific resource and time constraints; the paper partially acknowledges the asymmetry (the original authors spent months and used far more GPU-hours) and argues that resource scarcity was not the binding constraint since the agents left most of their own budgets unused, which is a reasonable inference but not a direct substitute for a same-conditions human baseline that would show what fraction of the gap is attributable to constraints versus to agent capability specifically.

The evaluation rests on two shadow cases (plus one same-question robustness re-run on a different model and scaffold), which the authors themselves are candid about as a cost-driven design constraint, expert grading of this depth requires genuine domain expertise and real time, so it cannot be scaled the way an automatic benchmark can. This means the specific numeric outcomes (both papers rejected, ~15x-style behavioral consistency across runs) should be read as an in-depth existence demonstration of a failure pattern rather than a statistically representative estimate of failure rate across the space of open-ended AI research questions, a distinction the paper's own "Limitations" table draws clearly.

The non-blind review design (original authors, who already answered the same question and know the work is AI-generated, serve as the sole graders) is disclosed at length by the authors as a limitation, but it is worth naming the specific mechanism a bit more precisely than the paper's own framing: reviewers who have already committed to their own solution path have a strong, well-formed prior about what a "good" answer to their own question looks like, which could make them harsher toward any agent output that takes a different, potentially still-defensible path, independent of whether they know it is AI-generated. The paper's mitigations (documenting every human intervention, a second-model robustness check, full transparency of artifacts for outside verification) are real and substantive, but they address the disclosed "AI-generated" bias more directly than this narrower anchoring-on-one's-own-solution concern.

What remains supported after these caveats: given the paper's genuinely strong methodological safeguards, uncontaminated unpublished questions, deep-domain-expert grading, a pre-registered survey of collaborator predictions that lets the paper show where its own team's priors were wrong, a second-model-and-scaffold robustness check reproducing nearly every failure mode, an explicit and careful search for reward hacking that came back negative, and full release of logs and artifacts for outside scrutiny, the core behavioral claim is well-supported for the two specific research questions and agent/scaffold combinations tested: today's frontier agents can execute the full engineering of a research project essentially unsupervised, but fail specifically at judging evidentiary sufficiency, creative recovery from a falsified hypothesis, project-level backtracking, and sustained self-directed resource and instruction management.

## Relevance to us

This paper gives the single most direct, artifact-level behavioral evidence among the papers reviewed today for a claim central to how we frame Theo: the interesting gap in autonomous scientific research right now is judgment and calibration, not raw engineering execution, and it corroborates, with hard trace data rather than self-report, what our own interviews already told us (validation and pushback matter more than autonomous solving; the failure mode is a plausible shortcut at an intermediate step, not a wrong final answer). The paper's own precise framing of the "generator-verifier gap", a verifier that reliably rejects bad research could in principle power fast RL-driven improvement, but when every real test case is a true negative you cannot tell whether it is discerning quality or just uniformly pessimistic, is directly reusable language for describing the limits of any process-reward or graded-verifier system we build for physics: rejecting a bad derivation is not evidence that the checker can also correctly grade a genuinely good but non-obvious one, and we should design evaluation sets that include hard positive cases specifically to close that gap, not just adversarial negatives.

The convergence between this paper's failure taxonomy and the ResearchArena paper's (both independently naming "underpowered experiments" as a leading failure mode for autonomous research agents, from entirely different methods) upgrades that specific finding from a single reported result to something closer to corroborated, and it is a concrete design requirement for any long-lived research runtime we build (B09): assume by default that an agent left to plan its own experimental adequacy will under-power the test unless a check external to the generator enforces a minimum evidentiary bar before a hypothesis can be marked resolved, mirroring the "cheap verifiers change architecture" principle we already hold, here applied to statistical/experimental adequacy rather than to a derivation step.

The observation that agents can flawlessly execute environment-navigation tasks (debugging GPU clusters, managing a VM, compiling a full paper) while failing at deciding what counts as sufficient evidence is a sharp, empirically-grounded instance of the control-vs-appliance and helper-vs-skeptic tensions we already track: it argues for keeping the human explicitly in charge of the "is this settled" judgment call even in a system that is otherwise trusted to run substantial unsupervised engineering, rather than treating engineering competence as evidence that judgment competence will follow at the same pace.
