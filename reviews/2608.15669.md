## What this paper is about

An LLM can propose plausible new molecules, protein sequences, or training-code edits, but its own confidence about whether a candidate is actually good is not a trustworthy signal — especially for candidates unlike anything in its training data. Bayesian optimization (BO) is the classical answer to "which expensive experiment should I run next," but it needs a workable representation of the design space and struggles when that space is unbounded or combinatorial. The Large Discovery Model (LDM) couples the two: an LLM proposes and edits candidates, while a continually-updated Gaussian process surrogate predicts each candidate's reward and uncertainty, and an acquisition function (not the LLM's own likelihood) decides where to spend the next expensive real-world evaluation.

## Extended summary

**Framing.** The paper distinguishes three regimes: exploitation (act on well-understood candidates), exploration (evaluate uncertain candidates the model can already represent), and discovery (expand what the model can represent or reach at all, e.g., a genuinely new molecular scaffold or program mechanism). Classical BO does exploitation/exploration well within a fixed space; the LLM's generative capacity is what can expand the space itself. LDM formalizes this as a KL-regularized objective that tilts the LLM's proposal distribution toward high-acquisition-value candidates while staying close to the LLM's structured prior (Proposition 1: the optimal search distribution is an exponentially-tilted reweighting of the LLM's base measure by the acquisition function). In practice this tilted distribution is approximated by sampling a finite candidate pool from the LLM and reweighting/selecting from it (deterministic top-b or Gumbel-top-b stochastic sampling), which is why larger candidate pools function as a test-time-compute scaling knob.

**Theory.** Section 5 proves a regret bound (Theorem 1) that decomposes error into three parts: a discovery gap (the LLM's reachable candidate set doesn't yet contain near-optimal designs), the usual GP-UCB optimization regret within the reachable set, and an LDM sampling shortfall from approximating the tilted distribution with a finite pool rather than exact maximization. This is a genuine extension of standard fixed-domain GP-UCB analysis to a setting where the candidate domain itself is LLM-controlled and evolving.

**Three case studies (all in-silico/digital-oracle, not wet-lab).** (1) AutoResearch: editing a single nanoGPT training file under a fixed 5-minute-per-run compute budget, reward = negative validation bits-per-byte. LDM uses indirect parameterization (the LLM identifies tunable dimensions like learning rate/width/depth; a GP with RBF kernel models that space; Expected Improvement selects the next run). Against the original LLM-only Karpathy loop under identical hardware, LDM achieves a 2.4x larger absolute val_bpb reduction (0.0727 vs. 0.0301 from the same starting point) and, on a separate matched-hardware run, beats two entries on a public community leaderboard. (2) Antibody CDRH3 design: a 20^11 discrete sequence space scored by the Absolut! structure-based binding-energy simulator across 5 antigen targets, 200 evaluations each. Four LDM variants (direct vs. policy-based generation, crossed with max vs. softmax selection) are compared against Pure LLM, TURBO, COMBO, and the domain-specialized AntBO; policy-mode LDM variants reach binding energies comparable to AntBO, while the authors explicitly frame the goal as competitive generality rather than beating a domain-specialized method. (3) Multi-objective molecular design targeting KRAS G12D (docking score + predicted binding activity, scored by Pareto hypervolume over 80 evaluations): LDM (both Direct-Softmax and ReaSyn-Softmax variants) substantially outperforms Pure LLM, classical multi-objective BO (independent GPs with string kernels), and random search, with 62.4% and 63.1% relative hypervolume gains over LLM-only and MOBO respectively.

**Ablations.** Increasing test-time search budget only helps when candidates are selected by a calibrated acquisition function rather than raw LLM likelihood; UCB/EI outperform posterior-mean-only (pure exploitation); disabling the "discovery" mechanism (letting the surrogate's feature set expand) caps performance at a worse plateau even with a large search budget; a pure-LLM loop with no surrogate at all plateaus well above (worse than) the full LDM result.

**Fine-tuning.** LDM-TTS search traces (candidate, acquisition value, optional surrogate-grounded rationale) are distilled into a smaller open-weight model (Qwen3.5-9B) via supervised fine-tuning. The fine-tuned, reasoning-augmented model outperforms both the untuned base model and, on the molecular task, a larger DeepSeek V4 Flash teacher variant. Held-out generalization tests (excluding 2 of 5 antibody targets from fine-tuning, and separately excluding the entire antibody task from fine-tuning) show the distilled policy matches or exceeds the in-distribution fine-tuned model on 4 of 5 held-out targets, with the one exception (`1H0D_C`) explicitly attributed by the authors to needing de novo motif design rather than candidate selection.

**Self-stated limitations.** Exact GPs scale cubically with observation count; kernel/feature choices still require manual per-domain customization; performance is capped by the LLM's pretrained domain coverage (weak in antibody design specifically); the closed loop does not yet incorporate external knowledge bases or parallel experiments ("unknown knowns" are left unused); and all evaluation is in-silico/digital-oracle, with wet-lab validation left to future work.

## Learnings

The central design principle — use the acquisition function (which explicitly trades off predicted value against epistemic uncertainty) as the test-time-compute allocation signal, rather than the LLM's own self-assessed confidence or linguistic plausibility — is a clean, generalizable answer to a problem several other AI-for-science/AutoResearch systems in this corpus wrestle with differently (namely, that LLM self-judgment is not a reliable proxy for external, costly-to-verify quality). The ablation showing that scaling test-time search budget without an acquisition function does not help (the "pure LLM reflection" loop plateaus far short of LDM) is a specific, falsifiable demonstration of this point rather than an assertion.

The three-way regret decomposition (discovery gap / surrogate optimization regret / sampling shortfall) is a reusable analytical lens: it makes explicit that "the LLM proposes bad candidates" and "the surrogate ranks good candidates poorly" and "search doesn't fully realize the ideal tilted policy" are three distinct, separately diagnosable failure modes, rather than one undifferentiated "search didn't work" outcome.

## Verification

Central, and unusually strong on the theoretical side: a full regret bound with proofs (Appendix B) under stated assumptions (UCB calibration, reservoir coverage/local regularity), rather than an informal argument. On the empirical side, ablations isolate each of the three claimed mechanisms (search budget, acquisition function choice, discovery/feature-expansion mechanism) one at a time against fixed baselines, and the out-of-distribution generalization tests are a genuine held-out design (excluding entire targets or the entire task type from fine-tuning data) rather than a same-distribution split. What is not verified: real-world (wet-lab) validation of any result — every case study runs against an in-silico or digital-oracle evaluator, a limitation the authors state plainly rather than obscure. Most headline comparisons report mean ± standard deviation over a small number of seeds (commonly n=4-10), without a reported significance test on the specific relative-improvement percentages emphasized in the abstract (2.4x, 18.2%, >60%).

## Field context

LDM sits at the intersection of two literatures it explicitly reviews and positions against: LLM-driven autonomous research/discovery agents (which it argues lack a calibrated external value signal, citing evidence that LLM self-assessed novelty rankings reverse once ideas are actually executed, and that frontier agents score well below human researchers on paper-replication and research-execution benchmarks), and hybrid LLM+Bayesian-optimization methods (LLAMBO, BoChemian, LABO, OPRO), which it distinguishes from by retaining an explicit calibrated GP posterior rather than asking the LLM itself to produce or interpret uncertainty — a design choice the authors justify by citing prior work (Gupta et al. 2025, Kristiadi et al. 2024) showing LLMs are insensitive to experimental feedback and poorly calibrated in BO settings. The paper positions its own regret analysis as extending classical infinite-armed-bandit theory (where new arms are drawn from a reservoir) by treating the LLM as an adaptive, context-conditioned reservoir.

Within this same batch, LDM's AutoResearch case study (editing Karpathy's single-file nanoGPT training loop) and the WMRL paper's Kaggle-competition RL training loop ([2608.12564.md](2608.12564.md)) both target the general problem of scaling automatic ML-experimentation search, but attack different bottlenecks: WMRL addresses the cost of running real execution during RL training of an agent's policy, while LDM addresses the reliability of the value signal guiding which candidate to try next at test time, without any policy training in its base form (fine-tuning is a separate, optional consolidation step). The two are complementary rather than competing. More broadly, this corpus's "AI for Science" positioning piece ([26aifsci.md](26aifsci.md)) asks in the abstract what specific technical capabilities are missing for AI to move beyond statistical pattern-matching in science; LDM's core argument — that calibrated epistemic uncertainty, not generative fluency, is the missing ingredient — is a concrete, testable answer to part of that question, though 26aifsci is a positioning essay rather than a paper LDM cites or responds to directly.

## Critical discussion

The paper's strongest evidentiary claims are the ones backed by a proven regret bound and a matched-condition ablation: the demonstration that scaling test-time compute only helps when gated by a calibrated acquisition function (not raw LLM confidence) is shown, not merely argued, via a direct comparison against a no-acquisition ReAct-style baseline under identical budgets. The antibody case study's explicit framing — positioning LDM as competitive-but-not-superior to the domain-specialized AntBO — is a notably honest choice that most papers proposing a new general-purpose method would be tempted to obscure.

Three things temper how far the headline percentage claims (2.4x, 18.2%, >60%) can be pushed. First, every case study is an in-silico or digital-oracle benchmark; the authors state this directly, but it means all reported gains describe how well LDM out-searches a simulator or fixed-hardware training loop, not how it would perform against real, noisier, and more expensive wet-lab or production feedback where evaluation noise and delay characteristics may differ substantially from the assumptions in the regret analysis (Assumption 1's UCB calibration and Assumption 2's Hölder regularity around the optimum) (`narrow-evaluation`). Second, most reported results rest on small seed counts (commonly n=4-10, and n=3 for the task-level OOD generalization study), and the headline relative-improvement percentages in the abstract are computed from mean point estimates without an accompanying significance test, even though standard deviations are reported per condition — for domains this expensive to evaluate, small samples are an understandable constraint, but the abstract's precise percentages could give a false sense of tighter certainty than the underlying variance supports (`missing-uncertainty`). Third, the molecular-design comparison set (Pure LLM, classical MOBO with string kernels, and random search) omits the specialized deep-generative molecular optimization methods (e.g., RL-based generative models, genetic algorithms tailored to molecule optimization) that are standard comparators in that specific subfield, so the reported >60% relative gains demonstrate superiority over general-purpose baselines rather than over the strongest domain-specific alternatives, a narrower claim than the abstract's framing might suggest to a reader unfamiliar with molecular-design benchmarking norms (`weak-baseline`).

What remains well supported after these caveats: the core mechanistic claims (acquisition-guided search beats confidence-only search; an explicit discovery/frontier-expansion mechanism outperforms search confined to a fixed feature set; fine-tuned proposers generalize to genuinely held-out targets and tasks) are each demonstrated via controlled, matched-condition ablations rather than resting only on the cross-domain leaderboard-style comparisons, and the regret-bound theory gives the empirical pattern a principled, non-post-hoc explanation. The magnitude of the specific headline percentages, and the strength of the molecular-design result relative to the strongest possible domain-specific baseline, should be read as an initial (v0.1, per the paper's own release framing) demonstration rather than a settled benchmark result.
