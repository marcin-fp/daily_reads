Citation: Zhang, Z., Zhang, S., Lambert, J., Zhou, W., Wang, Z., Chen, M., Hard, A., Mathews, R., Wang, L. Fantastic Reasoning Behaviors and Where to Find Them: Unsupervised Discovery of the Reasoning Process. arXiv:2512.23988, 2025.

## What this paper is about

When a reasoning-tuned LLM works through a hard problem, its chain-of-thought often shows recognizable patterns, like pausing to double-check a step (reflection) or abandoning an approach mid-way for another (backtracking). Most prior work studying these patterns starts by having humans define and label them in advance, which necessarily limits the analysis to the small handful of behaviors someone thought to name. This paper instead asks whether a model's own internal activations, examined without any behavior labels at all, already organize themselves into distinct, controllable directions corresponding to these (and other, previously unnamed) reasoning behaviors, using a classic unsupervised technique, the sparse autoencoder, to find out.

## Extended summary

The method, RISE, works in two stages. First, chain-of-thought responses are split into sentence-level "reasoning steps" at paragraph-break delimiters, and the hidden-state representation at each step's delimiter token (the model's residual-stream activation after a chosen transformer layer) is extracted as that step's representation; a sparse autoencoder (SAE), an encoder-decoder pair trained to reconstruct these activations while keeping only a small number of latent "features" active per example, is then trained on these step-level activations without any behavior labels. The key theoretical claim, building on the linear representation hypothesis, is that if the true underlying reasoning behaviors really do correspond to directions in activation space and satisfy certain regularity conditions, incoherence between distinct behavior directions, sufficiently sparse combinations of them per step, and a large enough sample, then a local optimum of standard SAE training will recover the true set of directions, up to permutation and rescaling (Theorem 1, proved via a five-part argument in the appendix covering identifiability, stable coefficient recovery, the SAE architecture's ability to realize the required threshold-based decision rule, a population-level separation between correct and incorrect decoder solutions, and a standard empirical-to-population risk convergence argument). Because the assumptions (in particular the incoherence and sparsity conditions) reference a ground-truth dictionary that is not directly observable, the paper validates them empirically using the trained SAE's own reconstructions as a stand-in proxy, reporting low reconstruction error, bounded cosine similarity among recovered directions, and empirical sparsity levels consistent with the theorem's requirements.

Empirically, the authors train an SAE (2048 latent dimensions) on step-level activations from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B generating solutions to 500 MATH training problems, later extending part of the analysis to Qwen3-8B. To check whether the unsupervised SAE actually recovers human-recognizable structure, each reasoning step is independently labeled as reflection, backtracking, or other via an LLM-judge (GPT-5) prompted with precise behavioral definitions, with the resulting labels mapped back onto the corresponding SAE latent activations; a UMAP projection of the SAE's decoder columns shows that reflection- and backtracking-associated columns cluster into distinct, separable regions of the decoder space, while the more heterogeneous "other" category remains comparatively diffuse. A quantitative Silhouette-score analysis across all 28 transformer layers shows separability between behavior categories increasing through the network and peaking in mid-to-late layers before a slight decline near the very final layer (interpreted as consistent with a previously-documented "oversmoothing" phenomenon in LLM representations), and finds reflection and backtracking are more distinguishable from the residual "other" category than they are from each other.

To move beyond correlational structure, the paper performs a direct causal intervention: after filtering out decoder columns that activate strongly across multiple behaviors, the remaining reflection-specific columns are averaged into a single reflection vector, which is then added to or subtracted from the model's hidden state at each reasoning step during actual inference, with no retraining involved. On a qualitative example, negative intervention (projecting the reflection direction out) shortens the response and suppresses metacognitive phrasing while preserving the correct final answer, positive intervention lengthens the response and amplifies self-checking language, and vanilla inference falls in between; this pattern is confirmed quantitatively across multiple models and benchmarks (AIME 2024/2025, AMC23, MATH500 for DeepSeek-R1-1.5B and Qwen3-8B), where negative interventions consistently reduce, and positive interventions consistently increase, the measured number of reflection and backtracking steps, and the effect scales smoothly and monotonically with a tunable intervention-strength parameter. Vectors learned purely from MATH500 training data are also shown to generalize when applied, without any retraining, to two substantially different held-out domains, commonsense/graduate-level science QA (GPQA-Diamond) and logical reasoning (KnowLogic), consistently modulating reflection and backtracking frequency in the expected direction in both.

Beyond recovering the two behaviors the paper explicitly labeled for validation, the SAE's unlabeled decoder space is also shown to encode structural information the labeling process never targeted: activations naturally separate into clusters corresponding to short versus long overall response length, again with separability increasing through mid-to-late layers, mirroring the pattern found for the labeled behavior categories. As a further demonstration that the framework can surface genuinely new, difficult-to-hand-label concepts rather than only rediscovering behaviors the authors already had in mind, the paper poses a targeted optimization problem, searching the SAE's decoder columns (via gradient descent with an entropy-minimization objective) for a direction that most reduces the model's next-token entropy, i.e., most increases its apparent confidence. The resulting "confidence vector," when injected during inference, causes frequent reflection- and backtracking-indicating tokens ("Wait," "Alternatively") to be replaced by more direct computational language, and measurably reduces both reflection step counts (90.53 to 33.77 on AIME25) and backtracking step counts (35.50 to 5.93) simultaneously, while accuracy changes only marginally (23.33% to 20.00%, a single question out of 30, which the authors note is not a statistically meaningful difference), and this confidence vector, like the reflection vector, also generalizes to the two held-out domains. Finally, a proof-of-concept application combines the top three confidence-related vectors into a test-time-learned, sample-specific steering combination (inspired by prior test-time bias-learning work), which is shown to improve reasoning accuracy by up to 4.66 points while simultaneously reducing token usage by 13.69%, outperforming two existing steering baselines (SEAL and TIP) in this comparison.

## Learnings

The paper's most broadly transferable methodological contribution is a concrete demonstration that reasoning behaviors, which are inherently fuzzy, overlapping, and hard to define precisely in advance, can nonetheless be discovered as clean, separable, and independently controllable directions in a model's activation space without ever supplying behavior labels during training, only using labels afterward to check whether the unsupervised structure lines up with human intuition; this is a directly reusable template for studying any other LLM behavior that resists clean upfront definition (the paper's own confidence-vector case study is offered as a second, independent demonstration of exactly this template).

A second, more specific and immediately actionable learning is the demonstrated separation between a reasoning behavior's surface frequency and its functional necessity for correctness: suppressing reflection and backtracking almost entirely via the confidence-vector intervention barely changed AIME25 accuracy (a one-question difference), while substantially shortening responses and reducing token cost; this is a concrete, quantified data point supporting the broader claim in the literature that much of the verbose, self-checking language in current reasoning-model outputs is not load-bearing for arriving at the correct answer, at least on the specific benchmark and model tested here.

A third, narrower but useful learning is the finding that these behavior-encoding directions generalize zero-shot across substantially different task domains (from math to graduate-level science QA to logical reasoning) without any retraining or domain-specific recalibration, suggesting that whatever internal machinery implements reflection, backtracking, and confidence in a reasoning model operates as a comparatively general-purpose mechanism rather than one narrowly tuned to the specific domain (mathematics) the vectors were originally extracted from.

## Verification

The paper takes several concrete steps to check that its unsupervised structure genuinely corresponds to real, interpretable phenomena rather than being an artifact of how the SAE happens to organize activations. The central identifiability claim (Theorem 1) is backed by an explicit, multi-step formal proof rather than only an informal argument, and its underlying assumptions (incoherence, sparsity) are checked empirically, via a stated proxy, rather than left unverified. The correspondence between SAE structure and human-recognizable behavior is checked with an LLM-judge labeling process whose reliability is itself independently assessed: agreement between three different LLM judges (GPT-5, GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.5) and a simple keyword-matching heuristic all exceed 85%, with the two same-family judges (GPT-5, GPT-4o) reaching about 94% agreement, a genuine and appropriately-reported check on annotation reliability rather than an assumed one. The central causal claim (that these vectors are not merely correlated with behavior but actively shape it) is supported by a real interventional experiment, not just an observational correlation, with the intervention's effect shown to scale monotonically and predictably with a tunable strength parameter, and replicated across multiple distinct models (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, Qwen3-8B) and four different benchmarks, and additionally shown to transfer to two domains never used to derive the vectors in the first place, a meaningful test of generalization beyond the original training distribution.

Verification is comparatively thinner in two related places. First, the theorem's key assumptions (incoherence, sparsity) are validated using the SAE's own reconstructed activations as a proxy for the unobservable ground-truth dictionary the theorem is about, which the paper states directly ("since the ground-truth dictionary... is not directly observable, we use the reconstructed activations from the sparse autoencoder for evaluation") but does not otherwise justify as an unbiased stand-in; because the object being validated (whether the SAE recovers the true underlying directions) and the object used to perform that validation (the SAE's own output) are not fully independent, this specific empirical check is better read as evidence of internal consistency than as an independent confirmation that the theorem's idealized generative model actually describes the true, unobservable structure of reasoning behaviors in the target LLM. Second, the paper's claims about intervention effects on task accuracy, including the specific claim that the confidence vector's roughly 3-percentage-point accuracy change on AIME25 is "not statistically significant," rest on very small evaluation sets (AIME25 has only 30 questions total) without any formal significance test, reported variance across multiple seeds or samples, or confidence interval; the qualitative direction of the behavior-count effects (large, consistent shifts in reflection/backtracking counts) is unlikely to be pure noise given how large and monotonic they are, but the specific, narrower claim that accuracy is essentially unaffected rests on an informal ("one question out of 30") rather than a formally computed statistical judgment.

## Field context

The paper positions itself against two related lines of prior work it distinguishes directly: activation-steering methods based on the Difference-of-Means approach and related techniques, which the paper argues are fundamentally constrained to concepts with a clear human-supplied contrastive pair (e.g., happy versus sad, safe versus harmful) and therefore poorly suited to reasoning behaviors that are fluid and overlapping rather than cleanly binary; and a small number of recent studies specifically targeting reasoning-behavior interpretability (reflection, backtracking) that the paper credits as identifying these behaviors as important but criticizes for still relying on predefined, human-labeled concepts as their starting point, and one specific concurrent effort using sparse autoencoders on reasoning models that the paper distinguishes by noting it primarily used the SAE as a bridge for transferring reasoning ability into a base model via supervised fine-tuning rather than as a tool for directly interpreting the reasoning mechanism, which is this paper's explicit focus instead. Within this corpus, this paper's central empirical finding, that suppressing reflection and backtracking behaviors via direct activation intervention barely changes task accuracy despite substantially shortening and restructuring the reasoning trace, offers a striking, mechanistically-grounded complement to [2508.17627] (the Reasoning Dynamics/RCP paper, reviewed earlier in this batch), which separately found, via a causal trace-editing experiment, that reasoning generated after a model's internal state has already converged functions mainly as rationalization rather than genuine re-verification; both papers, using entirely different methodologies (this paper via unsupervised representation discovery and direct vector intervention, the other via forced trajectory editing and resampling), converge on a similar underlying picture, that a substantial share of the metacognitive-sounding language in current reasoning-model chains of thought is not doing the correctness-critical work its surface form might suggest, a genuinely reinforcing cross-validation between two independently-conducted studies in this reading batch.

## Critical discussion

The paper's strongest asset is combining a genuine formal identifiability result with a real causal intervention study rather than resting on either alone: the theorem gives a principled account of why an unsupervised SAE should be expected to recover interpretable directions under stated conditions, the UMAP/Silhouette analysis checks whether the empirically-trained SAE actually exhibits the structure the theorem predicts, and the intervention experiments then go a decisive step further by manipulating the discovered directions and observing predictable, monotonic, cross-model, cross-domain effects on model behavior, a level of causal rigor beyond a purely observational interpretability study.

The clearest limitation is scale and model diversity: the paper's primary quantitative results, and essentially all of its most detailed analysis (the layer-wise Silhouette study, the annotation-consistency check, the response-length geometry analysis, the theorem's empirical assumption checks), are conducted on a single, comparatively small 1.5B-parameter distilled reasoning model, with the extension to Qwen3-8B treated as a secondary confirmation rather than an equally thorough second analysis; no larger, more capable, or more widely-deployed reasoning model (the 30B+ open models or proprietary frontier reasoning systems that are the actual subject of most current interest in reasoning-model behavior) is tested, leaving open whether the same clean decoder-space separability and intervention effects would hold, strengthen, or weaken at the scale where these models are actually used in practice. A second, more specific limitation concerns the statistical basis for the paper's accuracy-preservation claims, which are central to the practical appeal of the method (the ability to control reasoning style "without additional training" and, implicitly, without hurting correctness): these claims rest on evaluation sets as small as 30 questions (AIME25, AIME24) without formal significance testing, multiple-seed averaging, or reported confidence intervals, so while the large, consistent shifts in reflection/backtracking counts are compelling on their own terms, the specific and practically important claim that these interventions leave task accuracy essentially unchanged is asserted somewhat informally rather than statistically established. A third, more conceptual point is that the theorem's core empirical validation (Appendix C) uses the SAE's own reconstructed output as a stand-in for the unobservable ground-truth dictionary the theorem is meant to describe, an approach the paper is transparent about but which is better understood as a self-consistency check than as fully independent evidence that the idealized linear generative model assumed by the theorem accurately describes the actual, unknown structure of reasoning behaviors in the underlying LLM.

What remains well supported after these caveats: for the specific model, task domains, and behaviors studied here, sparse autoencoders trained purely on unlabeled step-level activations recover directions that align with human-interpretable reasoning behaviors (validated against a cross-checked, multi-judge annotation process) and that causally, predictably, and reversibly shape those behaviors when directly manipulated during inference, with the manipulation's effects generalizing to task domains never used to derive the vectors; and the paper's demonstration that this same unsupervised mechanism can locate a previously unlabeled concept (confidence) purely by specifying a target optimization objective, without any human-provided contrastive examples for that specific concept, is a genuine, methodologically distinct result from the labeled-behavior-recovery finding and stands as real evidence for the paper's broader claim that unsupervised latent discovery can surface reasoning-relevant structure beyond what human supervision would think to look for.
