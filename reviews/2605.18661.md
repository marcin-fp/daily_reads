## What this paper is about

A large survey that maps essentially the entire "AI-for-research" toolchain onto a single lifecycle: from generating a research idea, through literature review, coding and running experiments, making tables and figures, writing the paper, getting it peer reviewed, rebutting and revising, and finally disseminating it as posters, slides, videos, or interactive agents. For each of these eight stages, the authors catalog the state of the art, name the recurring capability gaps, and pull out a small number of quantitative findings that recur across many systems and benchmarks. The paper's central thesis is that AI is now good at producing research-shaped artifacts at every one of these eight stages, but far less reliable at verifying whether those artifacts are actually novel, correct, faithful, or scientifically sound — and that this generation/verification gap, not any single missing capability, is the field's defining bottleneck.

## Extended summary

The paper organizes the "research lifecycle" into four phases and eight stages: Creation (S1 Idea Generation, S2 Literature Review, S3 Coding and Experiments, S4 Tables and Figures), Writing (S5 Paper Writing), Validation (S6 Peer Review, S7 Rebuttal and Revision), and Dissemination (S8 Paper2X: posters, slides, video, social media, and paper-to-agent conversion). Each stage subsection follows the same pattern: a taxonomy of method families, a curated set of named systems with reported numbers, a dedicated "Assessment" subsection on how that stage is evaluated, and a "Findings and Observations" box with state-and-progress bullets paired against gaps-and-limitations bullets.

Selected figures the paper repeats or leans on as evidence: in S1, ideas that score above 0.6 on novelty often score below 0.5 on feasibility (IdeaBench), and AI-generated ideas that score well pre-implementation degrade sharply after execution (Δ = -1.98 vs. -0.63 for human ideas); a "diversity collapse" study (Artificial Hivemind, 26K queries) reports LLM ideation clustering in narrow regions of idea space regardless of added agents. In S2, citation top-1 accuracy for a leading system (ScholarCopilot) is only 40.1%, and relation-aware retrieval accuracy (SciNetBench) "often remains low." In S3, frontier systems clear 76%+ on SWE-bench Verified but only 23-39% on harder, less-contaminated research-code benchmarks (SWE-bench Pro, SWE-EVO, ResearchCodeBench, SciReplicate-Bench), with 58.6% of ResearchCodeBench errors being semantic (code that runs but implements the wrong algorithm); a cited finding attributes 80% of fully autonomous experimental results to fabrication (MLR-Bench) with downstream review catching only about half of methodological issues. In S4, Matplotlib/Seaborn-style plotting is now largely tractable (90%+ execution pass rate), but formula/LaTeX generation accuracy (TeXpert) drops from 78.8% to 15% as task complexity rises. In S5, up to 17.5% of CS abstracts and 13.5% of biomedical abstracts show detectable AI modification; the best automated paper-generation system (CycleResearcher) scores 5.36 on the ICLR review scale versus a 5.69 accepted-paper average, and AI-assisted papers with more complex language are reportedly less likely to be accepted despite higher output. In S6, standalone LLM reviewers assign inflated scores (6.86 vs. 5.70 human average) and reportedly misclassify 95.8% of rejected papers as acceptable in one cited study, while a randomized ICLR 2025 deployment of LLM feedback on human reviews improved review quality in 89% of cases without moving acceptance rates; the same section catalogs a fairly large adversarial-manipulation literature (prompt injection, benign-adjective triggers, presentation-only rewrites) showing review scores can be manipulated, and a large 2026 venue reportedly rejected 497 papers for AI-use policy violations. In S7, 75-81% of ICLR 2024-2025 scores are unchanged after rebuttal, 17-23% improve (mostly borderline 5→6 transitions), and papers with improved scores go on to be accepted at 55.7-57.6% versus 7.8-12.4% for unchanged scores; separately, about 25% of rebuttal commitments audited in ICLR 2025 were not fulfilled in the camera-ready version. In S8, poster generation cost has reportedly dropped to $0.005/poster with 87% fewer tokens, while video generation is flagged as still the hardest format because it must coordinate slides, subtitles, narration, and timing simultaneously.

Section 7 (Cross-Cutting Analysis) synthesizes these stage-level findings into four end-to-end system architecture families (sequential pipelines, search/self-improving systems, skill-based/tool-integrated systems, multi-agent/community-scale systems), a taxonomy of five evaluation methodologies (expert, LLM-as-judge, automated metrics, execution-grounded, process/trace-based), and five named cross-cutting insights, the most load-bearing being "Artifact Generation Outpaces Scientific Verification" and "Human-Governed Collaboration Remains the Most Reliable Deployment Mode" (the latter grounded specifically in the ICLR 2025 review-feedback study). Section 7.4 closes with six open-challenge themes (phase-boundary faithfulness, scientific judgment/novelty assessment, verification/reproducibility/accountability, citation/versioning provenance, governance/disclosure, and human cognitive ownership), and the paper explicitly frames its recommended target state as "reliable human-governed research automation" rather than full autonomy. The Responsible Use and Limitations section states the survey should be read as "a structured snapshot through our search cutoff" and that AI-generated research outputs should be independently verified before scholarly use.

## Learnings

The single most reusable idea in the paper is naming the recurring failure pattern precisely: at every one of the eight stages, the paper distinguishes "artifact generation" (can the system produce something research-shaped) from "artifact verification" (is that thing actually novel, correct, faithful, or sound), and shows the same qualitative gap — strong generation, weak verification — recurring at S1 (idea novelty vs. feasibility), S3 (executable code vs. semantically correct code), S5 (fluent prose vs. argumentative depth), S6 (coherent review vs. detecting real flaws), and S7 (persuasive rebuttal vs. fulfilled commitments). This gives a portable diagnostic question for any new AI-for-research system: not "can it produce X" but "does anything downstream check whether X is actually right."

A second transferable point is the paper's specific claim about where automated review adds real, validated value: not as a standalone reviewer, but as feedback on a human reviewer's own draft review (the cited ICLR 2025 randomized study improving review quality in 89% of cases without moving acceptance decisions). This is a more specific and better-evidenced version of "keep a human in the loop" — it names the exact point in the workflow (reviewer-facing feedback, not review generation or replacement) where the evidence is strongest, which is more actionable than the generic human-oversight recommendation this genre of survey often lands on.

A third learning, echoed by several individually reviewed papers in this corpus, is the survey's explicit list of documented adversarial-manipulation vectors against LLM-based peer review (covert prompt injection, benign-adjective lexical triggers, and — notably — presentation-only paper rewrites with no hidden text at all) shown to move review scores or flip rankings with only a small fraction of manipulated reviews. This is a concrete, mechanism-level caution for any pipeline (this survey's own S6, or systems like R-Debater in this corpus) that lets a generator or its author interact with the same signal that gates acceptance.

## Verification

As a survey, this paper's own "verification" is citation and categorization rather than independent testing: every quantitative figure attributed to a named system is that system's own reported result, restated here without re-running, replicating, or independently checking it — consistent with how this corpus has already treated the similarly-scoped 2510.09901. The paper is reasonably disciplined about sourcing: essentially every number in the text carries an inline citation, and the "Findings and Observations" boxes repeat only figures that also appear in the main prose with their citation intact, so a reader can trace each claim back to its origin. Where the survey goes further than typical taxonomy papers is in explicitly building "Assessment" subsections into every stage (e.g., 3.1.4, 3.2.4, 3.3.4, 4.1.3, 5.1.4, 5.2.3, 6.1.6) that catalog how that stage is itself evaluated (which benchmarks exist, which evaluation dimensions have "crystallized," where evaluation methodology itself is weak) rather than only cataloging systems — this is closer to a meta-evaluation of the field's evaluation practice than most surveys of this kind attempt, and Section 7.2.4 names five specific evaluation gaps (contamination/temporal validity, cross-system incomparability, cross-domain under-testing, cost as a missing evaluation axis, and the total absence of a full-lifecycle benchmark) as unresolved rather than treating benchmark coverage as adequate.

## Field context

The paper positions itself explicitly as the first survey to span the complete research lifecycle end to end, in contrast to prior surveys it does not name in the sections read but that other papers in this corpus do name covering narrower slices (e.g., 2510.09901's three-phase scientific-discovery framing, which stops at result analysis and does not cover writing, peer review, rebuttal, or dissemination). Within this corpus, this survey is a natural cross-reference point for nearly every system-level paper already reviewed: its S1 taxonomy (LLM-internal, external-signal-grounded, multi-agent-collaborative idea generation) and its citation of the "Artificial Hivemind" diversity-collapse finding directly parallel the reviewer-pleasing and consensus-narrowing dynamics independently observed in 2605.06651 (AI co-mathematician); its S6 Peer Review section's finding that standalone AI reviewers are lenient and inflate scores, with the safer deployment being "AI feedback on human review" rather than automated review itself, is the same structural concern already flagged in this corpus's review of 2512.24684 (R-Debater's circular Judgement-Agent/InspireScore design) and 2505.14946 (RLUF reward hacking against a fixed critic) — this survey gives that recurring corpus-level theme an explicit name ("Artifact Generation Outpaces Scientific Verification") and ties it to a large supporting citation base rather than a single paper's own result. Its S7 Rebuttal and Revision section, describing rebuttal automation as a "newest" and comparatively under-tooled stage that cannot yet generate new experimental evidence, is directly relevant background for 2512.24684's rebuttal-focused system. Its end-to-end systems taxonomy (Section 7.1) also gives useful vocabulary for classifying several multi-agent orchestration papers in this corpus, e.g., 2512.04388 (Conductor) and 2512.04695 (TRINITY) fit its "search-based and self-improving" and "multi-agent" architecture families respectively, and its observation that "more agents do not automatically improve performance... they can degrade on sequential reasoning tasks when coordination overhead and error propagation dominate" is a direct, citable caution against reading multi-agent scaling results in this corpus as unconditionally positive.

## Critical discussion

The paper's stage-by-stage organization and its consistent "Assessment" subsections are a genuine service: unlike many taxonomy surveys that stop at cataloging systems, this one repeatedly asks how each stage's claims are checked and names specific, sourced numbers (citation top-1 accuracy, semantic-error rates, score-inflation figures, rebuttal-commitment fulfillment rates) rather than qualitative impressions alone. The five cross-cutting insights in Section 7.3 are argued with specific citations at each stage rather than asserted generically, and the paper is unusually candid that fully autonomous deployment is not yet credible in any stage it surveys, closing with a concrete four-principle recommendation (provenance, execution/retrieval grounding, human checkpoints at phase boundaries, transparency) rather than a vague call for "responsible AI."

Two limitations are worth naming. First, as with any survey of this scope citing several hundred works, nearly every headline number in the Extended summary above is the cited system's own self-reported result, restated without the caveats (baseline strength, evaluator identity, dataset overlap with training data) that this same corpus's paper-review skill would apply to that system if reviewed individually; the survey itself acknowledges this only generically ("evaluation information might be uncertain" in the appendix table footnote) rather than flagging it for specific headline claims repeated in the main text and Findings boxes (e.g., the 80%-fabrication figure attributed to MLR-Bench, or the 95.8%-misclassification figure attributed to a single cited study, are both presented with the same declarative confidence as figures drawn from larger, more replicated bodies of evidence). Second, the survey's self-declared "through June 2026" cutoff sits inside a paper whose own frontmatter shows a "Latest Version: July 20, 2026" note and a living project page/GitHub repo, meaning this is closer to a continuously maintained reference resource than a fixed, citable snapshot — a reasonable choice for a fast-moving field, but it means the reviewed-version numbers here may already differ from what the authors' own hosted version currently states, and any future synthesis work drawing on this review should treat its cited figures as tied to the v2 (2026-07-20) revision specifically. Neither limitation contradicts the paper's central claim; if anything, the field-wide evidence assembled here (spanning eight independently studied stages, dozens of distinct benchmarks, and cited empirical audits at major venues) makes the "generation outpaces verification" thesis one of the better-supported claims of this kind in the corpus, precisely because it recurs independently across stages that use unrelated evaluation methodologies rather than resting on one paper's result.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the eight-stage lifecycle taxonomy is a coherent and citation-grounded organizing structure; the qualitative claim that AI-assisted research systems are more reliable at producing artifacts than at verifying them is corroborated independently across at least five of the eight stages with distinct evidence in each case; and the paper's specific, narrower claim that AI feedback on human review outperforms standalone AI review as a validated deployment mode rests on a described randomized study at meaningful scale (22,467 reviews) rather than a small or convenience sample.
