Citation: Zhou, L., Ling, H., Fu, C., Huang, Y., Sun, M., Yu, W., Wang, X., Li, X., Su, X., Zhang, J., Chen, X., Liang, C., Qian, X., Ji, H., Wang, W., Zitnik, M., Ji, S. Autonomous Agents for Scientific Discovery: Orchestrating Scientists, Language, Code, and Physics. arXiv:2510.09901, 2025.

## What this paper is about

Scientists have started building LLM-based "agents" that don't just answer questions but actively participate in doing research: reading literature, proposing hypotheses, writing and running experiment code, controlling lab robots, and interpreting results. This paper is a survey and position piece that organizes this fast-growing, fragmented landscape into a single framework, breaking the scientific process into three phases (forming a hypothesis, designing and running an experiment, and analyzing and refining the results), cataloging the specific techniques and dozens of named systems used at each phase and across many scientific fields, and then arguing for a new way to think about why some parts of this process are much harder to automate than others, using a borrowed vocabulary from information theory and thermodynamics.

## Extended summary

The paper structures the scientific discovery lifecycle into three phases: Hypothesis Discovery (extracting knowledge from literature and data, generating candidate hypotheses, then screening and validating them), Experimental Design and Execution (translating a hypothesis into a concrete workflow, then executing it via tool use or, when no suitable tool exists, tool creation), and Result Analysis and Refinement (interpreting outputs and iterating, via automatic self-correction, external evaluation, or a human in the loop). For each phase it builds a detailed taxonomy of methods (e.g., under Hypothesis Generation: prompt-based, knowledge-grounded/RAG, multi-agent, and evolutionary-algorithm-based approaches) and populates each category with concrete named systems and a one-to-two-sentence summary of what each reportedly achieves, drawing on several hundred cited papers. A large dedicated section (Section 6) surveys domain-specific agents across genomics, protein engineering, medicine, chemistry, materials science, physics, and other engineering fields, again cataloging dozens of named systems (e.g., A-Lab, an autonomous materials-synthesis robotic lab reported to synthesize 41 novel compounds from 58 targets at a 71% success rate over 17 days; Coscientist, reported to autonomously replicate a Nobel-Prize-winning palladium cross-coupling reaction in under 4 minutes; BioDiscoveryAgent, reported to outperform trained Bayesian-optimization baselines by 21% on average across six gene-perturbation datasets) largely by restating the headline results each cited system's own paper reports.

The paper's more original conceptual contribution is an "information-theoretic framework" for reasoning about why different phases and information types are harder or easier to automate. It defines three properties: Information Entropy (the size/uncertainty of a hypothesis space, e.g., the space of all possible scientific hypotheses is maximally high-entropy), Verifiability (whether a piece of information can be checked against a formal, logical, or empirical standard), and Dissipation (the unavoidable computational/exploratory cost of discarding unproductive paths while searching a high-entropy space, explicitly framed by analogy to thermodynamic dissipation and Landauer's Principle on the minimum energy cost of irreversible information operations). The paper argues that scientific discovery moves information through four levels, Human Intent, Natural Language, Computer Language, and Physical Information, with entropy generally decreasing and verifiability generally increasing as information moves from abstract intent toward concrete, testable physical data. Using this lens, the paper qualitatively rates each of five workflow stages (Hypothesis Discovery, Experimental Design, Tool Use, Tool Creation, Result Analysis & Refinement) on a five-point scale (Very Low to Very High) for entropy in each of the four information types plus an overall "dissipation" rating (Table 2, visualized as a heatmap and a radar chart), concluding that Tool Use is the easiest phase to automate (low entropy and dissipation throughout, since it involves calling predefined, deterministic functions) while Tool Creation is the hardest (very high entropy and dissipation throughout, since it requires designing genuinely novel algorithms and interfaces with the physical world with no existing template). Building on this analysis, the paper proposes a five-level autonomy ladder for scientific agents (Human-Led, AI-Augmented, Full Human-AI Collaboration, AI-Led Hybrid, and Full AI Autonomy), explicitly framed as grounded in an agent's growing capacity to independently reduce entropy and increase verifiability, which the authors argue is a more precise, information-grounded alternative to prior role-based autonomy-level frameworks proposed by other recent surveys they cite directly (Gao et al. 2024; Wei et al. 2025a; Zheng et al. 2025).

A discussion section (Section 7) engages more critically and speculatively with open problems rather than only cataloging achievements. It argues that reinforcement learning, particularly RL from verifiable rewards as demonstrated by systems like DeepSeek-R1, is the mechanism that could push agents from passive text generation toward active, tool-using decision-making, but identifies four specific obstacles unique to applying this successfully to science: an "Environment Problem" (agents must interact with a heterogeneous mix of digital tools and physical lab hardware, introducing latency, noise, and irreversible real-world risk that purely digital RL agents never face), an "Action Problem" (beyond using existing tools, an advanced science agent must also be able to write entirely new tools/code, expanding the action space from a finite set to the space of all possible programs, which the paper argues makes standard exploration-based RL scaling impractical), an "Observation Problem" (agents must integrate long-duration, mixed-modality data streams, e.g., connecting a serendipitous finding from months earlier to a current problem, which exceeds the short-context memory of current agents), and, described as the most central obstacle, a "Reward Problem" (unlike math or coding tasks with a clear correctness signal, scientific discovery's true payoff, novelty, impact, or reproducibility, can take years to materialize and has no well-defined, computable reward function, breaking down the reward paradigm that has made agentic RL work well in other domains). The paper further argues, drawing on and crediting a formulation by Kambhampati (2025), that an LLM used purely as a reasoning engine is fundamentally trapped within "Humanity's Knowledge Closure," able to efficiently recombine and infer from existing knowledge but structurally unable to generate discoveries that require actually testing an idea against the physical world, and that only by acquiring the ability to design and execute real experiments (closing an action-feedback loop with reality) can an agent begin to push past this boundary. A final short section raises a related concern about serendipity: since LLMs are trained to maximize likelihood over historical data, they are argued to be inherently biased toward predictable, high-probability outputs, in tension with the historical pattern that major discoveries (penicillin, cosmic microwave background radiation, graphene) often arose from chance observations that departed from expected patterns, and the paper suggests deliberately stochastic exploration strategies as one possible way to reintroduce this kind of productive randomness into agent behavior.

## Learnings

As a survey and position paper, this work's most transferable contribution is organizational rather than empirical: a clear three-phase decomposition of the scientific discovery lifecycle (hypothesis discovery; experimental design and execution; result analysis and refinement) paired with a fine-grained taxonomy of the specific technique families used within each phase (e.g., distinguishing prompt-based, knowledge-grounded, multi-agent, and evolutionary approaches to hypothesis generation specifically), which gives readers new to this fast-moving area a structured map and named entry points (dozens of specific systems per domain) rather than having to independently survey a highly fragmented literature.

A second, more conceptually distinctive contribution worth carrying forward as a way of thinking, even independent of how rigorously it is currently operationalized, is the paper's argument that the different stages of scientific discovery are not uniformly hard to automate, but instead differ systematically in the shape of the uncertainty involved: routine tool use is low-uncertainty and close to fully automatable today, while genuinely creating a new scientific tool or method combines the hardest parts of both open-ended creative reasoning and reliable engineering, a useful frame for setting realistic expectations about which parts of "autonomous science" are near-term versus far-term.

A third, more specific and actionable learning is the paper's naming of the "Reward Problem" as the central, qualitatively distinct obstacle separating scientific-discovery agents from the domains (math, coding, web navigation) where agentic reinforcement learning has recently succeeded: the observation that a true scientific discovery's payoff can take years to materialize, with no readily computable proxy for novelty, impact, or reproducibility, is a specific, well-posed research problem (rather than a vague statement that "science is hard") that could usefully orient future methods work on this topic.

## Verification

As a survey rather than an original empirical study, this paper's "verification" consists mainly of citation and categorization rather than independent testing: it reports the headline quantitative results that each cited system's own paper claims (success rates, percentage improvements over baselines, speedups) without re-running, replicating, or independently re-evaluating any of them, which is standard practice for a survey of this scope but does mean every specific number attributed to a named system in this review reflects that system's own paper's self-reported claim, not an independently verified fact. The paper is reasonably careful to attribute claims to specific cited sources throughout (nearly every named system is given an inline citation), which at least makes each claim traceable back to its original evidentiary basis for a reader who wants to check further. The information-theoretic framework itself (Section 2.3) is presented as a qualitative analytical lens rather than as an empirically validated model: the entropy ratings assigned to each phase and information type in Table 2 (Very Low through Very High) appear to reflect the authors' own reasoned qualitative judgment rather than any described measurement procedure, computed quantity, or empirical test, and the paper does not claim otherwise, but it also does not flag this distinction explicitly for readers who might otherwise read the specific invocation of information entropy, Landauer's Principle, and the Second Law of Thermodynamics as signaling a quantitatively derived, rather than narratively organized, framework.

## Field context

The paper explicitly positions itself within a small but rapidly growing cluster of very recent surveys covering the same general topic, directly naming and differentiating itself from several: Gao et al. (2024), which the authors credit with first framing AI agents as a new frontier in scientific discovery via autonomy levels; Ren et al. (2025), which focuses on implementation methods for agent core modules; Wei et al. (2025a), organized around correspondence between discovery-cycle stages and agent capabilities across four fields; Zheng et al. (2025), which proposes a similar agent-level and discovery-stage framework; Luo et al. (2025a), which extends scope to paper writing and peer review; and a separate line of work on multi-agent systems in science that the authors credit with arguing multi-agent collaboration can exceed single-agent capability by emulating human research teams. This paper's specific claimed differentiator relative to all of these is grounding its proposed five-level autonomy framework in the information-theoretic entropy/verifiability/dissipation lens rather than in a more holistic, role-based description of agent capability, which the authors argue makes level boundaries more precise and measurable, though as noted above this precision claim is not itself empirically demonstrated in the paper. Within this corpus, this survey's taxonomy of hypothesis-generation methods (particularly its Multi-Agent Systems category, citing AI co-scientist, ACCELMAT, VIRSCI, and AstroAgents as proposer-critic or simulated-research-team architectures) provides useful organizing context for other AI-for-science system papers reviewed elsewhere in this corpus, such as [26mira], which can be read as a concrete instance of exactly the multi-agent hypothesis-generation category this survey describes; a natural use of this survey for future synthesis work is as a reference map for classifying where any individual AI-for-science system paper in this corpus sits within the broader landscape it charts, rather than as a source of new, independently-verified findings in its own right.

## Critical discussion

The paper's taxonomy and domain-by-domain cataloging (Sections 3-6) provide real, practically useful value as an organizational reference: the breadth of coverage (dozens of named systems across at least seven scientific domains, each linked to a specific claimed capability and a citation) is a genuine service to a reader trying to orient themselves in a fragmented, fast-moving literature, and Section 7's discussion of the Environment, Action, Observation, and Reward Problems facing agentic RL in science is a substantive, well-reasoned, and appropriately critical piece of original analysis rather than uncritical cheerleading, correctly identifying that the reward and action-space challenges specific to open-ended scientific exploration are qualitatively different from those in the domains (math, code, web navigation) where agentic RL has so far succeeded.

The clearest limitation concerns the rigor of the paper's central original contribution, the information-theoretic framework used to justify both the phase-difficulty analysis (Table 2, Figures 5-6) and the five-level autonomy ladder. The framework borrows precise, quantitatively-defined physics and information-theory concepts (Shannon entropy, the Second Law of Thermodynamics, Landauer's Principle on the minimum energy cost of irreversible computation) but does not define any measurement procedure, quantity, or computation for the "entropy" or "dissipation" values it assigns to each phase and information type; the resulting Very Low through Very High labels in Table 2 read as reasonable qualitative judgments (and this review does not dispute their face plausibility, e.g., that calling a predefined API is more deterministic than inventing a new algorithm), but presenting them through the vocabulary of a rigorous, quantitative physical theory risks conveying a degree of formal precision that the paper does not actually establish, and a reader could reasonably come away believing the phase-difficulty rankings rest on a more rigorous foundation than a structured expert opinion. A second, more moderate limitation is that the extensive domain-specific survey in Section 6 reports each cited system's headline achievement largely without noting the evaluation caveats that would ordinarily accompany such a claim, such as whether a reported improvement was measured against a strong or weak baseline, whether results were independently reproduced, or how narrow or broad the tested domain was; this is a reasonable scope choice for a survey aiming for breadth across seven-plus fields and several hundred citations, but it does mean a reader relying on this paper's Section 6 alone for a sense of the field's current reliability, rather than reading the original cited papers, could come away with an overly uniform impression of how solid these many individually-reported results actually are.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the three-phase decomposition of the scientific discovery lifecycle and the accompanying taxonomy of technique families is a coherent, internally consistent, and citation-grounded organizing structure for a genuinely fragmented literature; the qualitative observation that routine tool use is currently far more reliably automatable than open-ended hypothesis generation or genuinely novel tool creation is well-argued and consistent with the broader pattern of results the survey itself catalogs; and the paper's identification of the Reward Problem, the Action Problem from unbounded tool-creation action spaces, and the Knowledge Closure limitation of purely text-based reasoning as the central open obstacles to more autonomous scientific agents is a substantive, specific, and defensible research-agenda contribution independent of how rigorously the entropy-labeled taxonomy that motivates it is formalized.
