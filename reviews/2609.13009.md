Citation: Ali Ansari, Haoran Sun, Andy Zeyi Liu, Mark Jabbour, et al. How Good Are Frontier Models at Physics? Expert Re-Grading Reveals Broken Evaluations and Near-Saturation of Leading Benchmarks. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13009v1

## What this paper is about

Physics benchmarks report that even frontier language models score poorly, which seems at odds with physicists' everyday experience that these models are quite capable in practice. This paper investigates the discrepancy directly: a team of Yale physics faculty and their graduate students manually re-examines every case where a frontier model was marked wrong on six leading physics benchmarks, to determine whether the model actually made a physics error, or whether the grader rejected a correct answer, or whether the problem or its reference solution was itself defective. The finding is that the overwhelming majority of "failures" are not model failures at all, and that once the benchmark materials and grading are corrected, frontier models solve nearly all of these problems.

## Extended summary

**Benchmarks and models.** Six closed-ended, text-only physics benchmarks are evaluated: three drawn from existing public physics exercises (UGPhysics, undergraduate; PHYBench, up to Olympiad difficulty; PRISM-Physics, advanced, final-answer only) and three built from original expert-authored questions (HLE-Physics, the physics subset of Humanity's Last Exam; CMT-Benchmark, condensed matter theory; CritPt, expert-curated frontier-physics challenges). Three frontier models are tested: GPT-5.6-Sol, Claude Fable 5, and Gemini 3.1 Pro, using each benchmark's own reported evaluation procedure to obtain "pre-audit" scores before any correction.

**Error attribution protocol.** Every rejected model response is assigned to exactly one of three categories by a subject-matter-expert auditor matched to that problem's subfield: model error (problem and reference are correct, model answer is wrong), grader error (problem and reference are correct, model answer is actually correct but the automated evaluator marked it wrong, most commonly a rule-based exact-match or symbolic evaluator failing to recognize an equivalent algebraic form or convention), or benchmark error (the problem statement or reference solution itself is defective: wrong reference answer, missing assumption, ambiguous convention, or internally inconsistent conditions). For the four benchmarks amenable to pooled review (HLE-Physics, PHYBench, PRISM-Physics, UGPhysics), 196 of 250 audited rejections were independently reviewed by two separate auditors, with 71.43% initial agreement; all 56 disagreements were resolved by a third review (Appendix F.2, Tables 3-4). Benchmark errors are excluded from the corrected evaluation set; for CritPt and CMT-Benchmark, where public per-question judgments are unavailable, auditors instead repair defective questions or reference solutions where a defensible fix exists (e.g. adding a missing boundary condition or spin-normalization convention) and exclude the rest.

**Headline results.** Across the pooled 250 audited rejections on the four public/expert benchmarks reviewable this way, 143 (57.2%) are benchmark errors, 95 (38.0%) grader errors, and only 12 (4.8%) are genuine model errors; in total 95.2% of audited rejections are not model failures. On the public-source benchmarks specifically, 148 of 152 audited cases (97.4%) are benchmark or grader errors and only 4 (2.6%) are model errors, with grader errors (mostly rule-based evaluators rejecting algebraically equivalent answers) dominating on PHYBench and PRISM-Physics. After correction, GPT-5.6-Sol's mean@4 rises from 47.3% to 78.7% on HLE-Physics and from 61.0% to 87.2% on CMT-Benchmark; on CritPt, its mean@5 of 32.3% (as reported by the third-party site Artificial Analysis, pre-audit) becomes a mean@4 of 87.5% and pass@4 of 94.4% on the 54 challenges retained from the 56 audited (of the original 71, 21 had defects identified, 19 repaired, 2 excluded, and 15 were never part of the audited subset). Corrected scores are computed only on the retained/repaired question subsets, which are smaller than the original benchmarks, and the pre-audit and corrected numbers are therefore not computed on identical question sets.

**A separately reported corroboration.** The paper adds a note that, independently, Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1/Mythos 5.1 system card reports its own expert correction of 31 of CritPt's 71 problem statements, on which Fable 5.1 achieves a pass@1 of 88.4% (mean@16, using a different model, judge, attempt budget, and corrected question set than this paper), which the authors read as independent support for the general finding that expert correction substantially changes measured CritPt performance, while cautioning that the two evaluations are not directly comparable given the differing setups.

**Discussion and stated boundary.** The authors frame their finding through a general principle: any benchmark's measured error rate is bounded below by its own defect rate, since a model marked wrong on a defective item cannot be credited no matter how it actually reasons; as true model capability improves and its error rate falls below the benchmark's defect rate, most of what shows up as "model error" on a scorecard is actually benchmark or grading failure. They explicitly do not claim this establishes end-to-end physics research capability: separately, GPT-based agentic harnesses (of a kind previously used successfully on open mathematics conjectures) were tried on several open theoretical-physics problems and made considerably less progress than on comparable mathematics problems, with none of the attempted problems solved.

## Learnings

The paper's central diagnostic — an evaluation's measured error rate is a mixture of genuine model error and a roughly fixed benchmark/grader defect rate, and the defect component dominates once model capability improves enough that true error falls below the defect rate — is a general statistical principle applicable to any fixed benchmark being used to track capability over successive model generations, not specific to physics: a benchmark that looked adequate when models were weak (because a faulty grader mainly just fails to catch already-rare correct answers) can become misleading once models get good, purely from the defect rate staying constant while the true error rate shrinks.

Splitting every audited rejection into exactly three mutually exclusive categories (model error, grader error, benchmark error), with a formal inter-rater-reliability check (double review plus a third-reviewer tiebreak) rather than a single auditor's unverified judgment, is a specific and reusable audit design for any effort trying to distinguish "the system under test is wrong" from "the test itself is wrong" in a graded evaluation, and the reported 71.4% initial two-reviewer agreement rate is itself a useful, disclosed baseline for how much of this kind of physics-problem adjudication is genuinely unambiguous versus judgment-dependent.

The specific mechanism behind most grader errors in this study, rule-based exact-match or symbolic evaluators rejecting a correct answer written in an equivalent algebraic form or physical convention, is a narrow but concrete and highly transferable failure mode: any automated grading pipeline for open-ended quantitative answers that relies on syntactic or normalized-form matching rather than genuine mathematical equivalence checking is likely to systematically undercount correct answers, and the size of the effect reported here (grader errors are 71.4% and 64.9% of audited rejections on PHYBench and PRISM-Physics, respectively) suggests this failure mode can be the dominant source of measured error, not a minor edge case.

## Verification

Verification is the entire subject of this paper, applied reflexively to other papers' verification pipelines rather than to the authors' own novel model or method. The audit protocol itself is transparently reported with a genuine reliability check: double review of 196 of 250 questions, a measured 71.43% raw agreement rate, and disagreements resolved by a documented third review rather than by unilateral author decision (Appendix F.2). The audit distinguishes three failure sources with a stated precedence rule (benchmark errors take priority when the statement or reference itself is defective, regardless of what the model or grader did), which avoids conflating a wrong-but-well-posed-problem case with an ill-posed one. For CritPt and CMT-Benchmark, where original per-question judgments were unavailable to the authors, every question in the audited subset was reviewed regardless of the model's original pass/fail status, avoiding survivorship bias in that portion of the analysis. What is comparatively less independently checked: the corrected reference solutions themselves, for CritPt and CMT-Benchmark, were derived by the same expert auditors doing the grading (there is no separate solution-verification step reported, such as a second, solution-blind expert re-deriving the answer independently), so the corrected ground truth's own error rate is not itself measured the way the original benchmarks' error rates were.

## Field context

This paper directly re-examines six benchmarks constructed by other groups, most centrally CritPt, which is already reviewed in this corpus as "[Probing the Critical Point (CritPt) of AI Reasoning](2509.26574.md)." That earlier paper reported GPT-5 (high effort, with code interpreter and web search) reaching only 12.6% challenge-level accuracy, framed as evidence of "a large disconnect between current model capabilities and realistic physics research demands," and this review's Critical discussion flagged that CritPt's central "junior-researcher-level difficulty" framing rested entirely on the problem-authors' own subjective judgment rather than an independently measured human baseline. The present paper does not address that specific human-baseline gap, but it does directly complicate the low-capability half of CritPt's narrative: after expert re-grading and repair of 19 of 56 audited CritPt challenges (excluding 2 unrepairable ones), a newer model (GPT-5.6-Sol, not the GPT-5 originally tested) reaches 87.5% mean@4 and 94.4% pass@4 on the 54 retained challenges, up from a 32.3% pre-audit mean@5. Because the tested model generation differs between the two papers, this is not a clean before/after measurement of the same model against the same fixed benchmark, but the magnitude of the gap between CritPt's original headline accuracy and this paper's corrected accuracy is large enough that benchmark/grading defects plausibly explain a substantial share of the originally reported difficulty, independent of any model-generation improvement. The paper positions its broader argument (benchmark defects compound as models improve, evaluators trade brittleness for flexibility, physics benchmarks lack SWE-bench-style executable checks) against prior audits of benchmark quality outside physics that it cites directly: Northcutt et al.'s finding of pervasive label errors across major ML test sets, Bowman and Dahl's four criteria for adequate NLP benchmarks (including the specific point that a benchmark loses statistical power to detect improvement as accuracy approaches saturation), and a chain of SWE-bench audits (the original benchmark, SWE-bench Verified, and later findings that roughly 30% of SWE-bench Pro tasks were themselves broken) showing that defects survive multiple rounds of curation even in a domain with executable ground truth.

## Critical discussion

The audit methodology is unusually careful for a paper making a claim this consequential: a formal three-way error taxonomy with a stated precedence rule, double review with a measured and reported inter-rater agreement rate, and disagreements resolved through a documented third-review process rather than left to a single auditor's judgment, together with full transparency about exactly which questions were excluded, repaired, or retained and why (Appendix B, Table 2). The paper's own framing of its central claim is also carefully bounded: it explicitly does not claim frontier models can do end-to-end physics research, and it reports its own negative result (GPT-based agentic harnesses making little progress on open theoretical-physics problems) rather than only reporting results that support the paper's thesis.

Some caution is nonetheless warranted in how far the corrected numbers should be read. The corrected scores are computed on retained or repaired subsets that are systematically smaller than the original benchmarks (e.g. 54 of 71 CritPt challenges, 116 of 202 HLE-Physics questions, 49 of 50 CMT-Benchmark questions), and because the excluded fraction is disproportionately drawn from cases the audit identified as unrepairable rather than a random sample, the corrected accuracy on the retained set is not a direct substitute for "corrected accuracy on the full original benchmark" — a reader should not treat the two headline numbers (pre-audit accuracy on all questions, corrected accuracy on a smaller retained set) as differing only because of grading and problem defects, since the retained set is also, by construction, the subset the audit judged solvable at all (`narrow-evaluation`). The audit coverage itself is not uniform across benchmarks: PRISM-Physics and UGPhysics used only a single attempt per problem during audit-data collection (versus up to five for HLE-Physics and PHYBench), and the CMT-Benchmark and CritPt audits, while reviewing every question regardless of pass/fail status, report no grader-error counts at all because the original per-question judgments were unavailable, so the paper's overall 95.2%-not-model-error figure is computed only over the four benchmarks where a full three-way breakdown was possible, not over all six. The comparison with the previously reviewed CritPt paper's headline numbers, while suggestive, spans two different model generations (GPT-5 there, GPT-5.6-Sol here) and two different attempt budgets and tool configurations, so the size of the apparent before/after gap conflates model improvement with grading correction in a way this paper's own text acknowledges only for the separate Anthropic comparison, not for its own relationship to the original CritPt numbers.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the specific, audited finding that the large majority of "incorrect" model responses on these six benchmarks, when checked by a subject-matter expert against the actual problem statement and reference solution, turn out to reflect benchmark or grading defects rather than genuine physics errors by the model, backed by a transparent, inter-rater-checked audit protocol rather than a single reviewer's unverified assessment; the specific and highly reproducible finding that rule-based exact-match or symbolic evaluators systematically reject algebraically or conventionally equivalent correct answers; and the general principle, argued from first principles and consistent with the reported numbers, that a fixed benchmark's usefulness for tracking capability degrades once true model error falls near or below the benchmark's own defect rate.

## Relevance to us

This paper is a direct, well-documented instance of the verifier wall problem central to our own physics-verification programme: it shows empirically, on real physics benchmarks graded against written reference solutions, that once a model's true capability improves enough, the great majority of its measured "errors" are actually grader or benchmark defects rather than genuine reasoning failures, and that this effect grows worse (not better) as models improve, exactly the dynamic our PVRE line design already anticipates (false pass/fail is worse than an honest "undecided," and a checker's own error rate needs to be characterized, not assumed away). The paper's specific dominant failure mode, rule-based exact-match or symbolic graders rejecting algebraically or conventionally equivalent correct answers, is a concrete argument for building any physics answer-checker (ours or a benchmark's) around genuine mathematical/physical equivalence rather than syntactic matching, and for treating the checker's own precision as a quantity to measure and report, not assume. The paper's audit protocol itself, a formal three-way error taxonomy with double review and a measured, disclosed inter-rater agreement rate, is a directly reusable template for auditing our own or any third party's graded physics evaluation, distinct from the informal spot-checking that most benchmark papers (including the earlier-reviewed CritPt) rely on. Separately, its brief negative result on agentic harnesses making little progress on open theoretical-physics problems, contrasted with their reported success on open mathematics conjectures, is a small but directly relevant data point for the frontier-backbone-vs-specialized-system tension: general problem-solving capability transferring well to mathematics does not currently transfer as well to open physics research, which is a scoped, physics-specific gap this paper reports rather than one this review infers.
