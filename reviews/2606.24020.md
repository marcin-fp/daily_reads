## What this paper is about

Modern LLM releases report scores on dozens of benchmarks, and many more evaluations are run behind the scenes during development, at real cost in time and compute. Two Microsoft Research authors ask whether this is necessary: given a public matrix of which models scored what on which benchmarks, is a model's entire scorecard predictable from just a handful of its own scores? They compile an 84-model x 133-benchmark public score matrix, find it behaves as if only two underlying numbers determine most of a model's performance everywhere, and build BENCHPRESS, a simple matrix-completion method that exploits this structure to recover a model's full scorecard from as few as five benchmark scores, to within about four points of the true value.

## Extended summary

The score matrix is built by crawling official sources (release blogs, system cards, technical reports, model cards) for a seed set of well-known models, recursively following every newly mentioned competitor model, then sweeping fixed leaderboards (MathArena, ARC-Prize, Terminal-Bench, LMArena, Epoch AI, LiveBench) to fill remaining gaps. Conflicting reports for the same cell are resolved by a fixed source-priority order (release blog > system card > technical report > HuggingFace card > leaderboard), and one canonical model-setting and one canonical benchmark-column are kept per variant family to avoid inflating apparent coverage with near-duplicates. The authors are explicit about two data-quality problems they knowingly retain rather than filter away: evaluation heterogeneity (different prompting, reasoning budget, sampling temperature, harness, and benchmark version across sources; citing prior work that harness choice alone can shift a score by more than 5 points) and structured missingness (popular models and popular benchmarks are systematically over-represented, violating the uniform-sampling assumption behind standard matrix-completion theory). After canonicalization and a threshold filter (minimum 15 observations per model, 8 per benchmark), the working matrix has 84 models, 133 benchmarks, and 2,604 observed cells (23.3% fill); roughly four in five observed cells come from a model provider's own materials.

The paper's first empirical finding is that this matrix is effectively rank-2: a rank-sweep of Soft-Impute matrix completion minimizes held-out prediction error at rank 2 in both raw and logit-transformed score space, and independently, SVDs of the largest fully-observed submatrices at several benchmark-coverage levels show two components explaining over 90% of variance in every case. The first component tracks general capability (all 133 benchmarks load on it with the same sign and similar magnitude, and it correlates at Spearman 0.893 with each model's naive mean z-scored score); the second separates domains rather than ranking models by strength (coding/long-context benchmarks load oppositely from agentic-search/competition-math benchmarks). Building on this, the authors compare seven feature transforms (identity, log, logit, arcsinh, square-root, probit, quantile) crossed with twelve prediction methods (from simple benchmark/model means through KNN, per-benchmark/per-model regression, several matrix-factorization variants, and a small MLP baseline) on a 50%-per-model holdout, evaluated over 10 seeds x 3 folds (~20,000 test predictions per configuration). Regression-based methods (BenchReg, ModelReg) top the leaderboard but leave some cells unpredicted; among full-coverage methods, logit-transformed, bias-decomposed alternating least squares (rank 2, regularization 0.1) is essentially tied with the best regression configurations at 4.6 median absolute error (MedAE) points, and is adopted as BENCHPRESS's default predictor specifically because it predicts every cell rather than leaving gaps.

A direct comparison against prompting a frontier LLM (GPT-5.5) to predict scores from five nearest-peer examples finds that when real model/benchmark names are visible, the LLM does noticeably better than BENCHPRESS (5.86 vs. 7.77 MedAPE); but in a blind condition where names are anonymized and only the numeric peer structure remains, the LLM's advantage nearly disappears (7.89 vs. 7.77 MedAPE), indicating the named-condition advantage comes substantially from public model/benchmark reputation and possibly memorized leaderboard facts rather than from a more sophisticated numerical inference, and BENCHPRESS remains a cheaper, deterministic, one-time-fit alternative.

Three downstream evaluations test what this predictor enables. First, budgeted scorecard recovery: a greedy probe-set search selects ten benchmarks (heavily reasoning/math-weighted: GPQA-Diamond, HLE, AIME variants, MMLU-Pro, ARC-AGI) that, revealed in sequence, drive pooled error down far faster than a random benchmark ordering; with only five probes, pooled MedAE reaches 3.93 points (4.55 when restricted to a low-cost benchmark allowlist). Second, ranking preservation: treating the completed matrix as a leaderboard, BENCHPRESS preserves 92.1% of pairwise same-benchmark model rankings when the true score gap is at least 5 points (83.8% at zero margin, where many near-ties are inherently fragile to any prediction error). Third, a genuinely held-out test on 27 models released after a temporal training cutoff (post-DeepSeek-R1 through GPT-5.1, each trained only on models released strictly earlier) finds that revealing just 1/5/10 of a new model's own scores brings median error down from 9.20 to 4.83 to 2.57 points respectively, showing a small seed evaluation on the new release itself contributes more than any amount of older historical data.

The paper then runs 16 pre-registered, FDR-corrected (Benjamini-Hochberg, alpha=0.01) hypothesis tests to identify what makes a prediction reliable. On the benchmark side, wider score spread across models increases error, more observed scores on the target benchmark reduces it, and having a strongly correlated (r >= 0.85) neighbor benchmark in the matrix reduces it; benchmark category membership alone (once correlational structure is controlled for) does not matter. On the model side, reasoning models are easier to predict than non-reasoning ones, higher-scoring models are easier than lower-scoring ones, having a strongly correlated (r >= 0.95) peer model helps, more observed scores on the target model helps, and having temporally recent models in the training matrix helps; model size and same-provider membership (once profile similarity is controlled for) do not reach significance under both metrics. A hybrid reliability estimator combining predictor-disagreement features with these matrix-support features achieves 1.83 MedAE when restricted to the most-trusted 20% of predictions, outperforming either feature family alone, and is proposed as a practical triage signal (with a corresponding conformal 90% prediction interval detailed in the appendix) for deciding which predictions are safe to use versus which cells still warrant actually running the benchmark.

The Discussion section explicitly separates what the paper shows from what it does not: prediction targets benchmark scores that happen to be currently inferable from the public matrix, not a claim that any benchmark's existence is unnecessary (running an eval can still surface failure modes, contamination signals, or new-data performance that aggregate score prediction cannot). The authors name six specific limitations paired with a proposed next step: no reliable prediction for models without a close matrix neighbor (candidate fix: incorporate external metadata like architecture/training-data composition); no support for instance-level (within-benchmark) prediction; untested transfer to non-text/vision-language domains (audio, robotics, scientific simulators); no guarantee the rank-2 geometry persists as the matrix grows; correlational rather than causal reliability factors; and a reliability layer that is a practical add-on rather than a principled uncertainty-quantification method. Robustness checks reported in the appendix include: rank-2 selection replicated under a stricter temporal holdout that separates newer from older models; a direct comparison against Observational Scaling Laws (Ruan et al., 2024) on the same matrix, where BENCHPRESS outperforms both OSL variants tested (7.77 vs. 9.85/21.87 MedAPE), with the authors noting OSL's dense-capability-block design is a better fit for a different, denser-data regime than their own sparse public matrix; and replication of BENCHPRESS's advantage over median baselines on two independently constructed third-party score matrices (HELM, 83x207; Every Eval Ever, 653x498), plus a full re-run of the 84-configuration method comparison on an updated August 2026 snapshot (129 models x 253 benchmarks), where Logit Bias ALS remains the strongest full-coverage predictor.

## Learnings

The specific, actionable design choice of predicting in logit-transformed rather than raw score space, combined with a bias-decomposed (global + model-offset + benchmark-offset) low-rank residual term rather than a plain low-rank factorization, is a concrete and directly reusable recipe for anyone building a similar sparse-matrix score predictor over bounded percentage metrics: the transform handles the ceiling/floor clustering of scores near 0 and 100, and the explicit bias decomposition lets the low-rank term focus only on genuine model-benchmark interaction rather than absorbing simple row/column-level offsets.

The blind-vs-named LLM comparison (Section 4.3) is a transferable methodological pattern for isolating what a frontier LLM's apparent predictive or reasoning skill on a task actually draws on: anonymizing model and benchmark identities while preserving the numeric structure of the problem strips out reputation-based and memorization-based shortcuts, and the sharp drop in GPT-5.5's advantage once names are hidden (5.86 to 7.89 MedAPE, converging with BENCHPRESS's name-free 7.77) is a concrete, quantified demonstration that an LLM's apparent edge on a structured numerical-inference task can be substantially a knowledge-retrieval effect rather than a genuinely superior inference capability, worth checking whenever an LLM is proposed as a predictor or estimator over any public, potentially-memorized dataset.

The finding that models are more predictable when they have a strongly correlated peer already in the matrix, or a temporally recent model near them in the training data (H5, H9), and that same-provider membership does not add predictive power once profile similarity is already accounted for (H7), is a specific, useful caution for anyone building or relying on capability-prediction or scaling-law tools across model families: a model's predictability, and by extension how much a benchmark-score forecast for it should be trusted, depends on how well the training pool covers its actual capability profile in time and in kind, not merely on which company built it.

## Verification

This paper is unusually thorough in stress-testing its own central predictor rather than reporting a single headline number. The core rank-2 finding is triangulated two independent ways (held-out completion-error rank sweep, and SVD of fully-observed submatrices at several different coverage levels), and separately confirmed under a harder temporal split that trains only on older models and predicts newer ones. The predictor itself is selected from an 84-configuration (7 transforms x 12 methods) grid search evaluated over 10 seeds x 3 folds on genuinely held-out cells, with hyperparameters selected on a separate validation split rather than on the reported test folds. Reliability claims (which factors predict where BENCHPRESS will be less accurate) are tested with 16 pre-registered, appropriately-chosen statistical tests (Spearman correlation for observational hypotheses, paired Wilcoxon for intervention/ablation hypotheses) with an explicit Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction across all 32 tests, rather than an uncorrected multiple-comparisons fishing expedition. Generalization is checked three separate ways beyond the main matrix: a genuinely time-respecting holdout on 27 newly released models trained only on strictly-earlier releases; replication on two independently constructed third-party score matrices (HELM, Every Eval Ever) built by different curators using different collection methodology; and a full re-run of the method comparison on a later (August 2026) snapshot of the authors' own matrix. A direct baseline comparison against a specific, named prior method (Observational Scaling Laws) on the same data, rather than only against generic mean baselines, is a further, meaningful piece of verification, and the paper candidly explains the specific data regime (dense, complete capability blocks) where the prior method would in fact be the better-suited tool.

The main verification gap, which the authors themselves name directly in the Discussion, is that predictability findings are correlational: the paper tests a curated list of 16 candidate factors (H1-H9 for models, H1-H7 for benchmarks) for association with prediction error, not a causal or exhaustive account, and unmeasured confounds (architecture family, training recipe) are explicitly flagged as possible alternative explanations the paper cannot rule out. The blind-LLM comparison in Section 4.3 uses a single LLM (GPT-5.5) as the sole point of comparison, so the specific finding that a frontier LLM's edge is mostly name-driven rather than inference-driven is demonstrated for one model rather than shown to generalize across LLM families.

## Field context

The paper explicitly builds on and extends two established lines of work it cites directly: findings of low-dimensional ("g-factor" or few-factor) structure in benchmark score matrices (Burnell et al., 2023a on HELM; Ilic and Gignac, 2024 on the Open LLM Leaderboard; Burnham, 2025 on the Epoch AI Capabilities Index), and prior fixed-panel score-prediction work (Aitchison et al., 2023's Atari-5; Ruan et al., 2024's Observational Scaling Laws), which the authors distinguish from their own contribution by removing the assumption of a fixed, pre-specified, densely-observed input panel: BENCHPRESS instead predicts from whatever subset of scores happens to be available for a new model, without requiring the input benchmarks or prediction targets to be fixed in advance. It is also explicitly positioned as complementary to item-level subset-selection methods (MetaBench, tinyBenchmarks, Scales++, DISCO, SubLIME, EssenceBench), which reduce cost within a single benchmark by choosing which test items to run, whereas BENCHPRESS operates across benchmarks and could in principle be combined with those methods for compounded savings. Within this corpus, the closest connection is [reviews/2603.28052.md](2603.28052.md) (Meta-Harness), reviewed earlier: that paper's finding that harness design choices alone can swing a model's score on the same benchmark by up to 6x is precisely the kind of "evaluation heterogeneity" this paper's own Scope-and-caveats section names as a fundamental ceiling on what its score matrix can represent, since BENCHPRESS predicts what the heterogeneous public record extrapolates to, not what a single controlled harness would report; read together, the two papers describe complementary sources of noise and structure in the same underlying object (aggregate benchmark scores), one showing how much of that noise is attributable to harness engineering, the other showing how much shared signal survives it anyway.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central empirical claims are well-supported by a genuinely broad set of independent checks: the rank-2 finding, the predictor's accuracy, and its generalization to new models and new score matrices are each tested more than one way, and the authors are careful throughout to distinguish "this score is currently predictable from the public record" from the stronger, unsupported claim that "the underlying benchmark run is unnecessary," explicitly listing what running an eval would still reveal that score prediction cannot (contamination, new failure modes, distributional shift). The direct, same-data comparison against a named prior method (Observational Scaling Laws) rather than only generic baselines, and the explicit statement of the regime where that prior method would actually be preferable, is a level of comparative honesty not universal in benchmark-methodology papers.

Two limitations are worth naming directly, both already partially acknowledged by the authors. First, the score matrix is built almost entirely from self-reported, provider-favorable sources (roughly four in five observed cells come from a model's own release materials) with acknowledged "structured missingness" toward popular models and popular benchmarks; while the cross-validation on two independently curated third-party matrices (HELM, Every Eval Ever) meaningfully mitigates the concern that the rank-2 structure is an artifact of self-reporting selection bias rather than genuine shared capability, those matrices are still built largely from similarly public, non-randomly-sampled sources, so a residual possibility remains that some of the observed low-rank structure partly reflects which benchmarks providers systematically choose to report (or omit) rather than purely a property of underlying model capability. Second, the specific comparison used to argue BENCHPRESS is competitive with, or preferable to, using an LLM directly for score prediction (Section 4.3) rests on a single model (GPT-5.5) as the LLM predictor; the qualitative finding that named-condition performance is substantially reputation-driven is a plausible and well-isolated mechanism, but whether the magnitude of that name-versus-blind gap, or BENCHPRESS's relative standing, would hold for a different LLM family is untested within this paper.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the core claim that a small number of a model's own benchmark scores carries substantial predictive information about its remaining scorecard is demonstrated across multiple genuinely distinct evaluation settings (in-matrix holdout, cross-matrix replication, and prospective prediction of models released after the training cutoff), not merely on a single favorable split; the specific numerical claims (roughly 4-5 point MedAE with five probe benchmarks, 92.1% pairwise ranking preservation at a 5-point margin) are precisely scoped to the paper's own snapshot and explicitly flagged as snapshot-specific rather than presented as permanent constants; and the practical, appropriately hedged framing throughout, that this method helps decide which evaluations to skip and which predictions to trust rather than replacing evaluation altogether, is consistent with the strength of evidence actually presented.
