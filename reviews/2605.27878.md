Citation: Li, Z., Zhu, Y., Wu, S., Bao, H., Evans, J.A. Narrative Flattening: How Post-Training Compresses Thematic, Affective, and Stylistic Variation in LLM Fiction. EMNLP 2026 (Main Conference). arXiv:2605.27878, 2026.

## What this paper is about

People who use LLMs for creative writing routinely complain that the output feels "flat," but that complaint has stayed impressionistic. This paper decomposes flatness into three measurable properties of a story's sentence-by-sentence trajectory — how evenly it moves between topics, how much emotional intensity it carries, and how much its style varies from one piece to the next — and traces how each property changes across the standard post-training pipeline (base pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, RL from verifiable rewards) using four checkpoints of the same underlying model. It finds that every stage of post-training compresses all three properties, that professional literary fiction is hit hardest because it starts farthest from where the model ends up, and that models trained this way converge on a single "voice" regardless of what kind of human writing they were asked to continue.

## Extended summary

The design is a matched story-continuation paradigm: for each story in three human-writing corpora (3,023 New Yorker stories 1945-2019, professionally edited; 230 Tell Me A Story (TMAS) stories, produced by 28 recruited writers under a shared prompt with a workshop review gate; 100 StoryStar stories, self-published on an open platform with no editorial gate), the paper truncates the story at one of four cut points (40/60/80/90%) and has four checkpoints along the OLMo 32B instruction path (Base, SFT, DPO, RLVR — same architecture, scale, tokenizer, and pretraining, differing only in post-training stage) generate 5 continuations each. Because the checkpoints share everything except post-training stage, the design isolates post-training's effect from confounds of model family, scale, or pretraining corpus (with one flagged exception: Base receives the raw prefix while instruction-tuned models receive the same prefix wrapped in a chat template, so the Base→SFT step confounds interface change with post-training — the paper runs a dedicated prompt-interface control in Appendix J to address this, and states explicitly that Base→SFT should be read as "interface + post-training" while later transitions are stage-only).

Three facets are measured per continuation, each validated with its own robustness checks. Thematic motion is the coefficient of variation of sentence-to-sentence semantic jump sizes (via text-embedding-3-large), capturing whether a story alternates dwelling and abrupt pivots (high CV) or advances in uniform steps (low CV); a length regression confirms this isn't confounded by continuation length (R²<0.001), and an order-shuffled null (Appendix I.1) checks the metric isn't just an artifact of sentence count. Affective prevalence uses a GoEmotions classifier adapted to literary prose via 12,000 LLM-distilled sentences (GPT-5.5 silver labels) and validated against 1,000 sentences independently annotated by three humans (family-level κ=0.68); the classifier is conservative on the focal high-arousal families (high precision, moderate recall on surprise-curiosity), so its errors are argued to attenuate rather than manufacture the reported gaps, and a soft-probability rescoring check (Appendix E.1, discarding the top-1 hard-label decision entirely) confirms the headline gap survives (41.1% human vs. 24.2% RLVR under soft scoring vs. 41.0%/20.7% under top-1). Linguistic diversity uses StyleDistance embeddings and Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) to the human style distribution plus across-story variance, with length-sensitive quantities residualized.

Headline results, reported primarily for The New Yorker with cross-domain results following: (1) Thematic CV falls monotonically from a human mean of ~0.105 to 0.096 (Base, -8.0%), 0.089 (SFT, -15.1%), and 0.081 (DPO/RLVR, -22.2%); a mixed-effects model with story-level random intercepts confirms the RLVR-human reduction (β=-0.0228, 95% CI [-0.0234,-0.0222], p<.001). Notably, mean adjacent-sentence jump distance is not smaller for RLVR than for humans (1.167 vs. 1.133) — what shrinks is the standard deviation of jump sizes (0.095 vs. 0.117), so the effect is compressed variation in step size, not slower or more cautious writing. (2) Affective prevalence: the Base model emerges from pretraining over-marked relative to humans (conflict ~47% vs. ~20% human; surprise-curiosity ~33% vs. ~21% human; neutral ~13% vs. ~29% human), and post-training does not return these to human levels but overshoots past them in the opposite direction (RLVR: conflict ~7.5%, surprise-curiosity ~13%, neutral ~45%). A family-level robustness decomposition (Appendix E, six affect families instead of three) shows this compression is selective rather than a global affect collapse: sadness/loss and warmth/affiliation stay comparatively stable across stages while the two focal high-arousal families are specifically suppressed. (3) Linguistic diversity: MMD to human style rises monotonically (0.24 Base → 0.41 SFT → 0.52-0.53 DPO/RLVR) while across-story style variance collapses (6.03× human at Base → 0.84× at SFT → 0.49-0.52× at DPO/RLVR); a within-prefix check (holding the same prefix fixed and looking at the model's own 5 samples) shows the model's own sample diversity also contracts (to 0.37-0.43× Base at DPO/RLVR), ruling out that the variance collapse is only an artifact of comparing one model against many distinct human writers.

Cross-domain analysis (Section 4.3) is the paper's second major contribution: human story domains differ substantially at baseline (The New Yorker separated from TMAS/StoryStar by higher affective charge and a markedly different style-PCA position, explaining 88.5% of variance), but post-trained endpoints converge across domains — cross-domain thematic-CV spread shrinks 62% (0.0116→0.0037), affective-charge spread shrinks 81% (17.8→3.3 percentage points), and cross-domain style MMD² shrinks from 0.61 (human) to 0.01 (RLVR), with all three domain centroids visually overlapping in style-PCA space at the RLVR endpoint. Because post-training pushes every domain toward the same regime, the domain that started farthest from that regime (The New Yorker) is compressed the most — the smaller StoryStar/TMAS gaps do not indicate the model handles those domains better, only that their human baselines were already closer to the model's default. The paper also runs Base-vs-Instruct endpoint checks (not stage-wise) on three other model families (Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-3-12B), finding the same directional reduction in affective charge and thematic CV in all nine family-domain cells tested, though style compression is described as less uniform across families. The Discussion explicitly considers and rejects a "professionalization" alternative reading (that post-training is just imitating what literary editors do): the post-trained endpoint overshoots past human affect levels rather than approaching them, and moves most aggressively away from The New Yorker specifically, the domain literary editing actually produces.

## Learnings

The most transferable methodological idea is the matched, single-lineage, multi-checkpoint design itself: by comparing Base/SFT/DPO/RLVR checkpoints that share architecture, scale, tokenizer, and pretraining, the paper isolates post-training-stage effects in a way that a single base-vs-aligned-endpoint comparison (the norm in most cited prior work — Kirk et al. 2024, O'Mahony et al. 2024, Murthy et al. 2025) cannot: it shows the compression accumulates progressively across SFT→DPO→RLVR rather than appearing all at once, which is a substantively different and more diagnostic finding than "alignment reduces diversity."

A second learning is the cross-domain design's specific methodological point, stated explicitly in the Discussion ("What the cross-domain design reveals"): evaluating a model's fiction-writing quality against a single pooled human reference can produce arbitrarily different conclusions depending on which reference is chosen (evaluating against StoryStar alone looks "largely faithful"; against The New Yorker alone looks "catastrophic"), because the human-model gap is a function of both where the model converges to and which human baseline is used to measure the gap, not a fixed model property. This is a generally useful caution for any paper (in this corpus or elsewhere) that reports a single human-baseline comparison for an open-ended generation quality claim.

A third learning is the specific decomposition showing that "less thematic variation" does not mean "smaller topic jumps": RLVR's mean adjacent-sentence semantic distance is actually marginally larger than the human mean, and what collapses is the standard deviation of jump sizes (the alternation between dwelling and pivoting). This distinguishes "the model moves through topics more cautiously" from "the model moves through topics more uniformly" — a mechanistic distinction that would be invisible to a metric measuring only average step size, and a useful methodological reminder that variance and mean can tell opposite stories about the same aggregate behavior.

## Verification

Verification here means metric validity, not claim-checking against citations, and the paper invests heavily in it, more so than most empirical papers in this corpus. Each of the three facets has a dedicated validity argument: the affect classifier is validated against independent human annotation (κ=0.68 at family level) and checked against a soft-probability rescoring that discards the top-1 label decision entirely (the headline gap survives, narrowing only slightly); the thematic-motion metric is checked against an order-shuffled null and a length regression (R²<0.001, ruling out a length confound); and the style-variance collapse is checked against a fixed-sentence-count control, a within-prefix sample-diversity check (ruling out that the collapse is only visible when comparing one model to many distinct human authors), and cross-referenced against non-neural surface features (Appendix F.3) as a check that the neural StyleDistance embedding isn't itself manufacturing the effect. The affect-family compression is also checked for selectivity via a six-family robustness decomposition (Appendix E) showing the effect is specific to the two focal high-arousal families rather than a global collapse — an important check against the possibility the paper cherry-picked the families that happened to show the effect. All headline point estimates are reported with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (story-level or sentence-state bootstrap depending on the metric), and several key contrasts are additionally confirmed via mixed-effects models with story-level random intercepts. The paper is explicit and correctly modest about what this verification does and doesn't establish: it repeatedly states its conclusions are "geometric" (where the post-trained endpoint sits relative to human baselines) rather than "mechanistic" (why it moves there), since the authors do not have access to the actual SFT/DPO/RLVR training mixtures or reward signals to test candidate causal channels directly.

## Field context

The paper sits in a fast-growing empirical literature on alignment-induced diversity reduction (Kirk et al. 2024 on RLHF reducing diversity relative to SFT; O'Mahony et al. 2024 on mode collapse; Murthy et al. 2025 on reduced conceptual diversity; West and Potts 2025 on base models outperforming aligned ones specifically on creative tasks; Zhang et al. 2025b on aligned systems showing less variation than human writers), and explicitly differentiates itself from that line by decomposing "flatness" into named, separately measurable narrative dimensions rather than reporting a single diversity or preference score, and by tracing the effect stage-by-stage within one model lineage rather than only comparing a base model to one aligned endpoint. It also extends Doshi and Hauser (2024), a Science Advances paper the authors credit with showing that AI co-writing reduces collective output diversity at the level of aggregate statistics; this paper explicitly frames its contribution as supplying the structural account (which narrative dimensions compress, and at which training stage) underneath that earlier aggregate finding. The paper also engages directly with two mechanism-adjacent papers it does not claim to test causally itself: Hamilton and Mimno (2026), cited as tracing recurring names/settings/professions in LLM stories to preference data rather than pretraining, and Zhang et al. (2025a), cited as arguing that annotator preference for familiar text is a data-level driver of mode collapse — this paper's "geometric, not mechanistic" framing positions it as motivating exactly the kind of causal intervention those two lines of work would need to adjudicate which channel (preference data, instruction-tuning style bleed, RLVR verifiability pressure) is responsible for the specific compression pattern documented here.

## Critical discussion

The paper is unusually disciplined about scoping its own claims, which narrows the space for legitimate objections considerably; most of what would otherwise be first-pass criticisms (single human baseline, classifier artifact, length confound, sample-diversity artifact, interface confound) are pre-empted by a specific named robustness check in the appendix, and the Limitations section is candid about what remains open rather than presenting the work as more complete than it is. The one limitation worth foregrounding beyond what the paper already states is a scope point: the paper's central, strongest claim — that compression accumulates progressively across post-training stages — rests on a single model lineage (OLMo 32B). The cross-family checks on Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Gemma-3-12B are Base-vs-Instruct endpoint comparisons only, which the paper itself states "establish direction but not stage attribution" (i.e., they cannot show whether the effect in those families also accumulates progressively through SFT→DPO→RLVR, or arrives all at once, or for different underlying reasons than in OLMo). This means the paper's most novel and mechanistically interesting finding — that flattening compounds stage-by-stage rather than appearing as a single alignment-induced jump — is currently evidenced in exactly one model family, even though the paper's title and framing ("Post-Training Compresses...") reads at the level of a general claim about post-training as a practice.

A second, smaller point: the paper explicitly declines to test whether the structural compression it measures corresponds to anything readers actually perceive as worse or more interchangeable fiction ("Flatness is ultimately perceptual, and our metrics operationalize it through structural proxies; we therefore make no claim about story quality"). This is honestly disclosed rather than glossed over, and the paper is careful never to claim a quality judgment — but it does mean the paper's motivating frame (starting from the widely-reported complaint that "LLM fiction feels flat") is connected to its measured structural metrics by assumption rather than by demonstrated correlation; a reader should not treat "narrative flattening" as already-established evidence that readers find post-trained fiction worse, only that it is measurably more structurally uniform.

What remains well supported after these caveats: within the OLMo 32B lineage specifically, the finding that all three narrative facets compress monotonically and significantly across the SFT→DPO→RLVR path is backed by multiple independent robustness checks per facet and by mixed-effects models with appropriate random-effects structure, not merely by point-estimate comparisons; the cross-domain convergence finding (professional literary fiction compressed most because its baseline sits farthest from the post-trained attractor) is demonstrated with both distributional overlap statistics and a dedicated Discussion section actively arguing against the most plausible alternative explanation (that this is professionalization/editing rather than flattening); and the paper's explicit refusal to claim a mechanistic account of why the compression happens, or a perceptual-quality claim about what it means for readers, correctly bounds the paper's contribution to what its evidence actually supports.
