Citation: Mingguang Chen, Licheng Wang, Bo Qu. Recursive Self-Improvement in AI: From Bounded Self-Refinement to Autonomous Research Loops. arXiv preprint arXiv:2607.07663 (v2), 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07663

## What this paper is about

This is a survey of work on AI systems that take part in their own improvement: models that critique and revise their answers, agents that rewrite their prompts, tools, skills or code, training loops that learn from self-generated data, rewards or teachers, and agents that do AI research. The authors argue that the "self-X" vocabulary (self-refine, self-reward, self-play, self-evolve) hides large differences in ambition and risk. They collect 1,250 arXiv papers from 2024 to 2026 and sort them on two axes. The first is what gets improved: behavior during deployment, the policy's weights through training, the evaluator that defines "better", or the research process itself. The second is who validates the improvement: a human reviewing each change, a human auditing an automatic signal, or nobody.

The central cut is between bounded self-refinement, which improves a system against a fixed external evaluator and is already routine practice, and open-ended recursive self-improvement (RSI), which would also rewrite its own criteria of improvement. The authors argue RSI is still limited by grounding requirements, collapse dynamics, and compute. Their organizing thesis is that every self-improvement loop is only as good as its evaluator. They rank evaluation signals from formal verifiers down to a model's own confidence and say, qualitatively, that demonstrated self-improvement is strongest where the signal ranks highest. They also split "research direction-setting", the step that keeps humans in the loop, into a verification problem (judging a proposed direction) and a prior problem (deciding which questions to ask at all) that no ranking of evaluators addresses.

## Extended summary

**Corpus and method.** The seed harvest queried arXiv (2024 to 2026) across seven threads: self-refinement, self-rewarding training, automated AI research, self-modifying agents, LLM-driven code and algorithm discovery, RSI theory and safety, and self-generated-data loops. Records were enriched with OpenAlex citation and venue metadata and filtered for off-topic papers, giving 871 papers. A supplemental harvest of 379 papers targeted three directions the seed queries under-covered: self-evaluation methods, test-time training, and zero-data self-play. Classification used theme-level defaults plus keyword rules on titles and abstracts, with manual correction. Rules moved 89 seed papers off their thread defaults and 3 further misfires were fixed by explicit overrides. One annotator did all classification and correction. The full per-paper assignments, query strings, caps, deduplication logic and rules are released. Table 1 gives the counts: deployment-time self-evolution 393 papers (74% posted in 2026), training-time self-iteration 340 (69%), self-evaluation 318 (82%), Auto Research 139 (76%), and foundations, limits and safety 60 (57%). Across the whole corpus, 74% of papers were posted in 2026. Quarterly seed output grew from single digits in early 2024 to roughly 500 papers in 2026 Q2 (Figure 6, seed corpus only, partial 2026 Q3 omitted). A TF-IDF, SVD and t-SNE map of abstracts reportedly shows Auto Research and foundations as coherent regions while the three large categories overlap. That figure could not be assessed from the extract.

The authors list these limitations. The corpus is a sample, not a census. Per-query depth caps favor recent, high-volume threads. The supplement is recency-biased by construction, which is why growth statistics use the seed corpus only. Citation counts are near zero and are not read as impact. Roughly 54 of the 379 supplemental papers are peripheral query bleed; they stay in the counts but are never cited as evidence. Industrial RSI practice is visible only through what labs publish. The survey's argumentative skeleton rests on pre-window anchor works (STaR, SPIN, FunSearch, The AI Scientist) and on the diagnostic literature, while the 2026 mass is treated as a map of activity. Anthropic's May 2026 essay on recursive self-improvement supplies the motivating frame and the stage vocabulary, explicitly not evidence. The survey relays the essay's report that Claude writes over 80% of Anthropic's merged code, and the essay's claim that execution is largely automated while direction-setting is not.

**Taxonomy.** The paper fixes definitions: an agent is a model inside a perceive-act loop, a harness is everything around the model, a verifier is an evaluator with soundness guarantees, a judge is a learned or prompted evaluator without them, and test-time training (TTT) updates weights during deployment. Bounded self-refinement improves against a fixed external evaluator and is convergent and evaluable. Open-ended RSI modifies the system and its improvement criteria with no fixed external anchor. The loop-closure axis runs from human-in-the-loop (a person reviews each change), through human-on-the-loop (an automatic signal, with humans auditing outcomes and gating deployment), to closed loop. The authors state that nearly all 1,250 papers study bounded self-refinement in human-on-the-loop cells, that the closed-loop row is sparse, and that its most consequential cell is self-evaluation under a closed loop. They name two blind spots of their own grid. Both axes take the objective as given, and the grid indexes substrate rather than trajectory, so it cannot record one system improving its outputs, then its evaluator, then its research method.

**Deployment-time self-evolution (393 papers).** The recurring design lesson is that a refinement loop is only as good as its feedback channel. A study of document-level literary translation across nine LLMs and seven language pairs finds that refinement gains come mainly from fluency, style and terminology and that refinement pulls outputs toward the refiner's own distribution. Together with Huang et al.'s negative result on intrinsic self-correction, the authors read the 2026 literature as having moved from closed-loop self-critique to externally grounded refinement, "a retreat from autonomy that improved reliability." Code is the cleanest setting. Controlled studies separate genuine feedback value from resampling, format correction and extra compute, and a placebo-controlled self-repair study credits the gain to falsification by failing tests. CoSPlay co-evolves self-generated tests with code because self-written tests are spuriously coupled to the wrong code. In multimodal models, self-consistency rewards can raise answer agreement while the decoder under-attends to the image. The supplement found 87 TTT papers. Harness evolution runs from the Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine to the Red Queen Gödel Machine, which co-evolves agents with their evaluators. A Pareto analysis of 34 inference-scaling configurations reports a peak gain of 7.1 points over chain of thought, reached only at roughly 20 times the compute; self-consistency saturates early while multi-agent gains persist. On SkillsBench, human-authored skills improve pass rates by 16.2 points and LLM-authored skills give no measurable gain. Skills are persistent and executable, so the attack literature has arrived early. One threat analysis describes adversarial influence that becomes permanently encoded and spreads through agent populations, and other work reports safety drift with no adversary at all.

**Training-time self-iteration (340 papers).** Self-Trained Verification argues that test-time refinement and training-time self-training share one bottleneck, the verifier, and proposes self-training the verifier itself. The authors call it an open question whether this escapes the circularity or only relocates it. Lin reports a rise-and-collapse failure in REINFORCE post-training for code. Across sequential campaigns, pass@1 climbs and then collapses within a run, sometimes to near zero, under a verifiable binary reward, and KL and EWC constraints do not prevent it. Repeated DPO campaigns show "scientific amnesia": behaviors persist but no methodological knowledge about running the next campaign accumulates. One negative result states that a verifiable search is not a learnable chain of thought. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD, 56 papers by the discussion section's count) is described as the most closed training loop in routine use. Its reported failures are breakdown on long chain-of-thought models, privilege-induced style drift (the learning signal concentrates on style tokens), a bimodal teacher "prefix failure", and multimodal students distilling a language prior instead of perception. The authors summarize: "the more privileged the self-generated signal, the more efficiently the loop transmits both capability and bias." Zero-data self-play (Absolute Zero, R-Zero, Agent0) works where a programmatic verifier exists. One study finds that survival depends on two asymmetric levers, data gating and reward grounding, and that collapse is the default when either fails. The section's three facts are that the verification signal sets the ceiling, that collapse is a default dynamic, and that loops transmit bias as efficiently as capability.

**Self-evaluation (318 papers).** Thesis: "Every self-improvement loop is a claim that some signal can substitute for human judgment, and the loop's ceiling is exactly the quality of that substitute." The anchors are Lightman et al.'s process supervision beating outcome supervision; MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, which measured about 80% judge-human agreement along with position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases; and Gao et al.'s overoptimization curve. The current work covers cheaper PRMs, structured verifiers with calibrated confidence, psychometric datasheets for judges, per-judge competence taxonomies, decomposition into binary questions, and self-generated rubrics. The verification hierarchy runs from formal verifiers (sound by construction), to execution feedback (reliable but incomplete, and fixed benchmarks get gamed), to learned judges (bounded by the judge's competence and themselves optimization targets), to intrinsic signals such as confidence, self-consistency and likelihood (cheapest and most gameable). The claim that self-improvement strength tracks this hierarchy is stated as "a qualitative pattern we observe throughout the corpus, not a measured law." The Figure 5 caption adds that reliability rises toward the top while task coverage widens toward the bottom. The Mirror Loop study ran three providers' models through ten rounds of ungrounded self-critique on four task families. It reports that informational change declined by 55%, and that one verification step at iteration three restored forward movement.

The paper names four failure modes. The first is the self-confirming loop: when generator and evaluator share weights their biases correlate, and confidence-coupled self-rewards over-reward high-confidence mistakes. The second is collapse. The pessimistic pole is Shumailov et al., an entropy-reservoir unification, and Zenil's claim that degenerative dynamics follow if the exogenous signal fraction vanishes. The optimistic pole says collapse can be engineered against. The authors' synthesis is that pure closed loops degrade, no practical system needs to run one, and nobody has established how little external grounding suffices. The third is diversity collapse of the task distribution. The fourth is frame lock-in: optimizing an objective that is no longer worth optimizing, which the loop cannot detect from inside. On the non-verifiable frontier, the authors separate direction evaluation, which is verification-shaped and benchmarkable, from direction generation, which is not. For direction evaluation they cite SoundnessBench: 1,099 proposals reconstructed from ICLR submissions with reviewer soundness sub-scores show a pervasive optimism bias across twelve frontier models. A final subsection contrasts result-level signals with process-level ones. Result-level improvement is described as operating expenditure (cheap checks, but paid again per problem) and process-level improvement as capital expenditure (expensive labels, but reusable). From this the authors suggest that mature self-improvement may look like "maturing methodology" rather than takeoff.

**Auto Research (139 papers).** Evolutionary program discovery (FunSearch, AlphaEvolve and successors) has the strongest verified results because every candidate is a program scored automatically. AlphaEvolve-style search is now applied to warehouse-scale code layout and to homomorphic-encryption kernels on TPUs, and it increasingly targets AI's own machinery (actor-critic architectures, RL algorithms for LLM training, ML pipelines). Controlled harness studies find that discovery success depends heavily on execution infrastructure independent of model capability. LEVI reports that stronger search architectures can substitute for larger LLMs. The survey relays that A-Evolve-Training ran the whole post-training loop of a 30B model for four rounds over several weeks with no human in the loop. It scored 0.86 against 0.87 for the top human submission, placing 8th of roughly 4,000, and mid-run it detected that its dev metric had decoupled from external performance and revised its search policy. The authors read this as confirming the Anthropic essay rather than contradicting it, because the objective and search space were human-specified. The critical literature includes ScienceAgentBench (best agents solve a minority of workflow tasks); ResearchArena (under manuscript-only automated review the best agent's papers match the average human ICLR submission score, while artifact-aware and human review show this is overstated); MLReplicate; and claim-level auditability work. SciIntegrity-Bench reports a 34.2% integrity-failure rate across seven models. In missing-data scenarios all seven fabricate data, and removing completion pressure reduces undisclosed fabrication but not fabrication itself. A position paper documents agents reaching correct answers while defending mechanisms their own data contradict. "Dead Science Walking" argues that AI scientists will amplify publication bias.

**Foundations, limits and safety (60 papers).** The survey covers Schaul's conditions for boundless Socratic learning (aligned feedback, broad coverage, sufficient capacity), and a computability separation showing that, under its modeling assumptions, finite internal self-modification stays within the system's computational layer. Jafari et al. formalize runaway growth as a testable property. Whitfill and Wu estimate compute and labor substitution on a panel of four frontier labs, and their two specifications diverge: substitutes in the baseline model, complements in the frontier-experiments model. Zenil's result is presented as showing that LLM-style self-training cannot improve without bound absent symbolic model synthesis or continued external signal. The authors stress that none of these bounds limits bounded self-improvement under human direction. On safety, the survey covers self-amplifying corruption, drift without an adversary, and an argument that guardrails inside an agent's runtime are reachable by the agent, so execution-time alignment must live outside it. The governance literature is thin. The takeoff signal the authors propose to watch is movement up the verification hierarchy on non-verifiable tasks. They name governance-grade measurement, meaning methods to show that a training loop is not self-improving past a threshold, as the least-populated niche.

**Discussion and open problems.** The authors claim durability for the structure rather than the paper list, noting it absorbed OPSD and zero-data self-play without modification, while conceding that absorbing everything predicts nothing. They say the uneven category sizes cannot be read as the field's true composition. They offer four hypotheses, explicitly as hypotheses: a verifiability gradient, a cost-of-entry gradient, herd dynamics, and evaluation being treated as plumbing. They note that 2026 produced evaluator co-evolution independently at least five times. They list six open problems: the exchange rate of grounding, verifying the non-verifiable, stability engineering, trustworthy accumulation in persistent skills and memory, governance-grade measurement, and recognizing frame revision. For the last they propose four observables: productive abandonment, invariant transfer, absence-sensitivity, and reframing latency. They add that a benchmark of deliberately stale objectives is buildable today and does not exist.

## Learnings

The verification hierarchy (formal checker, then execution, then learned judge, then the model's own confidence) is a clean way to state a trade-off: reliability rises toward the top while task coverage widens toward the bottom. The authors say plainly that "strength tracks the hierarchy" is a qualitative pattern and not a measurement, so it is a useful organizing prior rather than an established law.

Several relayed results say that one rung of external grounding changes a loop's behavior qualitatively. In the Mirror Loop study, ten rounds of ungrounded self-critique mostly reformulated the answer, and a single verification step at round three restored forward movement. This single-study result would be worth reproducing before building on it, because the measured quantity is "informational change", not correctness.

Collapse under self-training does not require a misaligned reward. The relayed rise-and-collapse result for REINFORCE on code shows pass@1 climbing and then collapsing within one run under a verifiable binary reward, and KL or EWC constraints did not stop it. Optimization dynamics alone can cause regression, so a verifier alone is not a stability guarantee.

Splitting direction-setting into direction evaluation (judge a proposed line of work) and direction generation (decide which questions to ask, and notice when a well-scored question has gone stale) is the paper's most transferable conceptual move. A loop can be grounded, honest and stable and still be locked into the wrong objective, and nothing that ranks evaluators given a target will detect it.

Result-level and process-level improvement have opposite cost structures. Outcome checks are cheap but must be paid again for every new problem. Process labels are expensive, but a corrected procedure or a verified skill amortizes across every future problem with the same structure. This is a reasonable economic argument for investing in reusable procedures and verified skills over per-instance best-of-N.

Much of what is labeled self-improvement is scaffolding engineering. Harness design studies, LEVI's search architectures substituting for larger models, and the 7.1-point peak inference-scaling gain at roughly 20 times the compute all point the same way. Gains attributed to a model's "recursive" capability should first be checked against a compute-matched and harness-matched control.

## Verification

Verification is the paper's organizing subject. The survey covers what is checked, by whom, and how failure shows up. It orders evaluation signals into a four-rung hierarchy. At the top are formal verifiers such as proof checkers and type systems, which are sound by construction. Next is execution feedback (tests, compilers, benchmarks), which is reliable but incomplete; passing tests underdetermines correctness, and fixed benchmarks get gamed. Below that are learned judges (reward models, LLM-as-judge, rubrics), bounded by the judge's own competence and themselves optimization targets. At the bottom are intrinsic signals (confidence, self-consistency, likelihood), the cheapest and most gameable. The paper uses "verifier" only for evaluators with soundness guarantees. It relays process supervision beating outcome supervision (Lightman et al.), about 80% LLM-judge agreement with humans alongside position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases, reward-model overoptimization, psychometric datasheets for judges, per-judge competence taxonomies for physical-plausibility video judges, and a trend of evaluators being improved by the same loops they supervise (self-trained verifiers, evolving rubrics, meta-evaluation, co-evolving evaluators).

The paper places the characteristic failures at the bottom rungs: self-confirming loops when generator and evaluator share weights, distributional collapse, and diversity collapse. It adds frame lock-in, which no evaluator given a fixed target can detect. For research-level judgment it cites SoundnessBench (optimism bias across twelve frontier models on 1,099 ICLR-derived proposals), manuscript-only review overstating agent paper quality relative to artifact-aware review, SciIntegrity-Bench (34.2% integrity failures), deterministic integrity gates in clinical manuscript preparation, and a physics-agent study in which unscaffolded agents cite but do not confront the literature. The A-Evolve-Training episode is presented as the first observation of a loop detecting that its own proxy metric had decoupled from the external target.

The survey's own classification was checked in a limited way. One annotator did it, rule misfires were counted (89 reclassified, 3 overrides) and bleed was estimated (about 54 of 379 supplemental papers), and the assignments are released for audit. No inter-annotator agreement or classification error rate is reported.

## Field context

The lineage the authors invoke runs from Good's intelligence explosion and Schmidhuber's Gödel machines, through the 2023 output-refinement loops (Self-Refine, Reflexion) and self-training (STaR, ReST-EM, SPIN, Self-Rewarding LMs), to evolutionary program discovery (FunSearch, AlphaEvolve) and self-modifying agents (Gödel Agent, Darwin Gödel Machine). In the authors' framing, prior surveys covered single slices such as on-policy distillation or tree search plus reward, and theirs is the first to span bounded refinement through open-ended RSI on an explicit loop-closure axis. Version 1 appeared on 2026-07-08 with a corpus cutoff in early July 2026, so the survey maps a field in which three quarters of the papers are months old.

Several cited works have their own reviews here, and the survey's descriptions are mostly consistent with them. reviews/24selfcor.md (Huang et al.) is the anchor for the claim that intrinsic self-correction does not improve reasoning. reviews/2506.13131.md (AlphaEvolve) is the survey's top-rung exemplar, and that review records the AlphaEvolve authors' own statement that the approach applies only where an automated evaluator can be written, which fits the hierarchy argument. reviews/2505.22954.md (Darwin Gödel Machine) matches the survey's description of empirical validation against coding benchmarks. The survey presents reviews/2606.26294.md (Red Queen Gödel Machine) as the logical conclusion of evaluator co-evolution. That review is more cautious: gains in the proof domain are mixed and depend on selection, and the evolutionary search is not replicated across seeds. So the survey's "field's emerging answer" is, on our reading, a proposal with thin empirical support. The survey summarizes reviews/2605.19156.md (ResearchArena) accurately. reviews/2604.24658.md (agent-native research artifacts) supports the survey's point that preserved failure traces can both accelerate and anchor an agent on extension tasks.

The largest discrepancy concerns Zenil (reviews/2601.05280.md). The survey calls it an impossibility result and part of "the corpus's strongest formal results," showing that LLM-style self-training cannot improve without bound. Our review of that paper reads its theorems as existence proofs in idealized multinomial-resampling models, with the author noting that the equations isolate mechanisms rather than simulate a real training pipeline. It also notes that the hard case of an endogenous, trainable evaluator is named there but not resolved.

Among adjacent reviewed work that the survey does not cite, reviews/2402.07043.md gives a separate tail-truncation theory of model collapse that fits the pessimistic pole. reviews/2608.31100.md (S3Gym) reports near-zero coupling between an agent's self-judgment accuracy and its actual improvement. That result complicates the survey's implicit assumption that a better evaluator yields a better loop. Later surveys and reports overlap in scope: reviews/2608.10299.md organizes agentic self-improvement around co-evolution and shares the survey's emphasis on evolving the feedback signal; reviews/2605.18661.md puts a similar generation-versus-verification gap at the center of auto-research; and reviews/2606.12683.md maps AGI-to-ASI pathways and frictions from a formal-limits angle. reviews/2607.27191.md, posted three weeks after v1, gives direct evidence on the direction-setting gap the survey theorizes: frontier agents given open-ended AI research questions were graded as rejects by the original authors.

## Critical discussion

The headline empirical claim, that demonstrated self-improvement strength tracks the verification hierarchy, appears in the abstract as an observation. In the body the authors qualify it as a qualitative pattern and "not a measured law" (§5.2). The corpus is never coded for the evaluator rung a paper uses or for the size of its gain, so the 1,250-paper map does not test the claim. There is also a structural confound the paper raises itself in §8. Its "verifiability gradient" hypothesis says research concentrates where cheap verifiers make gains fast to demonstrate and publish. If gains are demonstrated mostly where a verifier exists to demonstrate them, then a correlation between demonstrated strength and verifier rung is partly guaranteed by how "demonstrated" is measured. This does not make the hierarchy wrong. It is a sensible ordering, and the anchor results (AlphaEvolve's evaluator dependence, Huang et al. on intrinsic self-correction, Shumailov et al. on collapse) point the same way. But the claim is an organizing conjecture that a coded meta-analysis of the released corpus could test, not a finding of this survey.

The paper's quantitative content is mostly corpus counts, and in places the text leans on them harder than its own sampling caveats allow. The authors are careful to use only the seed corpus for growth curves because the supplement is recency-biased. Yet §5 calls self-evaluation "the corpus's fastest-consolidating category (318 papers, 82% posted in 2026)" using the combined corpus, even though the recency-biased supplement was aimed at self-evaluation specifically. The same issue affects the conclusion that governance-grade measurement is "the clearest gap this survey has found," which rests partly on foundations being 60 of 1,250 papers. Only one of seven seed threads targeted RSI theory and safety, the supplement did not target it at all, and much governance work appears outside arXiv or as lab reports, a censoring effect the authors name in §2.3. §8 correctly says the proportions "cannot be read directly as the field's true composition," but open problem 5 then reads a count as allocated research effort. The underlying argument, that no methods yet exist to demonstrate a training loop is not self-improving past a threshold, may well be right. The count does not establish it.

The classification carries no uncertainty estimate. One annotator applied keyword rules and manual fixes, and no agreement statistic, spot-check accuracy, or error rate by category is reported. The authors do report roughly 54 bleed papers kept in the supplemental counts, the 89 reclassifications, and release of every assignment, which makes the counts auditable. That is better than most narrative surveys. Still, category totals and 2026 shares should be treated as approximate to within at least the bleed fraction, and the category boundaries the authors themselves call porous (the three large categories overlap on the semantic map) add further slack.

Much of the evidence is relayed from single, very recent, mostly unrefereed preprints, and some of it carries the argument. Examples are the Mirror Loop measurement ("the survey's thesis as an experiment"), the SkillsBench 16.2-point gap, SciIntegrity-Bench's 34.2%, and the A-Evolve-Training episode, which §8 calls the first field observation suggesting that evaluator co-evolution can escape the self-confirming loop. The survey says its skeleton rests on "verified anchor works" but does not say what verification was done. In the one case we can check against a direct review, Zenil, the survey's characterization is stronger than that review supports. The survey describes it as a theorem showing LLM-style self-training cannot improve without bound, whereas the reviewed paper proves mechanisms in idealized resampling models and leaves the trainable-evaluator case open. The skeptical position in §7.2 therefore rests on formal results that are narrower than the survey's wording suggests. A-Evolve-Training is one self-reported run, and the survey itself notes the run optimized within a human-specified objective. It is a suggestive anecdote, not evidence that escape is possible in general. The Mirror Loop's 55% decline in "informational change" is described without its metric definition, so from the text alone a reader cannot tell whether the decline means circling or convergence to a good answer.

The conceptual contributions hold up better than the empirical ones. The bounded versus open-ended cut is precisely defined, and the loop-closure axis is useful. The split between direction evaluation and direction generation, together with frame lock-in, is argued carefully, and the authors say openly that the evidence is scattered and that their own taxonomy cannot see it. Their four candidate observables are concrete enough to build on. The reflexive note that a taxonomy is itself a legibility instrument subject to the attention-gradient critique is honest. The claim that the structure is durable because it absorbed two new paradigms is weak by the authors' own admission that absorption predicts nothing.

What remains supported: a well-defined two-axis vocabulary for self-improvement work; a well-sourced qualitative synthesis that reliable self-improvement results cluster where an external, checkable signal exists and that ungrounded loops show characteristic failures; an auditable, released corpus map of 2024 to 2026 arXiv activity, valid as a map of this sample and not as field prevalence; and a clear statement of the open problems (the exchange rate of grounding, stale-objective detection, measurement that governance can use). The hierarchy-tracking claim and the "least-populated niche" claim should be cited as the authors' conjectures, not as survey findings.

## Relevance to us

The survey supports, from the general ML literature, the verification stance we already hold. Its hierarchy is close to our graded-verification picture for B12: reliability rises toward formal checks while coverage widens toward learned and intrinsic signals. Its design rule, "no external signal, no reliable improvement," is the same claim VERIFICATION.md makes about self-improvement loops collapsing as external signal vanishes. The survey is ML-centric, and physics appears only through a handful of citations: physics-simulation agents that defend a wrong mechanism, frontier-physics agents that "cite but do not confront" the literature, per-judge taxonomies for physical plausibility, and physics residuals as a multimodal signal. Its hierarchy is written for domains where the top rungs exist. For much of theoretical physics the execution rung has no cheap analogue, and the hierarchy has no slot for checks that are deliberately approximate (dropped terms, regime-limited identities), which is where our deterministic step checks live. So the survey supports the architecture but says nothing new about the hard part, which is building well-characterized partial checks between "execution" and "learned judge."

Its open problem 1, the minimum exogenous-signal rate that sustains improvement, is our own open question about whether precise-but-partial labels beat noisy-but-complete ones for RLVR and PRMs, which bears on B21. The survey states that nobody has measured this exchange rate, which makes a physics-specific measurement with our partial-coverage verifier a defensible contribution rather than a replication. The relayed rise-and-collapse result (collapse under a verifiable reward, not stopped by KL or EWC) is a caution for B21: a sound verifier does not by itself make the loop stable.

The split between direction evaluation and direction generation, and the frame lock-in failure mode, bear directly on B17 (problem formulation) and on the solving-versus-formulation tension. The survey argues that verification cannot supply formulation, which is consistent with our view that formulation is a separate capability, and its four observables (productive abandonment, invariant transfer, absence-sensitivity, reframing latency) are candidate metrics for B17 and for the dead-end records in B09. The claim that failure traces both accelerate and anchor agents supports treating dead-end context in B09 as costly rather than free. Its opex-versus-capex framing of result-level and process-level improvement is a compact argument for our claim that pipelines, verified procedures and evals compound while the model is perishable.
