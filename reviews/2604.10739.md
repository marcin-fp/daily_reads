Citation: Zhou, S., Ling, R., Chen, J., Wang, X., Fan, T., Wang, H. When More Thinking Hurts: Overthinking in LLM Test-Time Compute Scaling. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10739v1

## What this paper is about

Test-time compute scaling — letting a reasoning model "think" longer before answering — is widely reported to improve accuracy, and the implicit assumption in that literature is that more thinking is always at least as good. This paper tests that assumption directly: it tracks how the marginal benefit of each additional block of reasoning tokens changes as the budget grows, and finds that beyond a certain point extra thinking not only stops helping but actively causes models to abandon previously correct answers ("overthinking"). The paper further shows the point where this happens depends heavily on problem difficulty, and proposes cost-aware evaluation metrics and an early-stopping method that exploit this.

## Extended summary

Using budget forcing (Muennighoff et al., 2025) — appending "Wait" tokens to force continued reasoning, then force-decoding an end-of-thinking delimiter at the target budget — the authors evaluate DeepSeek-R1-32B and s1-32B across 32 budgets from 500 to 16,000 tokens (500-token increments) on AIME 2024/2025 (60 problems), MATH-500 (with Level 1–5 difficulty labels), and GPQA Diamond (198 problems, for out-of-math generalization). Sampling is at temperature 0.

Three main analyses: (1) Marginal utility (accuracy change per +500 tokens) starts strongly positive (+3.2%/500 tokens for R1-32B at low budgets) and turns negative beyond ~12K tokens on AIME (Table 1); peak accuracy is at 12K (55.8%), with accuracy actually declining to 54.9% at 16K. (2) "Flip events" — tracking each problem's predicted answer across consecutive budgets and classifying changes as positive (wrong→right) or negative (right→wrong) — show a flip-ratio crossover (negative/positive > 1) at ~7K tokens on AIME for R1-32B (Table 2), confirmed via 1,000-sample bootstrap 95% CIs (e.g., at 8K tokens, CI [1.21, 1.68], entirely above 1.0; Table 3). The same pattern replicates on GPQA Diamond (crossover ~10K) and is difficulty-stratified on MATH-500: optimal stopping budget ranges from ~1.0K tokens (Level 1) to ~7.5K tokens (Level 5), a 7.5× spread. (3) A manual qualitative pass on 80 randomly sampled negative-flip cases from R1-32B/AIME categorizes 67.5% as "genuine overthinking" (explicit reconsideration and rejection of a correct answer), 20% as "exploration divergence" (valid alternative method, execution error), and 12.5% as "degradation artifacts" (repetitive, unfocused continuation). A separate check on 312 samples where R1-32B naturally generated >8K tokens without budget forcing shows the same decline pattern (accuracy 71.9% for <4K natural tokens vs. 44.7% for >12K), and that 71% of these naturally-long traces contain explicit reconsideration language, with reconsidering samples scoring 12% lower accuracy — offered as evidence that overthinking is not an artifact of budget forcing.

The paper also defines "overthinking indicators" from the reasoning trace (hesitation markers, answer oscillation, confidence trajectory) and reports their correlation with negative flips (answer oscillation r=0.78, combined indicators r=0.82, 76.3% precision at 80% recall). Building on this, a cost-aware utility function U(t) = Acc(t) − λ·(t/t_max) is proposed with three regimes (cost-agnostic λ=0, cost-balanced λ=0.5, cost-sensitive λ=1.0); at λ=0.5, stopping at ~6K tokens gives ~50% compute reduction for ~6% accuracy loss, and an indicator-based combined early-stopping rule reaches 97% of peak accuracy using 60% of the compute. Limitations acknowledged by the authors: only mathematical/scientific reasoning tasks tested; only open-weight models (no proprietary systems); and while the natural-reasoning check addresses the budget-forcing-artifact concern, establishing the causal mechanism behind overthinking (versus correlational indicators) is left to future controlled interventions. Funding: National Natural Science Foundation of China (grants 72574098, 72504122, 72074108) and Nanjing University central-university research funds (grant 010814370338), plus named fellowships (Jiangsu Young Talents in Social Sciences, Tang Scholar) with no grant numbers given. No code or data release is stated.

## Learnings

The reusable framing is the economics analogy made literal and measured rather than asserted: marginal utility of reasoning tokens (accuracy delta per fixed token increment) as a first-class metric, distinct from the level-accuracy curves papers usually report — this is a lightweight addition (compute a derivative of an accuracy curve) that any test-time-scaling evaluation could adopt without new infrastructure. Flip-event tracking (classifying each answer change across consecutive budgets as beneficial or harmful, rather than only reporting final accuracy at a budget) is a similarly transferable diagnostic, and the finding that the flip-ratio crossover consistently precedes the marginal-utility crossover (both here and conceptually) suggests it is the more sensitive early-warning signal of overthinking onset.

The difficulty-stratified optimal-stopping result (7.5× variation in the ideal token budget between MATH-500 Level 1 and Level 5) is a concrete, quantified argument against any fixed, uniform token budget in a deployed reasoning system, and generalizes as a design principle independent of this paper's specific models or benchmarks: budget allocation policies should condition on estimated problem difficulty, not use a single global cutoff.

## Verification

Verification is central to this paper's contribution, not incidental. Statistical robustness is checked via 1,000-sample bootstrap resampling with 95% percentile-method confidence intervals on flip ratios, marginal utility, and accuracy differences, and the flip-ratio > 1 crossover is confirmed to be statistically significant at ≥7K tokens (p=0.038 at 7K, p<0.001 at 12K and 16K) rather than asserted from point estimates alone. The central overthinking claim is triangulated three ways: quantitatively via flip ratio and bootstrap CIs, qualitatively via manual categorization of 80 sampled cases (with worked examples for each of the three categories), and via a robustness check using 312 naturally-long (non-budget-forced) reasoning traces to rule out budget forcing as the sole cause. The manual qualitative categorization itself, however, is under-specified: the paper does not state how many people did the coding, whether coders were blind to the budget/model, or report any inter-annotator agreement — a real limitation for a categorization (67.5%/20%/12.5%) that is used as supporting evidence for the "genuine overthinking" interpretation.

## Field context

This paper sits in the young "overthinking in test-time scaling" literature it explicitly reviews (Chen et al. 2024a on o1-like models, Wu et al. 2025b's inverted-U accuracy-verbosity curve, Zhang et al. 2025's structural over-verification/over-exploration analysis), and adds a large-scale marginal-utility and flip-event framing plus a cost-aware evaluation proposal on top of that line. It is directly complementary to [reviews/2508.17627.md](2508.17627.md) ("The Evolution of Thought: Tracking LLM Overthinking via Reasoning Dynamics Analysis," reviewed earlier in this corpus), which studies the same phenomenon from a more mechanistic angle — identifying a semantic "Reasoning Completion Point" inside a single trace and an early-stopping detector built on it — whereas this paper works at the aggregate accuracy/flip-event level across many budgets and problems, without inspecting the internal semantic content of the trace. The two papers' early-stopping numbers (this paper: 97% of peak accuracy at 60% of compute; 2508.17627: reviewed separately) would be a natural comparison point for any downstream synthesis on inference-time efficiency, though the two use different stopping mechanisms (indicator-based vs. semantic-completion detection) and are not compared against each other in either paper.

## Critical discussion

The core empirical claim — that extended reasoning eventually turns from helpful to harmful, on average — is well supported here: it is shown on two model families, three benchmarks (one out-of-math), with bootstrap confidence intervals establishing statistical significance rather than relying on raw percentages, and cross-checked against a natural (non-forced) reasoning condition to rule out the most obvious confound. That combination is more rigorous than a typical single-metric overthinking claim.

The evaluation is nonetheless narrow in model coverage: only two open-weight models, both 32B parameters and both trained with explicit long-chain-of-thought reasoning objectives (DeepSeek-R1 distillation and s1's supervised fine-tuning), a limitation the authors themselves flag. Whether the same marginal-utility and flip-ratio dynamics hold for proprietary frontier models, non-reasoning-tuned models, or substantially larger/smaller models is untested, so the specific crossover budgets reported (7K, 10K, 12K tokens) should be read as properties of this model pair, not universal constants — the paper is appropriately careful to frame its contribution as the methodology (marginal utility, flip events, cost-aware utility) rather than the specific numbers, but a reader skimming the abstract's headline claims could conflate the two. The qualitative "genuine overthinking" categorization (67.5% of 80 cases) is the paper's most interpretive claim and the least verified procedurally: with no reported annotator count, blinding, or agreement statistic, it functions more as illustrative evidence than a measured quantity, even though it is presented alongside the paper's otherwise rigorously quantified results. What remains solidly supported after these caveats: for at least two 32B reasoning models across math and science QA, there is a real, statistically robust point beyond which additional test-time compute reduces rather than improves accuracy, and this point varies substantially with problem difficulty.
