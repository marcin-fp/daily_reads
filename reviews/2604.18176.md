Citation: Qu, S., Sun, T.-P., Wang, Y.-J., Liu, H.-Y., Xue, C., Xu, X.-F., Fang, H., Yang, Y., Wu, Y.-C., Guo, G.-P., and Chen, Z.-Y. QuantumQA: Enhancing Scientific Reasoning via Physics-Consistent Dataset and Verification-Aware Reinforcement Learning. Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), 2026.

## What this paper is about

Standard ways of making LLMs more reliable through reinforcement learning run into trouble in a domain like quantum mechanics. General-purpose preference reward models optimize for what sounds plausible and well-formatted, not for what actually satisfies physical law, and can be fooled by fluent-sounding derivations that quietly violate a physical constraint. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) fixes part of this by grounding reward in an execution signal, but the typical version of RLVR reduces a whole response to a single pass/fail outcome, which is too coarse for scientific problems where a response can be right on some axes and wrong on others at the same time — for instance, a derivation can substitute correctly into a valid formula (mathematically flawless) and still describe a physically impossible state (violating quantum mechanics itself). This paper addresses both the data and the reward-signal side of that problem for quantum mechanics specifically: it introduces QuantumQA, a large, multiply-verified dataset of 92,749 samples built with an explicit hybrid verification pipeline, and a Verification-Aware Reward Model (VRM) that keeps mathematical correctness, physical consistency, and instruction-following as separate signals, checks the first two with actual symbolic solvers wherever possible, falls back to calibrated semantic judgment when a formal check isn't available, and learns to weight these signals per-instance based on how much each one can be trusted for that specific response.

## Extended summary

QuantumQA is built through a task-adaptive generation pipeline followed by a two-layer verification protocol. Seed material is drawn from authoritative textbooks (Griffiths and Schroeter; Nielsen and Chuang), decomposed into fine-grained sub-topics by an LLM, then expanded into five task types (Short Answer, Fill-in-the-Blank, True/False, Multiple Choice, and Problem Solving, the last emphasizing multi-step derivation) using a heterogeneous ensemble of generator models with semantic deduplication (cosine similarity threshold 0.85) to control redundancy. A task-adaptive rule governs response structure: retrieval-heavy tasks are kept concise to avoid verbose hallucination, while complex derivations are given explicit chain-of-thought reasoning only when the generating ensemble itself judges the transition from question to answer to require multi-step logical or numerical work, deliberately avoiding forced reasoning traces on tasks that don't need them. Verification then proceeds in two layers: an automated layer where a Scientific Execution Suite (SES) of 12 modular symbolic-verification scripts (built on SymPy and QuTiP) checks mathematical equivalence/numerical correctness and physics-specific axioms (e.g., unitarity of evolution operators, positivity and trace constraints on density matrices) via a three-stage semantic-parsing-then-execution pipeline, combined with an independent LLM critic (deliberately kept separate from the generation ensemble to avoid self-reinforcing errors) checking logic and formatting; and a human-in-the-loop layer where domain experts audit stratified batch samples, rejecting and iteratively regenerating an entire batch whenever its estimated error rate exceeds 5%. The final dataset is stratified into training (~95%), a small development set (~100 examples), and a held-out test set (~5%, N=4,675), with the RLVR training subset built from harder prompts not used for supervised fine-tuning (SFT), plus a small pool of high-complexity prompts identified during the SFT stage.

The Verification-Aware Reward Model (VRM) is built on a Qwen3-4B backbone with two added prediction heads. Given an instruction-response pair, a Scoring head produces a continuous semantic quality score along each of three evaluation dimensions — Mathematical Correctness, Physical Consistency (together the "verifiable" dimensions, for which SES can in principle produce a deterministic trinary outcome: verified, failed, or inapplicable), and Instruction Following (a purely semantic dimension with no formal checker) — while a Dynamic Weight Allocation (DWA) head, conditioned on both the model's hidden representation and the SES's actual verification outcome for this specific instance, estimates how much to trust each dimension's signal. An Adaptive Reward Fusion (ARF) mechanism then blends, per dimension, the SES's deterministic outcome and the semantic score using a confidence parameter that fully trusts a successful verification, falls back entirely to the semantic score when no verification is available, and only partially discounts (rather than zeroing out) the semantic score when verification actively fails, preserving a usable training gradient even in that case; a reliability-weighted sum over dimensions then produces the final scalar reward. The VRM itself is trained via supervised distillation from an "Oracle-as-a-Judge" pipeline: instruction/response/verification-outcome tuples (including deliberately generated hard negative responses from an adversarial prompting track, alongside standard correct responses, across models spanning three orders of magnitude in parameter size) are scored by an ensemble of two large frontier models (Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Gemini 2.5 Pro) that are explicitly disjoint from all downstream policy backbones and evaluation judges used later in the paper, to avoid self-preference bias and information leakage; the VRM learns to jointly predict the oracle's soft quality scores and dimension-importance weights, then is frozen and used as the reward signal for the subsequent RLVR stage.

The main experiments compare, across three open-source 8B-scale backbones (Qwen3-8B, Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and the already RL-distilled DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B), a Baseline (unadapted), SFT on QuantumQA, PPO-based RLVR guided by the VRM (the paper's main method), and GRPO-based RLVR guided by the same VRM (to test algorithm-agnosticism), against frontier proprietary and larger open models evaluated zero-shot (ChatGPT-5, DeepSeek-R1-0528, GLM-4.6, Qwen3-Max, Kimi-K2-Thinking, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct) and against human-expert performance on a stratified 400-instance sample. VRM-guided RLVR consistently improves over SFT across all five task types and all three backbones, with the largest gains on the two models without prior heavy RL optimization (e.g., Qwen3-8B's Problem Solving score rising from 0.621 under SFT to 0.680 under RLVR) and comparatively modest gains on the already-distilled DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, which the authors attribute to that model's reasoning trajectories already being highly optimized, leaving less headroom for reward reshaping. The VRM-optimized Qwen3-8B's Problem Solving score (0.680) exceeds the zero-shot scores reported for ChatGPT-5 (0.642) and DeepSeek-R1-0528 (0.665) on QuantumQA's own held-out test set, which the paper frames as evidence that precise, verifiable reward engineering can substitute for raw parameter scale. A Best-of-N sampling analysis shows the VRM discriminates response quality well enough that performance scales monotonically with sample count, and that the RLVR policy's single-sample output matches the SFT policy's Best-of-32 output, which the paper interprets as RLVR successfully distilling an expensive inference-time search process into the policy's own parameters. On two external, out-of-distribution scientific benchmarks (the quantum subsets of SuperGPQA and of the PHYSICS benchmark), RLVR again improves over SFT and the unadapted baseline for Qwen3-8B, though this OOD comparison does not include the proprietary/larger-model baselines used in the in-distribution comparison. The VRM is also shown to generalize across RL algorithms, improving GRPO training over SFT on all five metrics, though slightly below the paper's primary PPO configuration, an outcome attributed to GRPO's more restricted sampling budget in this setup.

Several analyses probe where the gains come from. A verbosity check finds that, unlike the Base and SFT models (which show weaker, noisier length-quality relationships), the RLVR-trained model achieves higher quality with lower token usage and a clearer negative correlation between length and quality, offered as evidence against length-based reward hacking. A blind human error analysis on 100 sampled Problem Solving outputs finds RLVR reduces logical/physical-violation errors from 41% under SFT to 28%, alongside a rise in accuracy from 48% to 62%. A finer-grained analysis on 500 sampled trajectories cross-tabulates the SES's strict deterministic outcome against the VRM's own semantic-verification judgment, finding these two signals disagree on 61.8% of cases (31.6% pass semantically but fail the strict deterministic check, most often via minor syntax, calculation, or constraint-satisfaction issues; 30.2% fail both) — evidence that hybrid supervision genuinely captures a different, complementary signal rather than being redundant with a pure execution check — and finds Physical Consistency is by a wide margin the hardest of the three semantic dimensions to pass (68.0%, versus 81.4% for Mathematical Correctness and 92.6% for Instruction Following). Ablations show removing either verifiable-dimension signal (mathematical or physical) hurts more than removing the purely semantic instruction-following signal, and that removing the domain-specific execution verifiers (math or physics) hurts more than removing the corresponding semantic dimension, indicating deterministic tool-based feedback carries more supervisory weight than internal semantic judgment alone; replacing the VRM entirely with a strong general-purpose preference reward model (Skywork RM) under an otherwise identical PPO pipeline causes a sharp performance drop across every task type. The paper's Limitations section is explicit that the dataset covers only textual/symbolic derivations (not multimodal circuit diagrams), captures only formalized post-hoc derivations rather than exploratory or self-correcting Long-CoT-style reasoning traces, and empirically covers only quantum mechanics, though the VRM/SES architecture is argued (not empirically demonstrated elsewhere) to be domain-agnostic in principle, requiring only that the symbolic solvers and rule checkers inside SES be swapped for a different scientific domain's equivalents.

## Learnings

A single, memorable, dataset-sourced example shows that "mathematically correct" and "physically correct" are genuinely separable failure axes, not the same thing measured at different resolutions: a model correctly substituting the quantum number n=0 into the standard formula for a particle in a 1D infinite square well is arithmetically flawless, yet produces a zero ground-state energy that is physically impossible, since bound-state quantum numbers must start at 1 and the resulting non-zero zero-point energy is a direct consequence of the uncertainty principle. A symbolic algebra check alone would pass this response; only an explicit, separate physical-axiom check catches it. This is a clean, reusable illustration of why physics verification needs more than a math checker, worth keeping as a concrete example whenever explaining that distinction to others.

Decomposing a single scalar reward into a deterministic component (trustworthy when a formal solver actually applies) and a semantic component (useful when no formal check exists), then learning a per-instance gating function that decides how much weight each deserves rather than fixing that weight in advance, is a directly reusable reward-design pattern for any setting that mixes verifiable and non-verifiable claims within the same response. The paper's own ablation makes this a checked claim rather than an assumption: both a uniform fixed weighting and a hand-designed heuristic weighting scheme underperform the learned, instance-adaptive fusion, showing the adaptivity itself is doing real work rather than adding unnecessary complexity.

Swapping a domain-specific, verification-aware reward model for a strong, well-regarded, general-purpose preference reward model, with every other part of the training pipeline held fixed, is a clean and directly reusable way to test whether a specialized reasoning domain actually needs specialized reward supervision or whether generic human-preference alignment is "good enough." Here the swap produced a sharp, consistent performance drop across every task type, a controlled demonstration that generic preference scoring lacks the granularity to catch subtle calculation errors or physical-law violations, worth citing whenever weighing whether to build domain-specific verification into a reward model versus relying on an existing general-purpose one.

## Verification

Verification is this paper's central subject, applied consistently at three separate stages, each independently checked rather than assumed. At dataset-construction time, the Scientific Execution Suite's symbolic solvers check mathematical equivalence and physics-specific axioms (unitarity, density-matrix positivity and trace), an independent LLM critic deliberately kept separate from the generating ensemble checks logic and formatting to avoid self-reinforcing errors, and a human-in-the-loop layer applies a concrete statistical rule (reject and iteratively regenerate an entire batch whenever its estimated error rate exceeds 5%) rather than an unspecified "we reviewed some samples." At reward-computation time during RLVR, the same SES machinery produces a per-instance, per-dimension deterministic outcome that is explicitly fused with, rather than replacing, a learned semantic judgment, with the fusion mechanism itself validated by ablation. At evaluation time, the open-ended LLM-as-judge protocol (used for the Problem Solving and Short Answer ACCU metric) is independently validated against human experts on a 100-sample stratified subset, reporting both human inter-annotator agreement (ICC = 0.80, establishing the ceiling the automated judge is being compared against) and judge-human rank correlation (Spearman ρ = 0.82, with a reported absolute-scoring offset of MAE = 0.55 that the paper appropriately notes matters less than rank correlation for the paper's relative comparisons), plus a secondary robustness check comparing the primary judge (Qwen3-Max) against two other frontier judges (Kimi-K2-Thinking, ChatGPT-5) on the same task, finding consistent relative rankings. This three-tier, independently-validated verification design is unusually complete for a single paper. Two things are comparatively less verified: the SES's own false-positive/false-negative rate is never independently established against a curated set of known-correct and known-incorrect derivations the way the LLM judge's reliability is established against human experts — the granular error analysis (Appendix G) shows the SES and the semantic verifier disagree on 61.8% of a 500-sample subset, but does not adjudicate, via a third independent check, which of the two signals was actually correct in the disagreement cases. Separately, the Best-of-N comparison used to argue RLVR "distills the accuracy of extensive inference-time search" does not state what selection mechanism identifies the best of the 32 SFT samples at test time, leaving it unclear whether that comparison assumes an oracle-quality selector unavailable in real deployment.

## Field context

The paper builds on, and explicitly argues beyond, the process-reward-model and RLVR literature developed for math and coding (Lightman et al.'s step-wise process verification; Uesato et al.'s process-versus-outcome feedback; Setlur et al.'s scaled automated process verifiers; tool-integrated reasoning agents such as ToRA and CRITIC), framing its central gap as the fact that these formal domains have relatively abundant discrete, unambiguous intermediate ground truths (a proof step either follows or it doesn't; code either passes a unit test or it doesn't), while open scientific domains like quantum mechanics generally do not, making dense process supervision harder to construct there without an explicit verification infrastructure like the paper's own SES. It positions QuantumQA specifically against a documented scale-versus-rigor tradeoff in existing quantum-focused resources: QuantumBench (expert-verified but too small for training) versus QuantumLLMInstruct (large-scale but lacking physical verification), plus a cluster of quantum-algorithm and circuit-design datasets (QCircuitBench, Qiskit HumanEval, and others) that the paper argues target implementation and circuit-level correctness rather than theoretical derivation. On the reward-modeling side, it distinguishes its contribution from the broader multi-objective RLHF literature (Safe RLHF, HelpSteer2, mixture-of-experts reward modeling), which the paper argues balances general human preferences like helpfulness and safety rather than balancing verifiable domain correctness against semantic judgment, and from a concurrent work (TRACE) that shares an emphasis on step-wise process analysis over outcome-only supervision but applies it to vision-language models rather than physics-grounded scientific text. None of these specific precedents (Setlur et al., QuantumBench, QuantumLLMInstruct, Skywork-Reward, TRACE) are yet reviewed in this corpus, and this is the first physics/quantum-domain RLVR paper reviewed here, distinct from the pure theoretical-physics dissertation and the more tangentially physics-adjacent papers reviewed earlier in this project.

## Critical discussion

The paper applies a rigorous oracle-disjointness discipline to the VRM's own training-time supervision (using Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Gemini 2.5 Pro as an oracle ensemble explicitly kept separate from every downstream policy backbone and evaluation judge, to avoid self-preference bias), but does not extend the same care to the final evaluation judge used for the paper's headline open-ended metric. Qwen3-Max is the primary judge scoring Problem Solving and Short Answer responses in Table 1, while one of the three optimized policy backbones being scored is Qwen3-8B, from the same model family. The paper's cross-judge robustness check (Table 5, comparing Qwen3-Max against Kimi-K2-Thinking and ChatGPT-5) shows reasonably consistent overall rankings, which meaningfully mitigates this concern, but that check establishes general inter-judge agreement rather than specifically ruling out a same-family stylistic or lexical preference for Qwen3-8B's outputs in the particular head-to-head comparisons that matter for the paper's own headline numbers.

The paper's "outperforms ChatGPT-5" and "competitive with proprietary systems" framing, repeated in the Abstract, the Contributions list, and Section 4.2, is demonstrated only on QuantumQA's own held-out test set, where the small model has been directly fine-tuned end-to-end (SFT plus RLVR) on data from the same construction pipeline as the test set, while the proprietary comparators are scored zero-shot with no domain-specific adaptation at all. This is a reasonable comparison for the paper's stated "parameter-efficient alternative to pure scaling" claim, but on the paper's own out-of-distribution generalization benchmarks (the quantum subsets of SuperGPQA and PHYSICS, Table 2), the proprietary and larger-model baselines are not re-evaluated — only the Qwen3-8B baseline-to-RLVR progression is reported — so whether the specific "beats ChatGPT-5" comparison would hold outside the exact dataset the small model was tuned on is left untested rather than demonstrated one way or the other.

Two specific behavioral explanations offered in the Results section are plausible but not directly tested against alternative explanations: the more modest RLVR gains on the already RL-distilled DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B are attributed to that model having "limited margin for reward reshaping" from prior optimization, and the GRPO variant's slight underperformance relative to PPO is attributed to "the restricted sampling budget." Both are reasonable, domain-informed hypotheses consistent with the reported numbers, but neither is isolated by a controlled ablation (for instance, varying the sampling budget directly to test the second claim), so they should be read as informed post-hoc interpretation of the pattern observed rather than an established causal finding.

The Best-of-N scaling result (Section 4.2, Figure 3), used to argue that RLVR "distills the verification signal into the policy," does not specify what selection mechanism picks the "best" among the 32 SFT samples at test time. If that selection used an oracle-quality signal unavailable in a real deployment (rather than a genuinely deployable verifier or judge), the comparison would somewhat overstate what SFT-plus-sampling could realistically achieve relative to RLVR's oracle-free single-sample output, which matters for how strongly the "reduced computational cost for equivalent accuracy" framing should be read.

## Relevance to us

This is one of the most directly relevant papers reviewed in this corpus to our core interest in physics-domain verification and reward design, closer to our own subject matter than most other papers reviewed this session. Its central design principle — decompose scientific correctness into distinct axes (mathematical validity, physical consistency, instruction-following) rather than collapsing them into one label, verify each axis with the most appropriate tool available (a symbolic solver where one exists, calibrated semantic judgment where it doesn't), and learn an instance-adaptive fusion between the two rather than a fixed weighting — is a directly actionable blueprint for any reward-modeling or evaluation pipeline we might build for physics-reasoning tasks ourselves. The paper's showcased failure case (a mathematically flawless substitution that yields a physically impossible zero ground-state energy) is a concrete, well-chosen illustration of exactly the "physics is not math plus noise" distinction central to our own framing, worth keeping as a canonical example. The granular error analysis quantifying how often deterministic and semantic verification signals actually disagree (61.8% of a 500-sample subset), and the finding that physical-consistency checks are by far the hardest dimension to pass (68.0% versus 92.6% for instruction-following), together give a concrete existence proof that physical-consistency checking deserves independent attention and does not follow automatically from mathematical correctness, which is directly relevant evidence for any of our own decisions about how much separate verification infrastructure a physics-reasoning evaluation actually needs. Finally, the paper's own inconsistency — rigorous oracle-disjointness for training-time reward supervision but a same-family judge left unexamined for the headline evaluation metric — is a useful cautionary data point about how easy it is to apply a well-understood rigor principle inconsistently across different stages of the same pipeline, worth checking for explicitly whenever we build or review a multi-stage evaluation pipeline of our own.
