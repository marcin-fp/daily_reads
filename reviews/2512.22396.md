Citation: Vangala, B. P., Mahmud, S., Neupane, P., Selvaraj, J., and Cheng, J. HalluMat: Detecting Hallucinations in LLM-Generated Materials Science Content Through Multi-Stage Verification. arXiv preprint, 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22396

## What this paper is about

Language models asked materials-science questions often answer fluently and wrongly. The authors build two things for this setting. HalluMatData is a set of materials-science questions, paraphrases of some of them, answers generated by LLaMA-2, curated reference answers, and a Low/Medium/High hallucination label for each generated answer. HalluMatDetector is a pipeline that decides whether an answer is hallucinated. It first uses signals from the generating model itself (agreement across samples, token-probability spread, entropy, self-review). Only when those look unreliable does it retrieve text from Google Scholar, search engines, and Wikipedia and check the answer against it with a natural-language-inference classifier. It also builds a similarity graph over the answer's individual factual statements and ends with a thresholded reliability score. The authors claim a 30% reduction in hallucination rates relative to plain LLM outputs, 82.2% classification accuracy, and a new paraphrase-consistency score (PHCS) that shows answers to reworded questions can receive different hallucination levels.

## Extended summary

Dataset. Questions were taken from materials-science literature, textbooks, and research papers and cover material properties, phase stability, synthesis methods, and computational modeling. Each question was paraphrased to vary syntax while keeping meaning; the paraphrasing procedure (human or model) is not described. Responses were generated at scale by an automated pipeline using LLaMA-2 models (sizes and decoding settings not given). Reference answers were "carefully curated" from peer-reviewed publications and databases such as the Materials Project; who curated them and how they were checked is not stated. The per-response hallucination score is a similarity between the generated response and the reference answer computed with BERT embeddings, and the Low/Medium/High level is derived from it. Table 1 reports 2629 unique queries, 640 paraphrased queries, 3269 generated responses, and 57 Low, 872 Medium, and 2346 High hallucination responses. The dataset's hallucination scores were then "recomputed" with the authors' own framework: intrinsic checks first, and if those fail to establish reliability, extrinsic fact checking against Google Scholar and Wikipedia (Figure 1, an image, shows the recomputed scores).

PHCS. The Paraphrased Hallucination Consistency Score is defined as the standard deviation of hallucination scores within a paraphrase group, with Eq. 1 written as the population form (1/N) around the group mean. Table 2 lists the ten most inconsistent groups: three groups at 1.09545, two at 0.894427, one at 0.83666, and four at 0.547723. Group sizes and the total number of groups are not reported. The authors read high-PHCS groups as cases where minor rewording shifts the hallucination level, and argue for paraphrase-aware detection.

Detector. HalluMatDetector has four components.

- Intrinsic evaluation: a SelfCheckGPT-style self-consistency check over multiple sampled responses, which also produces "fact fragments"; confidence variance analysis over token probabilities; entropy-based uncertainty quantification citing Farquhar et al. 2024; iterative self-refinement over several rounds at different temperatures, where large deviations flag unreliability; and internal contradiction detection by the model reviewing its own response.
- Multi-source retrieval, triggered when intrinsic scores fall below a predefined threshold (value not given): hierarchical chunking of a knowledge base, cosine similarity between chunk and query embeddings, FAISS approximate nearest-neighbour search, BM25 reranking, then an NLI classifier (model not named; citing MultiNLI) labels the response as entailment, neutral (no supporting evidence, no contradiction), or contradiction.
- Contradiction graph analysis: nodes are fact fragments, edges are semantic-similarity links, Louvain community detection finds consistent clusters, and low-similarity nodes are read as contradictory statements. Figures 3 to 5 (a similarity heatmap and 2D and 3D graph renderings) are images only. The authors state that hallucination-prone queries form highly fragmented fact clusters.
- Metric-based assessment: ROUGE, BLEU, and BERT cosine similarity against the reference answer, combined with the intrinsic and extrinsic results into a reliability score (the combination rule is not given). Table 3 thresholds: above 0.7 is high reliability (factually correct), 0.5 to 0.7 is medium (requires review), below 0.5 is low (hallucination detected).

Evaluation. The authors compare hallucination levels "computed using existing detection methods" with levels "recomputed" by HalluMatDetector in a confusion matrix (Figure 6, image, values not in the extract). They report in prose that Medium cases were reassessed more accurately, reducing false positives; that a significant fraction of High cases stayed High, which they take as validating robustness; and that some low-confidence cases moved upward. The conclusion reports 82.2% accuracy on HalluMatData with precision 71.2% and recall 62.03%; the reference labels, the positive class, and the averaging are not specified. The 30% figure appears three ways: as a reduction in hallucination rates versus standard LLM outputs (abstract), as effectiveness "in detecting factual inconsistencies by 30%" (methodology), and as a 30% improvement in factual reliability versus baseline LLM outputs (conclusion). The text does not show how it was computed. The abstract and conclusion also state that hallucination levels vary significantly across materials-science subdomains, with "high-entropy queries" showing greater inconsistency; no per-subdomain numbers appear in the text.

Author caveats. ROUGE and BLEU are called insufficient for factual correctness. The authors say the current framework relies primarily on textual and semantic similarity and that domain-specific, fine-grained evaluation is needed, for example judging a high-entropy-alloy synthesis answer by whether it includes the right thermodynamic principles, techniques such as arc melting, and experimental constraints. They describe the precision and recall as moderate and needing refinement for nuanced hallucinations and false positives. Extrinsic retrieval is deliberately secondary because comprehensive structured materials databases are scarce. Future work lists Sentence-BERT, GPT-4, MatSciBERT, and BioBERT embeddings for PHCS, contrastive and hybrid metrics, RLHF-based mitigation with flagged responses in an active-learning loop, and extension to biomedicine (PubMed, DrugBank), computational chemistry, and physics.

## Learnings

When a relabeling system is evaluated only against the labels it replaces, a confusion matrix records how much the labels changed, not whether they got better. Here unchanged High labels are read as robustness and changed Medium labels as fewer false positives, so every outcome counts as success. A small independently labeled subset is the minimum needed to turn such a matrix into evidence.

Embedding similarity to a curated reference answer marks most free-form answers as hallucinated: in Table 1, 2346 of roughly 3270 LLaMA-2 responses are High and only 57 are Low. Whatever the true error rate, that skew means an accuracy figure has to be read against the majority-class rate, about 72% here.

The spread of a hallucination label across paraphrases of one question is a cheap probe of whether a verdict depends on wording. In this paper the reported values are consistent with the spread being computed over three discrete levels, so the top groups are simply those whose paraphrases split between Low and High. The probe says nothing about which paraphrase was answered correctly.

## Verification

Verification is the paper's subject. What is checked is the final free-form answer to a materials-science question, partly decomposed into fact fragments. Verdicts come from a stack of learned and statistical signals, not a deterministic domain procedure: the generating model's own sample agreement, token-probability variance, entropy, multi-temperature self-refinement, and self-review for internal contradictions; an unnamed NLI classifier comparing the answer with chunks retrieved from Google Scholar, search engines, and Wikipedia via FAISS and BM25; a similarity graph with Louvain communities; and BERT, ROUGE, and BLEU similarity to a curated reference answer. Retrieval is gated: it runs only when the intrinsic score falls below an unstated threshold, which the authors motivate by the scarcity of comprehensive materials databases.

Two places allow a non-verdict. The NLI stage has a neutral class for claims with no supporting evidence that contradict nothing, and the final score has a middle band (0.5 to 0.7) labeled "requires review". No domain-specific checks are run (no database lookup of a stated property at inference, no stoichiometry or thermodynamic check); the authors name this as future work. Reported performance is 82.2% accuracy, 71.2% precision, and 62.03% recall, with the reference labels undefined. No human validation of the labels or of the detector is reported.

## Field context

The authors position the work against general hallucination detection: sampling-consistency methods (SelfCheckGPT), entropy-based uncertainty (Farquhar et al. 2024), and trained discriminators (RelD), and against general benchmarks (HaluEval, DefAn). Their argument is that these methods either need comprehensive external databases, which materials science lacks, or ignore domain specifics such as complex compositions and property spaces. They describe HalluMatData as the first curated dataset for evaluating hallucination detection in materials-science content. The setup (LLaMA-2 as generator, BERT embeddings, an AAAI 2025 template) looks older than the December 2025 arXiv date, though the text gives no earlier date.

Against already-reviewed work, and as our comparison rather than the authors' framing: semantic entropy (reviews/24semant.md) is one of the cited ingredients here. That paper validated detection with AUROC against correctness labels across 30 model-dataset combinations and compared against named baselines. HalluMat uses "entropy-based uncertainty quantification" without saying whether it clusters samples by meaning, and does not report it as a stand-alone baseline. Selective abstraction for long-form generation (reviews/2602.11908.md) checked its fact-checking oracle against 102 manually labeled claims (F1 0.93), which is exactly the step HalluMat's evaluation lacks. MatterChat (reviews/26matter.md) goes at materials-QA reliability from the other side, grounding answers in atomic structure instead of detecting hallucinations after generation. HalluMat extends the sampling-consistency and NLI-over-retrieval line to a new domain; it does not challenge any reviewed result.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claims are that HalluMatDetector classifies hallucinations reliably (82.2% accuracy), that it reduces hallucination by 30%, and that hallucination varies across subdomains and paraphrases. The text does not establish the first two. It weakly supports the third, and only for paraphrases.

The main problem is where the reference labels come from. HalluMatData's original hallucination score is BERT-embedding similarity to the curated answer (L98). The dataset section then says the scores were "recomputed using our evaluation framework" (L108), so the benchmark's labels are at least partly the output of the detector it is meant to benchmark. The evaluation (L296) compares "computed" levels with "recomputed" levels. The 82.2% accuracy is reported "on the HalluMatData dataset" without saying which labels count as ground truth. If they are the recomputed labels, the detector is being scored against itself. If they are the original BERT-similarity labels, the detector's own final stage uses BERT cosine similarity to the same reference answers (L272), so part of the agreement is built in. There is no human-annotated subset, no inter-annotator agreement, and no description of how the 2629 reference answers were curated or checked. None of this shows the detector is wrong. It does mean the reported numbers cannot be read as detection accuracy against ground truth.

The confusion-matrix reading makes this worse. Unchanged High labels are taken as "validating our model's robustness" and changed Medium labels as "reducing false positives" (L302 to L304), so both agreement and disagreement with the old labels count as success. Without an independent reference, off-diagonal cells only show that the two labelers differ. The matrix counts are in Figure 6, which is an image, so even the size of the change cannot be recovered from the extract. The text also cites this matrix as "fig(3)" (L292), which is the similarity heatmap.

Controls are missing. The components (SelfCheckGPT-style sampling, entropy, NLI over retrieval) are all existing methods, yet none is run alone as a baseline, and the four stages are not ablated. So we cannot tell whether the multi-stage design adds anything over its cheapest part. Class balance matters too. High makes up about 72% of Table 1's labels (2346 of 3275), so if the accuracy is over the three levels, always predicting High would score about 72%, and 82.2% is roughly ten points above that. Precision 71.2% and recall 62.03% are given without the positive class or averaging, and no confidence intervals or run-to-run spread are reported, even though several stages sample stochastically.

The 30% figure is stated three different ways (L27, L268, L334), and no table or computation in the text produces it. A detector does not lower a model's hallucination rate unless outputs are filtered or regenerated, and neither is described for this number. It may come from Figure 1 or Figure 6, which could not be assessed. As written, "reduce hallucination rates by 30%" is an overclaim for a classification pipeline. "The first curated dataset" and "scientifically rigorous" are likewise asserted, not shown.

The reported tables are also internally inconsistent. In Table 1 the three levels sum to 3275, not the 3269 generated responses. For PHCS, Eq. 1 is a population standard deviation. Applied to a similarity score bounded in an interval of width at most 2 (cosine similarity), it can never exceed 1, yet Table 2's top value is 1.09545. The listed values equal the square roots of 1.2, 0.8, 0.7, and 0.3. Those are exactly the N-1 sample standard deviations of five integer-coded levels (for example 0, 0, 0, 2, 2 gives 1.0954, the maximum possible for five values on a three-step scale), and ties to six digits across groups also point to discrete inputs. PHCS as reported therefore appears to be computed on Low/Medium/High codes with a different normalization from the stated formula. The finding that some paraphrase groups get very different labels still holds. The metric simply measures label flips, not continuous score drift.

The subdomain claim has no support in the text: there is no per-subdomain table, no description of how subdomains were assigned, and no test behind "significantly". "High-entropy queries" is undefined. If it means queries with high model entropy, entropy is one of the detector's inputs, so the finding is partly circular. If it means high-entropy alloys, no numbers are given. The graph component has a mechanism problem. It treats low semantic similarity between fact fragments as contradiction (L245), but two correct statements about different properties are dissimilar without contradicting each other. The claim that hallucination-prone queries form fragmented clusters (L247) rests on visualizations, not a measured quantity, and the graph stage's contribution to the final score is never isolated. Reporting gaps compound all this: no LLaMA-2 size, sample counts, NLI model, knowledge-base contents, Google Scholar access method, retrieval-trigger threshold, reliability-score weights, paraphrase procedure, or released data or code. A single older generator family also says little about current models.

What remains supported: a materials-science question set with reference answers, paraphrase groups, and LLaMA-2 responses is described, although not released in any way the text documents. Composing sampling consistency, entropy, and retrieval-gated NLI into one pipeline with a "requires review" band is a reasonable, conventional design. Table 2 shows that paraphrases of the same question can receive labels spanning Low to High under this labeling, though how often that happens is not reported. The authors' own caveats, that similarity metrics are not factual verification and that precision and recall are moderate, are accurate and arguably understate the gap.

## Relevance to us

This is materials science, not physics, but it is a clear instance of the verifier wall. The authors put intrinsic signals first because there is no comprehensive database to retrieve against, and physics has the same problem. Their answer is the complete-but-biased family in our verification framing: same-model self-consistency, entropy, embedding similarity, and an NLI judge, with no deterministic domain check and no validated labels. So it adds little to B12 beyond a negative reference point. It shows what a verification stack looks like when every stage is learned or statistical, and how hard it then is to show that it works.

Two small things transfer. The three-band output, with a neutral NLI class and a "requires review" middle band, matches our rule of flagging rather than issuing a verdict, although the thresholds here are uncalibrated. Label dispersion across paraphrases of one question is a cheap robustness probe we could run on physics QA or verifier outputs, provided the labels underneath come from an independent check. The paper's main lesson for us is methodological. A verifier has to be scored against labels it did not produce, which reviews/2602.11908.md did and this paper does not.
