Citation: Wang, Y., Sheng, R., Shao, E., Qian, Y., Li, H., Cao, N., Wang, D. Figures as Interfaces: Toward LLM-Native Artifacts for Scientific Discovery. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08491v2

## What this paper is about

Scientific figures are usually dead-end images: once rendered, a human or an AI can only re-read them from pixels or captions, with no way back to the data or code that produced them. This paper proposes making figures "LLM-native" instead — each figure carries its own provenance (the data subset, the analysis code, and the visualization spec) so an LLM agent can trace a chart back to its source, extend the analysis, or regenerate linked views on request. The authors build a prototype, Nexus, for the "science of science" (SciSci) domain — studying research and innovation activity itself — and test it with a case study and a computational benchmark of the system's core mechanism.

## Extended summary

The central object is the "LLM-native figure": a tuple of rendered visualization, data subset, generating code, and metadata, connected by a bidirectional mapping between visual interactions (brushing, clicking) and analytical operations (SQL/Python/Vega-Lite). A "data-driven artifact" chains multiple linked figures together with their coordination rules, forming a version-controlled, revisitable exploration trail rather than a single static output.

Nexus implements this with a hybrid language + GUI front end and a three-agent backend (Planner, Executor, Evaluator) built on LangChain/LangGraph with Claude 4.5 Sonnet as the primary model, plus a SciSci relational database (built from Microsoft Academic Graph, PatentsView, Reliance on Science, and proprietary university tech-transfer and HR data), a RAG literature store (SciSciCorpus), and an exploration-history database. The Planner classifies and decomposes queries and does tree-based action planning; the Executor generates and runs SQL/Python/Vega-Lite in sandboxes; the Evaluator self-scores whether a result answers the query and either terminates or routes back for replanning.

Two evaluations are reported. (1) A qualitative case study on a real university innovation dataset, where a researcher iteratively explores inventor productivity, log-transforms axes, drills into high-disclosure faculty by department, and compares senior vs. junior faculty whose papers are cited by patents but who have no disclosures of their own — surfacing an observation that junior faculty disproportionately populate this "untapped potential" group. (2) A computational evaluation of bidirectional mapping fidelity using 308 LLM-generated paired test cases (initial question + follow-up visual interaction) spanning chart types, interaction types, and two complexity tiers. Reported metrics: Execution Success Rate, Conditional Accuracy (given success), and End-to-End Accuracy. Forward mapping (query → figure) reaches 96.7% execution success and 92.7% end-to-end accuracy; backward mapping (visual interaction → SQL/data) reaches 79.8% end-to-end accuracy for follow-up questions and 91.0% for inter-figure coordination. Correctness of generated code, retrieved data, and figures was assessed "through manual inspection by two authors and cross-validation with the generated LLM-based solution." The authors explicitly scope this as functional/computational validation, not a human-subject usability study, and list as limitations: LLM-generated code/interpretation errors, reliance on structured data and declarative visualization grammars (not images or molecular structures), and no test in confirmatory (as opposed to exploratory) scientific workflows. Data and code are stated as "will be made freely available" but were not released artifacts at the time of this version. Funding: NSF award 2404035 (D.W.). No competing interests declared.

## Learnings

The reusable mechanism is representing a figure as a first-class four-part object (visualization, code, data, metadata) with an explicit bidirectional mapping layer, rather than bolting provenance onto a chart as an afterthought. The bidirectional-mapping framing — visual interaction resolves to a data query and analytical action, and an analytical action resolves to a visualization — is a clean, transferable way to describe what "chatting with a chart" actually requires under the hood, independent of the SciSci case study used to demonstrate it.

The gap between the two mapping directions (92.7% forward vs. 79.8% backward end-to-end accuracy) is itself a useful, generalizable observation: translating discrete GUI gestures (a brush over a continuous axis range) into correct query semantics (BETWEEN vs. IN) is a harder, more error-prone problem than generating a chart from a well-formed natural-language question. Any future system built on gesture-to-query translation should expect this asymmetry rather than assuming both directions are equally tractable.

## Verification

The paper's own claims about the system's core mechanism (bidirectional mapping fidelity) are checked computationally: 308 test cases generated by an LLM, diversified across chart type, interaction type, and complexity tier, with generated SQL/Python/Vega-Lite and retrieved data subsets checked by "manual inspection by two authors and cross-validation with the generated LLM-based solution." Coverage is moderate (308 cases across a defined but self-authored interaction taxonomy) and precision at the level of exact accuracy percentages is reported, though no confidence intervals or inter-rater agreement between the two author-judges are given. The qualitative case study is illustrative, not independently verified — its substantive finding (junior faculty populate the untapped-innovation-potential group at roughly double the rate of senior faculty) is reported as an observation from a single exploration session on one institution's data, not validated against ground truth or a second dataset.

## Field context

This sits within the "LLM agents for scientific workflows" line surveyed broadly in [2605.18661.md](2605.18661.md) (AI for Auto-Research), but occupies a specific, less-covered niche within it: rather than automating hypothesis generation or full research pipelines end-to-end, it targets the artifact layer — making the figure itself the interactive, auditable unit of exchange between human and model. No other paper reviewed in this corpus to date addresses visualization-as-interface or figure-level provenance directly, so this is a novel thread rather than an extension of prior reviewed work. The authors position their own contribution against static computational-reproducibility efforts (executable papers, notebooks) and against existing chat-canvas/malleable-UI systems (ChatGPT Canvas, Claude Artifacts), arguing those either capture the final published state or support single-round generation but not iterative, provenance-linked data exploration; that framing is the paper's own claim and is not independently checked here.

## Critical discussion

The forward-mapping evaluation (natural-language question to figure) is reasonably convincing: 96.7%/92.7% over 308 diversified test cases is a real, moderately-sized sample with an interpretable failure mode (SQL fuzzy-matching and entity misidentification). The backward-mapping result is more equivocal — 79.8% end-to-end accuracy on follow-up questions means roughly one in five gesture-to-query translations is wrong, which matters a great deal for a system whose central pitch is that visual interaction reliably "traces back" to correct data and code; the paper reports this honestly but does not go further to characterize which interaction types or complexity tiers drive the failures beyond one illustrative BETWEEN-vs-IN example.

The evaluation has a circularity concern: the 308 test cases were themselves generated by an LLM, and correctness was judged partly through "cross-validation with the generated LLM-based solution" — i.e., an LLM-authored ground truth checked in part against an LLM-generated answer, with two of the same paper's authors as the human check. This is not disclosed as a limitation, and no independent (non-author, non-LLM-in-the-loop) adjudication is reported. The case study is also narrow in a way the paper's "domain-agnostic" framing understates: it uses one institution's proprietary dataset, one researcher's exploration session, and reports a striking demographic finding (junior vs. senior faculty disclosure gap) without any statistical test, confidence interval, or robustness check — appropriately, the paper frames this as illustrating the tool rather than as a validated scientific finding, but a reader skimming the abstract could easily mistake the framing. Finally, the claim that "the principles underlying LLM-native figures are domain-agnostic" is asserted in the Discussion but tested only within one domain (SciSci) with one backend model (Claude 4.5 Sonnet); the paper is candid that this generalization is future work rather than a demonstrated result. What remains solidly supported: the architectural proposal (structured, bidirectionally-mapped figure representation) is implemented and functions with high fidelity in the easier (generation) direction, with a clearly quantified and honestly reported degradation in the harder (interaction-to-query) direction.
