Citation: Madsen, A., Chandar, S., Reddy, S. Are self-explanations from Large Language Models faithful? ACL 2024. arXiv:2401.07927.

## What this paper is about

Instruction-tuned language models will readily explain their own reasoning when asked, but a convincing explanation is not the same thing as an accurate one, and a wrong-but-persuasive self-explanation can make a model seem more trustworthy than it actually is. This paper asks whether such self-explanations are faithful, meaning whether they actually reflect what drove the model's real prediction, for three specific kinds of explanation (counterfactual edits, feature-importance word lists, and word redaction) that can each be checked without needing access to model internals or confidence scores: the trick is to have the model generate the explanation in one conversation, then re-run the model in a fresh conversation on the modified input the explanation implies, and see whether the model's own behavior actually changes the way the explanation promised. Testing five open-weight models across four classification tasks, the paper finds faithfulness is inconsistent in a specific, checkable way: which explanation type is trustworthy depends on which model and which task, with no explanation type reliably faithful across the board, leading the authors to conclude LLM self-explanations should not be trusted by default.

## Extended summary

The paper's core methodological move is the "self-consistency check," illustrated with a hiring-recommendation example: if a model says "no" to hiring a candidate and, when asked to make a minimal resume edit that would flip its answer to "yes," produces an edited resume that the model itself (in a fresh, separate chat session, to avoid the model simply parroting its own prior turn) does classify as "yes," the counterfactual explanation is deemed faithful; if re-classifying the edited resume still yields "no," the counterfactual failed the model's own stated claim about what mattered. The same logic extends to feature-attribution explanations (a list of words the model claims are necessary for its prediction, checked by redacting exactly those words and confirming the model can no longer make the prediction, i.e., must answer "unknown") and to redaction explanations (where the model itself performs the redaction rather than an external algorithm choosing which words to mask, checked the same way). All three explanation types share a key property that makes this approach practical for API-only, black-box models: they only require the model's own eventual behavior (a discrete label, or a stated inability to decide), never internal confidence scores or logits, which many deployed instruction-tuned models do not expose.

Experiments cover five models (Llama2-7B and -70B, Falcon-7B and -40B, Mistral-7B v0.1) across four classification tasks chosen for structural diversity: sentiment classification (IMDB), two multi-choice QA tasks (bAbI-1, MCTest), and a two-paragraph natural-language-inference task (RTE). Prompt templates are carefully engineered to support an explicit "unknown" class (so a model whose critical information has been redacted can honestly decline to guess, rather than being forced into a false discrete answer) and to test robustness across six prompt-pattern variations (explicit vs. implicit counterfactual targets, "[REDACTED]" vs. "[REMOVED]" tokens, and an objective vs. "you" vs. "a human" persona framing, inspired by prior persona-prompting work). Classification accuracy is measured first as a prerequisite check (an unfaithful-looking counterfactual is meaningless if the underlying classification itself is unreliable), and the paper explicitly discards any example the model classifies incorrectly before evaluating faithfulness on the remainder, since a faithfulness check performed on a wrong classification could look misleadingly faithful by accident. Accuracy is found to vary substantially by model and task but to be largely unaffected by persona or redaction-token wording, which the authors treat as a desirable robustness property letting them focus subsequent analysis on a single "default" prompt configuration without worrying that wording artifacts are driving the results. Notably, all reported results come from a single inference sample per example at a fixed random seed, a deliberate cost-saving choice given the expense of running inference across five models, four tasks, and six prompt variants.

The central faithfulness results show a clear, specific pattern of inconsistency rather than uniform failure or uniform success. For counterfactual explanations, Llama2 and Mistral are consistently faithful only on IMDB sentiment classification, and Falcon is never consistently faithful on any task tested. For feature-attribution explanations, Llama2-70B is faithful specifically on RTE and bAbI-1 but not IMDB or MCTest, while smaller models (Llama2-7B, Falcon-7B) perform poorly across the board, with the striking exception that Mistral-7B is unexpectedly strong specifically at feature attribution for sentiment classification, substantially outperforming every larger model tested on that combination. Redaction explanations track feature-attribution results closely for a given model and task, which the authors use as an internal validity check: since redaction is generated by the model itself (making the masked-out input in-distribution for that model) while feature-attribution's masking is applied algorithmically after the fact, the two methods producing similar faithfulness patterns is evidence that the redaction token itself is not an artificial confound distorting the feature-attribution results. Model scale interacts unevenly with faithfulness: results generally improve with size, but Falcon is a specific, named exception, with Falcon-40B performing worse than Falcon-7B on both the bAbI-1 counterfactual task and (separately) the redaction task on the same dataset, a pattern the authors state they cannot explain given the limited public documentation available for that model family. Prompt-pattern variation, meanwhile, has little effect on Llama2-70B's faithfulness scores across five of the six variations tested (the one exception being implicit vs. explicit counterfactual targets specifically on the two multi-choice datasets, where an implicit target is expected to be ambiguous since multiple "opposite" answers can exist), which the authors treat as a positive robustness finding: whatever faithfulness a model does or does not have is not merely an artifact of how the explanation request happened to be phrased.

The Related Work section carefully distinguishes this paper's contribution from adjacent lines of work: prior self-consistency-based feature-attribution faithfulness checks used a separate explanation algorithm and continuous logit scores rather than the model's own discrete self-explanations (Samek et al. 2017; Hooker et al. 2019); a directly comparable prior attempt to apply self-consistency to ChatGPT's self-explained feature importance (Huang et al. 2023) found the approach ineffective specifically because ChatGPT's self-reported confidence and importance scores were uncalibrated and it frequently refused to classify with missing information, a problem this paper's design (discrete labels plus an explicit "unknown" option, rather than requesting numeric confidence scores) is built to sidestep; and Lanham et al. (2023)'s chain-of-thought self-consistency work on mathematical reasoning is noted as reaching a similarly model-and-task-dependent conclusion via a different mechanism (injecting false facts into a CoT and checking whether the final answer follows), though the authors flag that approach's own validity concern (injected false facts may look like typos rather than genuinely altering the model's reasoning path).

The Limitations section is unusually extensive and candid: the paper cannot show a model is fundamentally incapable of faithful self-explanation, only that a given prompt template fails to elicit one; it evaluates only binary faithful/unfaithful outcomes per observation rather than a graded faithfulness measure the authors acknowledge would be more informative (following Jacovi and Goldberg 2020's call for nuance), though they note the aggregate faithfulness ratio across many observations does provide a form of graded summary at the model level; it fundamentally assumes prompts are correctly understood by the model, which cannot be fully verified; it covers only three explanation types (the only ones current self-consistency methodology can evaluate at all, and feature attribution and redaction are explicitly noted to be quite similar to each other, limiting the paper's actual diversity of explanation forms); and, notably, the paper explicitly declines to test ChatGPT or GPT-4, citing both a practical reproducibility problem (these models are frequently updated with no fixed, citable version) and a stated concern about personal financial liability under those models' license indemnity clauses were the paper to demonstrate unfaithfulness in a way that could be read as damaging to the vendor.

## Learnings

The paper's most broadly transferable methodological contribution is a specific recipe for measuring faithfulness using only a model's own discrete behavior, generate the explanation in one session, act on what the explanation implies, then re-query the model in a completely fresh session and check whether its behavior matches the explanation's own stated claim, which requires no access to logits, confidence scores, or model internals. This is directly reusable for evaluating any deployed, API-only chat model, a real practical constraint the paper explicitly designed around (contrasting with prior faithfulness metrics that assumed access to confidence scores and were shown, in a directly cited prior attempt, to fail when applied to a model like ChatGPT that gives uncalibrated confidence estimates).

A second, more surprising and specific learning is that faithfulness does not track model capability or scale in a simple, monotonic way: the paper's single clearest counterexample is Mistral-7B dramatically outperforming every larger model, including Llama2-70B, at faithful feature attribution for sentiment classification specifically, while otherwise unremarkable on other tasks. This is a concrete, named data point against assuming that a more capable or larger model will automatically also be a more faithfully self-explaining one; faithfulness appears to be a genuinely separate axis from general task competence that has to be measured directly rather than inferred from capability benchmarks.

A third, more design-oriented learning concerns evaluation robustness itself: rather than reporting faithfulness under a single prompt phrasing, the paper deliberately tests six prompt-pattern variations (persona framing, redaction-token wording, explicit versus implicit targets) specifically to check whether an observed faithfulness result is a property of the model's behavior or an artifact of one particular way of phrasing the request, and explicitly treats even a single non-faithful prompt variation as a significant finding (rather than averaging it away), since an ordinary end user is unlikely to phrase a request in exactly the one way that happens to elicit faithful behavior. This asymmetric standard, one bad variation counts as a real problem even if five others look fine, is a specific and exportable design principle for any evaluation where a system will face uncontrolled, naturally-varied real-world phrasing rather than a single canonical prompt.

## Verification

Verification is the entire subject of this paper, and its own verification standard is carefully matched to the practical constraints it identifies: because ground-truth faithfulness is fundamentally inaccessible (there is no independent oracle for what a model "really" used to make a prediction), the paper is explicit that a self-consistency check can only produce informative positive results (a passed check demonstrates faithfulness for that observation) while a failed check is ambiguous (it could mean the model cannot generate a faithful explanation for that input under any circumstance, or simply that this particular prompt or sample failed to elicit one), and the paper states this asymmetry plainly rather than treating negative and positive results as equally conclusive. The requirement that self-consistency re-evaluation happen in a fresh chat session, rather than continuing the same conversation, is a specific and necessary methodological safeguard against a plausible confound (the model complying with its own prior conversational turn rather than genuinely re-deriving its answer from the modified input), and the paper states this explicitly as a design requirement rather than leaving it implicit. The redaction-versus-feature-attribution result comparison functions as an internal validity check on a specific alternative explanation (that masked tokens create out-of-distribution artifacts that could make feature-attribution results look unfaithful for reasons unrelated to genuine faithfulness), and the close correspondence between the two methods' results is used as direct evidence against that alternative explanation rather than merely asserted. One place the paper's own verification standard is thinner than elsewhere: all results rest on a single inference sample per example at one fixed seed, explicitly for cost reasons, so the paper cannot itself distinguish a genuinely stable faithfulness ratio from one that would shift meaningfully under repeated sampling at the same model, task, and prompt configuration, a gap the paper discloses implicitly (in its experimental-setup description) rather than naming directly as a limitation in its dedicated Limitations section.

## Field context

This paper sits in the interpretability-faithfulness literature founded on Jacovi and Goldberg's (2020) definitional distinction between plausibility (how convincing an explanation seems to a human) and faithfulness (whether it accurately reflects the model's actual reasoning), and it extends a pre-existing self-consistency-check tradition (Samek et al. 2017; Hooker et al. 2019; Ribeiro et al. 2016) from externally-generated feature-attribution explanations with continuous logit scores to LLM-generated, discrete self-explanations across three distinct explanation forms, a genre this paper's introduction frames as fundamentally new because the same weights now generate both the prediction and its justification. It directly engages and improves on the one closely comparable prior attempt (Huang et al. 2023's ChatGPT self-consistency check), explicitly diagnosing why that attempt failed (uncalibrated confidence scores, refusal to classify under missing information) and designing around exactly that failure mode via discrete labels and an explicit "unknown" class. It also draws a direct parallel to, while noting a validity concern with, Lanham et al.'s (2023) chain-of-thought self-consistency work on mathematical reasoning, converging independently on the same broad conclusion (faithfulness is model- and task-dependent) via a different mechanism (false-fact injection into intermediate reasoning steps rather than counterfactual input editing). Within this corpus, the paper's central question, whether a model's own account of its internal reasoning process can be trusted at face value, is a direct methodological sibling to [26nla] (the Natural Language Autoencoder, reviewed in an earlier batch), which pursues the same underlying concern (verbalized self-reports of internal state are not automatically accurate) through a structurally different route: this paper checks faithfulness behaviorally, via self-consistency of the model's own discrete outputs under controlled input edits, with no need to inspect internal activations, while 26nla trains a separate model to translate a target model's raw internal activation vectors into natural-language descriptions and validates those descriptions via activation-reconstruction accuracy rather than behavioral consistency. Read together, the two papers offer converging caution from two independent evidentiary routes (external behavioral testing versus internal-activation-grounded translation) that a language model's own verbal account of what it is doing internally, whether framed as a self-explanation of a classification decision or as an activation description, requires independent verification and should not be accepted on the model's own say-so.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central empirical claim, that self-explanation faithfulness is model-, explanation-, and task-dependent rather than uniformly reliable or unreliable, is well-supported by a genuinely comprehensive experimental design: five models spanning three families and two orders of magnitude in scale, four structurally distinct tasks, three explanation types, and six prompt-pattern variations, with the specific, named counterexamples (Mistral's surprising strength at sentiment feature attribution; Falcon's scale-inverted performance) each reported plainly rather than smoothed into an aggregate summary that would obscure exactly the heterogeneity the paper's thesis depends on. The paper's own Limitations section is also unusually thorough and precise, correctly distinguishing what a failed self-consistency check can and cannot establish, rather than overclaiming that a non-faithful result proves a model incapable of faithful explanation in principle.

The clearest place to apply independent caution is the single-sample, fixed-seed experimental design: because every reported classification accuracy and faithfulness ratio comes from exactly one generation per example, the paper's own specific numerical comparisons, for instance, the claim that Falcon-40B underperforms Falcon-7B on particular task-explanation combinations, cannot currently be distinguished from single-sample noise using anything reported in the paper itself, even though the paper is otherwise careful to test six prompt-pattern variations specifically to guard against a different kind of instability (sensitivity to phrasing). This is an explicit, disclosed cost tradeoff rather than a hidden flaw, but it does mean the paper's more surprising specific findings (the Falcon scale-inversion, in particular) should be read as suggestive rather than fully confirmed until independently replicated with repeated sampling. A second, related point concerns the scope of the paper's central conclusion relative to what it tests: the abstract's claim that "self-explanations should not be trusted in general" is drawn from only three explanation types, the only ones current self-consistency methodology can evaluate at all, and the paper's own Limitations section acknowledges these three are "not a great variety," with feature attribution and redaction in particular being closely related variants of the same underlying idea rather than genuinely distinct explanation forms; the inferential leap from "these three checkable explanation types are inconsistently faithful" to "self-explanations in general, including the much larger space of free-form explanations that cannot be checked this way, should not be trusted" is a reasonable and explicitly-flagged extrapolation rather than a directly tested claim, and readers building on this paper's conclusion for free-form explanations specifically should keep that distinction in mind. The paper's explicit decision to exclude ChatGPT and GPT-4, while defensible on the stated reproducibility grounds (no fixed, citable model version), is also a genuine scope limitation for anyone hoping to generalize these findings to the most widely deployed commercial chat systems, which the paper itself acknowledges directly rather than glossing over.

What remains well supported: self-explanation faithfulness for counterfactual, feature-attribution, and redaction explanations is measurably and substantially inconsistent across the specific combinations of model, explanation type, and task tested, with no single explanation type reliably faithful across every model or task; this inconsistency is not an artifact of prompt phrasing, since faithfulness results are shown to be robust to persona and wording variations for the model tested in detail; and redaction and feature-attribution explanations' close correspondence in results provides genuine internal evidence that masking-induced distributional shift is not driving the feature-attribution findings.
