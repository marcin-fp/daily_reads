Citation: Abram, M. Toward Evaluation Frameworks for Multi-Agent Scientific AI Systems. arXiv preprint arXiv:2603.26718, 2026.

## What this paper is about

This is a position and framework paper on a practical problem: how do you evaluate an AI system meant to assist with physics research, once that system is more than a single model answering a single question — once it can search the live internet, call simulators and computer-algebra tools, and iterate over multiple turns? The paper argues that existing physics and science benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA, PhysReason, and similar exam-style tests) mostly measure knowledge recall and single-shot procedural computation, which is not the same as the parts of scientific practice that matter most: generating hypotheses, catching mistakes, synthesizing across sources, and knowing when a result is meaningful. It further argues that once a system can retrieve from an ever-updating literature, the evaluation problem gets structurally harder, not easier, because it becomes difficult to tell whether a correct answer reflects genuine reasoning or successful retrieval, and because contamination can enter through multiple channels beyond a model's training cutoff. The paper proposes a taxonomy of benchmark types and construction strategies designed to resist these specific failure modes, grounds the discussion in a small set of interviews with practicing quantum scientists and engineers about what they actually want from such a system, and runs a preliminary feasibility pipeline that mines recent arXiv papers for stated future-research directions, as an initial test of one of the proposed benchmark families.

## Extended summary

The related-work section surveys the current landscape in three tiers. First-generation closed-form benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-Pro, GPQA/GPQA-Diamond, UGPhysics, PhysReason, SciBench, OlympiadBench) are described as approaching saturation (frontier models now exceed 90% on GPQA-Diamond in some evaluations) while sharing a structural limitation: their single-shot, closed-form format privileges recall and procedure, encourages guessing under uncertainty, and does not distinguish retrieval from reasoning. A second, newer generation (CURIE for long-context scientific understanding across full papers; FrontierScience for rubric-decomposed PhD-level open problems; CritPt for frontier-physics problems sourced from unpublished expert work specifically to dodge retrieval contamination, credited as reflecting current best practice but noted as costly, individually biased, and opaquely graded; ResearcherBench for autonomous web-research report generation) is treated as addressing some, but not all, of these gaps. Paper-replication benchmarks (PaperBench for ML research, where the best tested agent reached 21.0% against a 41.4% human-PhD baseline; ReplicationBench applying the same idea to astrophysics, with frontier models scoring below 20%; the commercial Lemma product for automatic verification of mathematical claims extracted from papers) are discussed as establishing a rubric-graded, agentic-scaffold methodology. The formal-mathematics/Lean-based theorem-proving line (AlphaProof, Aristotle, DeepSeek-Prover reaching IMO gold-medal level; the miniF2F benchmark; the APOLLO framework combining LLM generation with symbolic repair) is explicitly distinguished as a structurally different verification regime: closed, axiomatic systems where every step is mechanically checkable, versus theoretical physics, which requires connecting formal derivation to physical intuition, choice of regime, and experimental relevance in ways a proof checker cannot adjudicate. End-to-end autonomous-discovery systems (AI Scientist and AI Scientist-v2, the latter producing a workshop paper that passed peer review) and industry initiatives (Google DeepMind's AI Co-Scientist, OpenAI for Science, Anthropic's Claude for Life Sciences) round out the survey.

The core argument (Section III) is that benchmarking agentic scientific systems is harder than benchmarking a single model along several specific, structural axes: there are many interacting components to isolate (tool selection, individual tool accuracy, comprehension, synthesis, orchestration); a system with live retrieval creates a retrieval-reasoning confound (a problem already solved in the literature mostly tests retrieval; a genuinely unsolved one has no ground truth to check against) and a temporal confound (comparing scores across different time windows conflates system improvement with drift in what has newly been published); contamination must be controlled across multiple channels simultaneously (training-data contamination and retrieval-time contamination both need addressing, and information about a specific result can leak through secondary sources like forum posts or talk transcripts even when the primary paper itself is excluded); and scoring open-ended scientific output cannot be reduced to one number, since correctness, physical plausibility, clarity, novelty, specificity, and acknowledged limitations are separate, only partially correlated dimensions. Section IV states a design principle that follows from this: results should be automatically checkable wherever possible, worked out concretely by answer type (comparing against known values for numerical results, checking step-level validity via proof tooling for analytical derivations, and verifying that a constructed object such as a stabilizer code or counterexample actually has its claimed properties).

Section V lays out a taxonomy of four benchmark families, each with worked examples and an explicit strengths/weaknesses discussion. Replication Benchmarks exploit the gap between what a paper states and every step actually needed to reproduce it, scaling to thousands of existing papers and doubling as a strong integration test across literature search, comprehension, symbolic manipulation, and simulation; the paper candidly flags that source papers can themselves contain errors (unfairly penalizing a system that correctly deviates), that a system could fake a plausible-looking derivation chain without genuine derivation, and that matching a final numerical value does not verify that every intermediate step was valid. Error Detection Benchmarks plant errors (simple, like a sign flip, or subtle, generated by a second agent that shifts assumptions) and ask the system to find them; the paper draws a precise distinction between two different tasks that get conflated under this label — "find the error I planted" (a search problem with a guaranteed target, easy to score automatically) versus "review this for correctness" (a calibration/judgment problem where the correct answer might be "no error," much harder to score and much closer to what a real reviewer does) — and notes that crude injected errors (a sign flip) can be caught by pure algebraic consistency-checking rather than physical understanding, proposing physically-plausible-but-algebraically-consistent errors (e.g., using the wrong dispersion relation) as a partial fix. Scientific Reasoning Benchmarks cover four sub-types: Future Research Directions (mask a paper's stated future-work section and score a system's proposed directions against it via semantic overlap, acknowledging the ground truth itself is incomplete and strategic, and proposing delayed verification via later follow-up papers that actually pursue a proposed direction); Minimal Counterexample Construction (present a false or boundary-case conjecture, ideally freshly engineered or obscured rather than textbook-famous, and ask for the simplest counterexample); Technique Recommendation (ask which theoretical or numerical method fits a given problem, checked against methods actually used in the corresponding published work). Discovery-Style Benchmarks cover Fabricated Phenomena (construct a self-consistent theoretical explanation for a plausible but made-up physical phenomenon, modeled explicitly on the real LK-99 and faster-than-light-neutrino episodes, with the acknowledged central weakness that verifying self-consistency may require a verifier as capable as the generator, risking shared blind spots and rewarded confabulation) and Solving Novel Problems (attempt genuinely open problems, such as tightening a known bound or one of the field's listed open problems, explicitly acknowledged as unable to distinguish "the system failed" from "no tighter result exists," but framed as a strong one-shot existence-proof test on success).

Section VI proposes five construction strategies for making these benchmark families resistant to contamination and gaming: Planted-Structure Problems (engineer the problem so the answer is known by construction, e.g., stabilizer-code discovery under hardware-realistic constraints, or phase-boundary identification after obfuscating a known model's representation); Constraint-Modified Problems (introduce realistic but non-standard constraints, illustrated with real IBM-hardware qubit-connectivity and per-gate error-rate maps, that are unlikely to appear verbatim in existing literature); Dynamic Difficulty Families (vary a continuous parameter — qubit count, circuit depth, Hilbert-space dimension, bond dimension, numerical precision — to obtain a scaling curve rather than a single accuracy number, citing a prior complexity-scaling methodology as a model); Self-Consistency Tests (solve the same problem via two structurally independent methods, e.g., Monte Carlo versus variational estimation, or primal versus dual semidefinite-programming formulations, with the explicit caveat that agreement between methods is necessary but not sufficient for correctness, since the same conceptual error could affect both); and Multi-Turn Scientific Dialogue (reveal information progressively and score whether the system asks the right clarifying questions rather than only answering what it is given, illustrated with a worked example escalating from a generic bipartite system to a two-qubit Bell-inequality scenario). Section VII proposes a multi-level system evaluation protocol spanning ablation studies (disable specific subsystems to see how much score depends on retrieval versus reasoning), tool-use analysis (check via the reasoning trace whether retrieval versus first-principles derivation was chosen appropriately), process-level evaluation (grade the reasoning trace, not only the final answer), scaling curves, point tests, integration tests, red-teaming/adversarial robustness (near-duplicate-but-critically-different questions designed to expose "mode collapse" toward a more familiar problem), and expert-sourced problems.

Section VIII reports open-ended interviews with four professionals in quantum science and engineering (an academic researcher with a habilitation at a Polish research institute; a national-lab research scientist; a startup quantum-software engineer; an industry research scientist), covering workplace context, which tasks are considered essential versus delegatable, current AI use, and desired AI role, with selected verbatim quotes given for each. The cross-interview analysis identifies a consistent theme — all four want AI framed as a partner ("fellow scientist," "sparring partner") rather than an autonomous replacement, with decision-making, interpretation, and validation explicitly reserved for the human, and trust/critical pushback valued more than raw problem-solving, illustrated with a small sentiment scatter plot rating each interview along "critical thinking ability" and "problem-solving ability." A role-based split is noted: academic researchers frame their needs around the scientific workflow and want a peer-level interlocutor, while industry engineers frame theirs around implementation and want a reliable junior collaborator that asks before acting. The interviews are then translated into concrete gaps in the paper's own proposed benchmark taxonomy: existing framings mostly test complete-solution delivery rather than hint-giving (the engineer's stated preference); test finding errors in a paper rather than pushing back on a user's own idea; test single-paper replication rather than the multi-source synthesis one engineer described spending a year on; and motivate adding a dedicated uncertainty-calibration benchmark, given how central trust was across all four interviews.

Section IX runs a small feasibility pipeline for the Future Research Directions benchmark family: collect quant-ph arXiv papers, filter by abstract for topical relevance, use a keyword heuristic to pre-select likely future-work sections, use Claude Sonnet 4.6 to extract explicit future-direction claims, then use the same model to reformulate each extracted claim with added context. Run on 300 papers from a two-week window, this extracted 414 future-direction statements; three worked examples are shown verbatim, one explicitly flagged by the authors as a partial failure (the pipeline failed to recognize that a single quoted sentence bundled two distinct extensions). The extracted directions are clustered into 13 labeled thematic categories using a single top-1 category match per claim (multi-category assignment is explicitly noted as untested), with "Extension to new systems/settings" (21%), "Optimization/performance improvement" (19%), and "Theoretical formalization/proofs" (17%) as the largest categories. The paper frames this explicitly as a demonstration that such a dynamic database of research-direction concepts is feasible to build, listing a verification loop, multi-category alignment, and filtering of already-pursued directions as remaining work.

## Learnings

The distinction between "find the error I planted" (a search problem with a guaranteed target, easy to score automatically) and "review this for correctness" (a calibration/judgment problem where the correct answer might be that nothing is wrong, much harder to score and much closer to what a research assistant or reviewer actually does) reframes "error detection" as at least two genuinely different skills that get conflated under one label, and identifies the harder, less-guaranteed-target version as the one closer to real research practice — a distinction worth applying whenever any benchmark is described simply as testing "error detection" or "critique."

Self-consistency across two structurally independent solution methods (e.g., Monte Carlo versus variational estimation, or primal versus dual formulations) is explicitly identified as necessary but not sufficient evidence of correctness for a novel problem with no independent ground truth, since a system can be consistently wrong by making the same conceptual error under both methods — a specific, well-stated caution against treating cross-method agreement as a validity guarantee rather than a weak, defeasible signal that still requires an independent check.

An ablation-based diagnostic for a benchmark itself — disabling retrieval or specific tools and observing how much the score drops — is proposed as a cheap way to learn what a benchmark is actually measuring before trusting a single headline accuracy number: if the score barely changes without retrieval, the benchmark was mostly testing retrieval all along regardless of its intended framing, and if it collapses, retrieval genuinely was not carrying the result. This is useful not only for designing new benchmarks but as a general auditing tool for any existing benchmark that reports one accuracy number without this kind of decomposition.

## Verification

This paper is itself substantially about verification as a subject (what makes a scientific-AI benchmark result trustworthy), rather than reporting one system's verification pipeline against a fixed benchmark, so its most relevant content here is its explicit, type-specific breakdown of what "automatically checkable" should mean: numerical comparison for numerical results, step-validity checking via proof tooling for analytical derivations, and confirming a constructed object (a stabilizer code, a counterexample, a quantum state) actually satisfies its claimed properties — a more precise decomposition than treating "verifiable" as one uniform property. The paper is also consistently self-critical about the specific ways each proposed verification strategy can fail: numerical-match verification for replication tasks does not catch a correct final answer reached through an invalid intermediate step; naive automated scoring for planted-error detection cannot distinguish a correctly-flagged pre-existing real error from a false positive; and verifying self-consistency for fabricated-phenomena benchmarks may require a verifier as capable as the generator, risking a verifier that shares the generator's own blind spots. Where verification is comparatively thin is in the paper's own primary empirical contribution, the feasibility pipeline: the 414 extracted future-direction claims and their 13-category clustering are not checked against any independent ground truth or human annotation, the one worked "partial failure" example is illustrative rather than part of a systematic error-rate estimate over the full extracted set, and the single-category clustering explicitly was not cross-checked against a multi-category assignment — reasonable caveats for an explicitly preliminary feasibility demo, but worth naming plainly given how forcefully the same paper argues elsewhere for rigorous, type-specific verification in the benchmarks it proposes.

## Field context

The paper organizes a fast-moving subfield into a clear structure rather than simply listing precedents: first-generation closed-form exam-style benchmarks now approaching saturation; a second generation of richer, more open-ended benchmarks (CURIE, FrontierScience, CritPt, ResearcherBench) addressing some but not all of the identified gaps; paper-replication benchmarks (PaperBench, ReplicationBench) establishing a rubric-graded agentic-scaffold methodology; and the formal-mathematics/Lean theorem-proving line (AlphaProof, Aristotle, DeepSeek-Prover, miniF2F, APOLLO), which the paper argues occupies a structurally different, more mechanically verifiable regime than theoretical physics. It engages most directly with CritPt (expert-sourced frontier-physics problems designed specifically to dodge retrieval contamination, credited as reflecting current best practice while flagged as costly, individually biased, and opaquely graded) and with Xuemei Gu and Mario Krenn's prior work on LLM-and-knowledge-graph-driven "interesting scientific idea" generation, which is cited as validating the underlying premise tested here in the feasibility study. None of these specific precedents (GPQA, PhysReason, CURIE, FrontierScience, CritPt, PaperBench, ReplicationBench, AlphaProof) are yet reviewed in this corpus. Notably, this paper's own taxonomy and vocabulary — planted-structure problems, constraint-modified problems, contamination resistance via separating training-data and retrieval-time channels, difficulty-scaling curves, self-consistency as necessary-but-not-sufficient, the retrieval-versus-reasoning ablation diagnostic — closely track, and appear to be a direct source for, the terminology this review corpus's own reference documents already use throughout the paper-review process. This paper should therefore be read less as an external data point to weigh against an existing internal framework, and more as an earlier or parallel statement of that same framework, which makes a close, honest critical read of it directly useful for auditing our own working assumptions.

## Critical discussion

The interview study (Section VIII) is a genuine and unusual strength relative to most benchmark-design papers, which typically skip primary human data altogether, but it rests on four professionals from a single subfield (quantum science and engineering) and a narrow set of institutional contexts (one academic lab, one national research center, two industry roles). The paper is appropriately modest about this, framing the interviews as providing "initial intuition" rather than a representative survey. The specific quantitative claim that "critical thinking" was the most desired quality across all interviews, illustrated with a 2D sentiment scatter plot, is not accompanied by any description of how sentiment was scored or by whom, which makes the figure closer to an illustrative summary of qualitative impressions than an independently reproducible measurement, despite its numerically precise-looking presentation.

The feasibility pipeline (Section IX) is explicitly and appropriately framed as preliminary, but two specific gaps are worth naming. The three worked examples in the main text, one of them a self-reported partial failure, are hand-picked to illustrate the pipeline rather than sampled systematically from the full 414 extracted claims, so a reader cannot tell from the paper how frequently the demonstrated failure mode (bundling two distinct extensions into one extracted claim) actually recurs across the dataset. The 13-category thematic clustering uses only a single top-1 category per claim, with the paper openly noting multi-category assignment was untested; since research directions plausibly span more than one theme (an "extension to new systems" can also be a "theoretical formalization"), the reported category percentages likely understate real overlap and should be read as a first-pass sketch rather than a validated taxonomy.

More broadly, essentially all of the paper's most specific claims about what would or would not work as a benchmark are conceptual predictions accompanied by worked hypothetical examples, not benchmarks that have actually been built, run against a real system, and checked against the failure modes the paper itself anticipates — for instance, the prediction that crude sign-flip error injection would be caught by algebraic consistency-checking alone, with physically-plausible-but-algebraically-consistent errors proposed as a fix, is argued convincingly but not tested against an actual model. This is clearly signaled by the paper itself (the Conclusion explicitly lists open problems it does not resolve) and is an appropriate scope for a position/framework paper, but it does mean the single exception with any empirical grounding is the small future-research-directions pipeline, which is itself preliminary in the ways noted above.

This paper is not merely relevant to our work in the way an external paper might be — its taxonomy and vocabulary appear to be the direct conceptual source for this review corpus's own VERIFICATION.md and TAXONOMY.md reference documents used throughout the paper-review process this session. Read that way, it functions as an earlier or parallel statement of our own working framework rather than a new data point to weigh against it, which makes a close, honest critical read of it directly useful for checking our own assumptions rather than importing someone else's. Two things from this specific paper are worth carrying forward explicitly if not already fully reflected elsewhere: first, the sharp distinction between "find the planted error" (a search problem) and "review this for correctness" (a calibration/judgment problem with no guaranteed target), which is a more precise framing of an error-detection benchmark's actual difficulty than "detect errors" alone conveys; second, the interview-derived, primary-source finding that practicing scientists and engineers rate a system's willingness to exercise critical judgment and push back above its raw problem-solving ability, and specifically distinguish wanting a hint over a complete solution — a concrete data point for calibrating what "helpful" should mean in any physics-assistant system we build or evaluate, beyond accuracy on a benchmark.
