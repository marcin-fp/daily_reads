Citation: Ikeda, W., Yano, K., Takahashi, R., Lee, J., Shibata, K., Suzuki, J. Layerwise Importance Analysis of Feed-Forward Networks in Transformer-based Language Models. Published as a conference paper at COLM 2025. arXiv:2508.17734.

## What this paper is about

Transformer language models are usually built by stacking identical layers, each with the same-sized self-attention and feed-forward network (FFN) block, but prior interpretability work suggests FFNs mainly function as a kind of key-value memory that stores factual knowledge from training. If that is right, a natural question is whether every layer's FFN is equally important for this job, or whether some layer positions are structurally better suited to storing knowledge than others. Rather than only analyzing existing pretrained models, this paper tests the question directly: it trains language models from scratch with deliberately non-standard architectures, removing the FFN entirely from some layers and correspondingly enlarging the FFN in others (while keeping total parameter count fixed), and measures how downstream task performance and factual-knowledge recall change depending on which layers keep their FFN.

## Extended summary

The experimental design uses three types of Transformer layer: standard (the usual LLaMA-style layer), FFN-expanded (same self-attention, but a wider FFN intermediate dimension), and FFN-deactivated (self-attention only, no FFN at all). For a given baseline model, the authors construct "experimental models" that redistribute the FFN parameter budget: a chosen percentage r of layers (r ∈ {10, 30, 50, 70, 90, 100}) become FFN-expanded (with intermediate dimension recalculated so total parameters match the baseline exactly), and the remaining layers become FFN-deactivated, placed either near the input (first), symmetrically around the midpoint (middle), or near the output (final). This yields 18 distinct architecture configurations per baseline model.

Three baseline architectures are trained from scratch on FineWeb-Edu following Chinchilla-optimal token budgets (20x parameter count): 285M parameters/12 layers, 570M/24 layers, and 570M/40 layers (the latter two sharing parameter count but differing in depth, specifically to separate scale effects from depth effects). A fourth, larger 1.2B/40-layer model is trained later to test whether findings persist at greater scale, but only for the three ratio-70% configurations (first, middle, final) rather than the full ratio grid. Models are evaluated on standard downstream benchmarks (LAMBADA, WikiText perplexity, Winogrande, PIQA, HellaSwag, ARC-easy/challenge) via the lm-evaluation-harness, plus a dedicated knowledge-capacity probe using the Zero-Shot Relation Extraction (zsRE) dataset, which measures token-level factual-recall accuracy under teacher forcing. All comparisons are reported as a sign-corrected relative improvement (RI) over the matched baseline, so a positive RI always means better performance regardless of whether the underlying metric is "higher is better" (accuracy) or "lower is better" (perplexity); baseline models are separately validated against Pythia checkpoints trained on matched token counts, showing performance in a comparable or better range, to establish that the baselines themselves are reasonably trained before drawing architecture conclusions from them.

The central finding is that FFN placement ratio matters substantially: concentrating FFN capacity into only 10-30% of layers (with the rest FFN-deactivated) consistently hurts performance relative to the standard uniform baseline, sometimes severely (HellaSwag degrading by up to 6.53%, WikiText perplexity by up to 19.07% relative to baseline), while concentrating FFN capacity into 70-90% of layers tends to outperform the uniform baseline across most tasks and model configurations, particularly for the 285M/12-layer and 1.2B/40-layer models. Within this high-performing 70-90% range, layer position matters too: for the smaller, shallower models (285M/12-layer and 570M/24-layer), placing the FFN-expanded layers in the middle or final portion of the network outperforms placing them near the input, e.g. at 90% ratio on the 285M model, the "first" placement yields only +0.23% HellaSwag improvement versus +1.94% (middle) and +2.04% (final). This position effect becomes noticeably less consistent in the deeper 40-layer model, where the "final" placement sometimes underperforms both "middle" and "first." Aggregating relative improvement across six downstream tasks, the middle-70% configuration (concentrating FFNs in 70% of consecutive layers around the network's center) emerges as the single most consistently strong configuration across all three baseline scales (ranking 3rd of 18 for 285M, 1st for 570M/24-layer, 2nd for 570M/40-layer), and this configuration is then specifically re-tested at 1.2B parameters, where it again produces the highest average relative improvement (+1.29%) among the three positions tested, outperforming both first-70% (+0.69%) and final-70% (+0.52%), with especially large gains on WikiText, LAMBADA, and the zsRE knowledge-probe task.

To further quantify per-layer contributions rather than only per-configuration averages, the authors build a layerwise importance metric: whenever a group of layers is jointly FFN-deactivated in some configuration, the resulting performance degradation is attributed equally across all deactivated layers in that configuration, and each layer's final importance score is the average of these attributions across every configuration in which it was deactivated. This produces a standardized (zero-mean, unit-variance) importance profile per layer for each of the three smaller baseline architectures, consistently showing that the first few and last few layers carry below-average importance while a broad middle band carries above-average importance. Notably, the location of this important band shifts with model depth: for the 12-layer model it sits somewhat past the midpoint, for the 24-layer model it is roughly centered with a slight late-half bias, and for the 40-layer model it shifts earlier than the midpoint, a "progressive forward shift" the authors speculate may reflect deeper networks' middle layers becoming over-contextualized by accumulated self-attention before reaching them, making FFN processing comparatively less effective there than earlier in the stack. Appendix G separately checks whether the middle-70% advantage is an artifact of under-training: repeating the 285M and 1.2B experiments at up to 8x and 4x the original (Chinchilla-optimal) token budget respectively, using a Warmup-Stable-Decay scheduler to make resuming from earlier checkpoints computationally efficient, the middle-70% configuration retains a consistent positive relative improvement over baseline across all tested training-token multiples for the 285M model, and remains positive (though more modest) for the 1.2B model from 2x to 4x Chinchilla tokens.

## Learnings

The paper's central, most transferable finding is a specific, quantified architectural recipe rather than only a qualitative observation: concentrating roughly 70% of a fixed FFN parameter budget into consecutive layers around the network's middle, while removing FFNs entirely from the remaining layers, outperforms the standard uniform-FFN-per-layer convention at equal total parameter count, across three different model scales (285M, 570M, 1.2B) and two different depths at fixed scale (24 versus 40 layers at 570M). This is a directly actionable design lever for anyone training a Transformer LM from scratch under a fixed parameter budget, rather than only a post-hoc interpretability observation about existing models.

A second, more mechanistic learning is the specific U-shaped, direction-dependent pattern in FFN importance by depth position: the first few and last few layers' FFNs are comparatively dispensable (removable with less performance cost, and their function apparently absorbable by FFNs elsewhere), while a broad middle band is comparatively indispensable, and this middle band's exact location shifts earlier in the network as total depth increases. This is a concrete, falsifiable claim (not just "the middle matters") that a future architecture-design or pruning effort could directly test or exploit, e.g. as a prior for where to prioritize FFN capacity or where to safely prune it in deeper models specifically.

A third, more methodological learning worth carrying forward is the paper's own robustness-checking habit: rather than presenting the middle-70% finding as established from a single training run per configuration, the authors separately validate it against an independent literature baseline (Pythia, to rule out their own baselines being poorly trained) and against a substantially extended training-token budget (up to 8x Chinchilla-optimal, to rule out the finding being an under-training artifact), both of which strengthen confidence in the central claim beyond what the headline relative-improvement numbers alone would establish.

## Verification

The paper takes concrete, specific steps to rule out two plausible alternative explanations for its results rather than presenting the headline numbers as self-evidently trustworthy. First, to rule out the concern that the paper's own baseline models might simply be poorly trained (which would make any relative-improvement comparison against them uninformative), Appendix E compares each baseline's absolute performance directly against Pythia checkpoints trained on matched token counts, finding the paper's baselines perform equal to or better than Pythia across nearly all tasks and scales. Second, to rule out the concern that the reported architecture effects might only hold at the specific, comparatively modest (20x Chinchilla-optimal) token budget used for the main experiments, since many production LLMs are substantially over-trained relative to compute-optimal, Appendix G repeats the two most relevant configurations (285M and 1.2B) at up to 8x and 4x that token budget respectively and confirms the middle-70% configuration's advantage persists, with only modest magnitude change. The paper is also careful about a specific evaluation-validity issue: tasks where either the baseline or an experimental model failed to clear chance-level performance (ARC-challenge, Winogrande, for the smaller models) are explicitly excluded from the relevant comparisons rather than included and potentially misinterpreted as a meaningful architecture effect. The layerwise-importance metric itself, however, rests on an explicitly stated simplifying assumption, that performance degradation from jointly deactivating a group of layers' FFNs is spread equally across those layers, which the authors state directly as an assumption rather than something independently verified; since FFN layers likely interact non-additively (the true contribution of any single deactivated layer plausibly depends on which other layers are simultaneously deactivated), this equal-attribution simplification is a real, acknowledged approximation rather than a rigorously isolated per-layer causal estimate, though the resulting importance profiles are broadly consistent with the paper's separately-measured, non-decomposed configuration-level RI results, which offers some independent corroboration.

## Field context

The paper builds directly on, and structurally tests, the widely-cited hypothesis that FFN layers function as key-value memories storing factual knowledge (Geva et al. 2021, Dai et al. 2022, Meng et al. 2022), distinguishing itself from that entire prior line by using an interventional, train-from-scratch architectural manipulation (removing or resizing FFNs during pretraining) rather than analyzing frozen, already-pretrained models with probing or attribution methods; it also explicitly engages with a competing account (Kobayashi et al. 2024) that FFNs, combined with layer normalization, mainly contextualize inputs rather than storing facts, without directly adjudicating between the two accounts but treating knowledge-storage as its working hypothesis to test structurally. Its layer-position findings are also framed against a separate recent observation (Skean et al. 2025) that mid-depth layers in pretrained LMs provide unusually robust general representations while final layers over-specialize to the pretraining objective, offering a complementary, causally-manipulated (rather than only observational) architectural angle on a similar mid-layer-importance theme. Within this corpus, no other reviewed paper directly investigates FFN-level or layer-level architectural interpretability in Transformer LMs, so this paper stands as an isolated but methodologically distinct data point (interventional pretraining-time architecture search, rather than either post-hoc mechanistic interpretability or benchmark-style capability evaluation) relative to the corpus's broader coverage of LLM evaluation, alignment, and reasoning-analysis papers; a natural connection for future synthesis work is to the corpus's knowledge-editing and memorization-adjacent papers, since a layer-position prior for where factual knowledge concentrates is directly relevant to where a model-editing or unlearning intervention should target.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central empirical claim is well supported for the scale range it actually tests: the middle-70% configuration's advantage is demonstrated consistently across three different parameter scales (285M, 570M, 1.2B), two different depths at fixed scale (24 vs. 40 layers), multiple downstream tasks, and is separately shown to survive both a baseline-quality check (against Pythia) and a training-length robustness check (up to 8x more tokens), which is a genuinely thorough set of controls for a paper of this scope. The layerwise-importance visualization additionally converts a set of per-configuration averages into a specific, checkable per-layer profile rather than leaving the position effect only implicit in aggregate table comparisons.

The most significant limitation is scale: the largest model evaluated is 1.2B parameters with 40 layers, roughly three orders of magnitude smaller than the frontier-scale models (tens to hundreds of billions of parameters) that motivate the paper's own opening framing (LLaMA 3's largest variants, Qwen3 14B mentioned specifically for its 40-layer depth). The paper's conclusion states that the middle-layer-concentration advantage "represents a fundamental architectural property of Transformer LMs rather than a scale-dependent phenomenon," but this claim is stronger than the tested evidence range can fully establish on its own: three data points spanning roughly a 4x parameter range (285M to 1.2B) is suggestive of a scale-independent effect, but is a comparatively narrow basis for ruling out scale-dependence altogether, particularly since the paper's own layer-position finding (the "forward shift" of the important band with increasing depth) already shows that at least one aspect of the phenomenon does shift systematically with model depth within the tested range, which sits in some tension with the "not scale-dependent" framing for the ratio-versus-position finding specifically. Relatedly, the 1.2B scale-up experiment tests only the single ratio value (70%) previously identified as best at smaller scale, rather than the full ratio grid, so the paper does not directly verify whether 70% remains the optimal ratio at 1.2B parameters or only that it remains better than the position alternatives at that one ratio; a different ratio might plausibly be optimal at larger scale even if 70% still beats first/final placement at that ratio. A third, smaller point is that the layerwise-importance metric's equal-attribution assumption, acknowledged directly by the authors, means the specific per-layer scores in Figure 5 should be read as a reasonable approximate visualization of the position-level pattern already established by the RI tables, rather than as independently-derived, non-additive per-layer causal effects.

What remains well supported after these caveats: for Transformer LMs in the 285M-1.2B parameter, 12-40 layer range studied here, redistributing a fixed FFN parameter budget so that roughly 70% of consecutive middle-to-later layers retain (enlarged) FFNs while the remaining layers have none reliably outperforms the standard uniform-FFN convention, and FFNs in the very first and very last layers of the network contribute comparatively little to downstream task performance and factual-knowledge recall relative to a broad middle band, a band whose depth-relative position itself shifts earlier as the network gets deeper.
