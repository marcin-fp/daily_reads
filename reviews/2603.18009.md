Citation: Chen, W., Ju, G., Qi, Y. How Confident Is the First Token? An Uncertainty-Calibrated Prompt Optimization Framework for Large Language Model Classification and Understanding. arXiv:2603.18009, 2026.

## What this paper is about

For LLM classification tasks, the standard way to measure "how confident is the model" — entropy over the first predicted token — has a known blind spot: a model can be very confident simply because it's predicting a common, high-frequency label, not because it actually understood the input. This paper proposes a fix, Log-Scale Focal Uncertainty (LSFU), which multiplies ordinary entropy by a focal-loss-style penalty term that shrinks toward zero for common labels and toward one for rare labels — so confidence on a frequent class counts for less, and confidence on a rare class counts for more. Built on top of LSFU, the paper proposes UCPOF, a two-stage inference pipeline: first answer every input with a cheap, LSFU-selected static few-shot prompt; then, only for the subset of inputs where LSFU says the model's confidence looks unreliable, spend extra compute retrieving similar labeled examples and re-asking with a "your first answer may be wrong, here's more context" reflective prompt. The pitch is that this captures most of retrieval-augmented generation's accuracy benefit while triggering it on far fewer inputs than an always-on RAG pipeline would.

## Extended summary

LSFU is defined as log10(H(P_top-k) · (1 − P_prior(y))² + ε), where H(P_top-k) is Shannon entropy computed over the top-K=50 renormalized first-token logits (chosen because K∈{50,100,500,full-vocab} give negligible entropy differences per an appendix check), P_prior(y) is the predicted label's empirical frequency in the training/calibration set, and the squared (1−P_prior)² term is the focal-loss-inspired risk modulator: it collapses toward 0 for high-frequency predicted labels (suppressing "safe" low-entropy-but-uninformative confidence) and toward 1 for low-frequency labels (amplifying the signal for potentially risky long-tail predictions).

LSFU is used in two ways. First, for static "Gold Shot" exemplar selection: from a seed-prompted pass over the training pool, keep only correctly-predicted examples, compute their LSFU, and select the N lowest-LSFU examples (i.e., the ones the model handles with both low raw entropy and good alignment to the label prior) as the few-shot prompt's exemplars — the claim being that these sit near class-cluster centers and provide the cleanest "teaching examples," in contrast to boundary-case exemplars that would increase cognitive load. Second, for dynamic gating in UCPOF: at inference, every test input first gets a prediction under the static gold-shot prompt, and its LSFU score S(x) is compared against a threshold T; if S(x) ≤ T the static prediction is kept as final (no further compute spent), while if S(x) > T the system retrieves the top-K most semantically similar labeled examples from a pre-built vector knowledge base (via all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings) and re-prompts with a structured "reflective" template (informing the model its initial guess may be wrong, providing correctly-labeled similar examples, and instructing it to re-judge) to produce the final corrected label. The gating threshold T is set offline by running the static prompt over a calibration set, looking only at the LSFU distribution of the samples it gets wrong, and picking the LSFU value that covers at least 90% of those errors — justified by an explicit cost-benefit curve (error-coverage vs. RAG-trigger-rate) showing diminishing returns past roughly 85-95% coverage, with 90% argued to sit near the "elbow."

Experiments use six classification/extraction datasets spanning event extraction with pronounced long-tail label distributions (ACE, CASIE), long-tail news/text classification (AgNews, Reuters-21578/R8), fine-grained 5-way sentiment (SST5), and natural language inference (CB), evaluated primarily on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (with Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct as cross-model/scale checks in an appendix), against three baselines: a fixed random-exemplar prompt, the LSFU-based Gold Shot static prompt alone, and an always-on ("Full") RAG pipeline that retrieves and reflects on every input. Headline results (Table 2): UCPOF achieves the best average accuracy (78.09%) across the six datasets, a +6.03-point gain over the random-exemplar baseline (72.06%) and a +5.75-point gain over Full RAG (72.34%), with the largest single-dataset gain on ACE (+11.01 points over baseline). UCPOF clearly beats Full RAG on ACE and CASIE, and dramatically avoids a collapse Full RAG suffers on CB (48.21% vs. 78.57% for the plain baseline), attributed to always-on retrieval injecting irrelevant or misleading context; on AgNews, Full RAG slightly exceeds UCPOF (91.42% vs. 89.99%), attributed to that dataset being unusually robust to retrieval noise. Efficiency results (Table 8) show UCPOF's average retrieval-trigger rate at 49.34% versus Full RAG's mandatory 100%, with per-dataset trigger rates ranging widely (16.07% on CB to 95.48% on SST5). Ablations isolate two design choices: (1) selecting the highest- rather than lowest-LSFU examples as static exemplars substantially hurts accuracy on the hardest datasets (ACE, CB, R8), and removing Gold Shot selection entirely (reverting to random exemplars in the static stage while keeping dynamic correction) drops UCPOF's average accuracy by 2.20 points (78.09%→75.89%); (2) removing the P_prior focal term from LSFU (i.e., using plain first-token entropy as the gating signal) reduces average AUC for distinguishing correct/incorrect predictions from 0.7555 to 0.7429 and average UCPOF accuracy from 78.09% to 74.37%, with corresponding increases in Expected Calibration Error and Brier score, presented as confirming the prior term's necessity — though the CB-specific numbers in this ablation (82.14% with prior vs. 60.71% without) drive a disproportionate share of the aggregate gap. A risk-coverage analysis (Fig. 4, shown only for CASIE and ACE) shows accuracy rising monotonically as increasingly LSFU-uncertain samples are excluded from consideration, offered as evidence LSFU meaningfully ranks sample difficulty.

## Learnings

The most transferable idea is the specific mechanism for separating "confident because the answer is genuinely clear" from "confident because the label is common" — multiplying entropy by a squared (1−label-prior) term borrowed directly from focal loss's hard-example weighting, rather than inventing a new calibration formula from scratch. This is a concrete, cheap-to-compute (no additional training, no extra forward passes) recipe for anyone building a confidence-gated system on top of a classification-style LLM prompt, and the specific choice to compute the prior from the same calibration/training pool used for exemplar selection (rather than requiring separate corpus statistics) makes it easy to deploy without additional data collection.

A second, more general learning is the paper's explicit two-stage "fast static / slow reflective" inference design as an instance of selective prediction applied to prompt correction rather than to output abstention: instead of the usual selective-classification setup (abstain vs. answer), the "reflective" branch here re-engages the model with additional retrieved context and an explicit self-correction instruction rather than refusing to answer — a reusable pattern for adaptive-compute LLM pipelines where the fallback action is "spend more compute and re-ask" rather than "decline."

A third learning, visible in the ablation results rather than asserted directly, is the finding that a naive always-on RAG pipeline can catastrophically underperform a plain fixed-prompt baseline on some tasks (the CB collapse, 48.21% vs. 78.57%) — a useful, concrete cautionary data point (rather than a generic "retrieval can introduce noise" claim) for anyone assuming that adding retrieval is a safe, monotonic improvement over a well-constructed static prompt, particularly on tasks like natural language inference where superficially similar retrieved sentences may not be relevant to the actual entailment judgment being made.

## Verification

The paper takes several concrete steps to validate LSFU as a meaningful signal rather than an arbitrary formula: a risk-coverage curve (Fig. 4) shows monotonically increasing accuracy as high-LSFU samples are progressively excluded, a distributional plot (Fig. 3) shows correct and incorrect predictions occupying visibly separated (if overlapping) LSFU regions, and calibration metrics (ECE, Brier score) are reported alongside raw accuracy and AUC to check that the prior term genuinely improves calibration rather than just discriminative accuracy. The choice of K=50 for the top-K entropy computation is checked against K∈{50,100,500,full-vocab} in an appendix and found to make negligible difference, which is a reasonable, disclosed robustness check for a specific hyperparameter. However, this validation is uneven across the six evaluated datasets in a way the main text does not discuss: the AUC for distinguishing correct from incorrect predictions using LSFU is 0.81 on ACE and 0.92 on R8, but only 0.5242 on SST5 (Table 5) — essentially indistinguishable from chance — and on SST5 the P_prior ablation (Table 6) shows removing the focal-prior term actually improves accuracy slightly (51.45%→52.44%), the only dataset where the paper's core proposed mechanism is not just ineffective but mildly counterproductive. The risk-coverage and Pareto-efficiency figures that constitute the paper's main qualitative evidence for LSFU's validity (Figs. 3-7) are shown only for CASIE and ACE (with AgNews relegated to an appendix and SST5 not shown at all in this form), so the reader cannot see whether the same accuracy-rises-as-coverage-drops pattern the paper highlights as core supporting evidence actually holds on the dataset where the underlying AUC signal is weakest.

## Field context

The paper positions LSFU against two families of existing uncertainty-quantification approaches it names directly: consistency-based methods (self-consistency, self-scoring via Madaan et al.'s Self-Refine) which it correctly notes scale poorly for real-time use due to needing multiple sampled generations, and logit-based methods (entropy over top-K logits per Clark et al.; semantic entropy per Kuhn et al.; black-box confidence per Lin et al.) which it critiques specifically for ignoring label-prior frequency effects — citing Sharma & Chopra's sequence-level entropy as a directly comparable recent confidence signal that, like plain entropy, does not incorporate class priors. On the RAG side, it distinguishes itself from always-on RAG and from other adaptive-triggering approaches it names (Active RAG's active-learning-based sample selection; Yuan et al.'s instance-adaptive zero-shot chain-of-thought; Dong et al.'s intrinsic response-planning for retrieval timing), arguing these existing adaptive approaches share the same label-prior-blindness problem LSFU is designed to fix. Notably, despite naming all of these as directly relevant prior approaches to the same problem the paper addresses (calibrated, efficient uncertainty-gated inference), none of them — plain entropy-only gating as an ablation is the closest analogue, but semantic entropy, self-consistency, Active RAG, and instance-adaptive CoT specifically are not — appear as experimental baselines in Table 2's main comparison, which instead compares only against a random-exemplar fixed prompt and an always-on RAG pipeline. Within this batch, the paper's central design principle — using a cheap, always-available internal signal (first-token confidence) to decide when to invoke a more expensive downstream mechanism (retrieval) rather than invoking it unconditionally — is structurally similar to 2603.12201's IndexCache (reviewed earlier in this batch), which uses a cheap internal signal (cross-layer index overlap, validated via LM loss) to decide when a more expensive computation (running a fresh indexer) can safely be skipped; both papers share the general strategy of using model-internal signals to convert an always-on cost into a conditionally-triggered one, though IndexCache validates its triggering decision with a considerably more rigorous search-and-ablation protocol than this paper's single 90%-coverage heuristic.

## Critical discussion

The comparison set in the main experiments (Table 2) is narrower than the paper's own Related Work section suggests it should be: semantic entropy, self-consistency, Active RAG, and instance-adaptive zero-shot CoT are all specifically named as addressing closely related problems, yet none is run as an experimental baseline; the only alternative confidence signal actually compared against is plain first-token entropy (via the P_prior ablation), and the only alternative retrieval strategy compared against is unconditional Full RAG. This leaves open whether LSFU's advantage would persist against a more sophisticated but still efficient competing uncertainty signal, or whether a substantial fraction of the reported gain is attributable simply to using any reasonable confidence-based gate rather than specifically to the focal-loss-style prior weighting. The Full RAG comparison is also potentially a weak point in the other direction: its dramatic 30-point collapse on CB (48.21% vs. a 78.57% fixed-prompt baseline) is a striking result the paper attributes to retrieval noise without further diagnosis (e.g., whether the retrieval corpus or similarity metric is simply poorly matched to an entailment-style task), and since CB is the single largest driver of UCPOF's average-accuracy advantage over Full RAG in Table 2, a reader should weigh how much of the paper's "UCPOF beats Full RAG" headline rests on one dataset where the "always-on RAG" comparator may be an unusually poor implementation for that task type, rather than on a broad, even margin across all six datasets.

A second concern is that none of the reported accuracy, AUC, or calibration numbers carry variance, confidence intervals, or a stated number of repeated trials, despite fine-grained percentage-point comparisons being central to nearly every claim in the paper (a +2.20-point ablation delta, a +1.75-point Gold Shot gain, single-decimal-place AUC differences of 0.01-0.02). Given that several of the evaluated datasets are used at what appear to be modest sample sizes for LLM classification benchmarks and inference is run with temperature 0.1 (not fully deterministic), some of the closer reported deltas could plausibly fall within run-to-run noise, which the paper does not report the statistics needed to rule out.

Third, the paper's own results contain a discordant, unaddressed case: on SST5, the LSFU signal has near-chance discriminative AUC (0.5242), UCPOF provides essentially no accuracy improvement over baseline across every table it appears in (51.45%→51.54%), and removing the paper's central proposed mechanism (the P_prior focal term) very slightly improves rather than hurts accuracy on this dataset. Rather than treating this as an informative boundary case worth discussing (e.g., hypothesizing why a 5-way fine-grained sentiment task might not benefit from label-prior calibration, perhaps because its label distribution is closer to uniform or its errors stem from genuine semantic ambiguity rather than prior-driven overconfidence), the main text's illustrative figures for LSFU's core validity (risk-coverage curves, Pareto frontiers) are drawn exclusively from the two datasets where the method performs best (ACE, CASIE), leaving the SST5 near-null result to speak for itself only in raw appendix-adjacent tables without discussion.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the qualitative claim that incorporating label-prior information into a first-token confidence measure improves both discriminative power (AUC) and calibration (ECE, Brier score) relative to plain entropy is demonstrated with a direct, controlled ablation (Table 5-7) holding everything else fixed, and the direction of the effect (improvement on 5 of 6 datasets, roughly neutral on the sixth) is internally consistent across multiple metrics rather than resting on accuracy alone; the finding that gating retrieval by an uncertainty threshold can avoid both the cost and, on at least one task, the catastrophic failure mode of always-on retrieval is a real and useful demonstration of the adaptive-RAG principle, even if the magnitude of the specific reported average gain depends heavily on how representative the CB result is; and the cross-model/cross-scale appendix checks (Llama-3-8B, Qwen2.5-14B) showing the same qualitative ranking of methods holding across different backbones is a genuine, if secondary, piece of evidence against the method being a narrow artifact of one specific model.
