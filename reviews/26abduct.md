Citation: Zahavy, T. LLMs can't jump. Self-published, 2026.

## What this paper is about

Could a modern AI, given everything Einstein knew in 1905, have invented General Relativity? This position paper, by a Google DeepMind researcher, argues no, not because current AI lacks raw capability, but because scientific discovery requires three distinct modes of inference, and AI has only mechanized two of them. Using a detailed historical reconstruction of Einstein's path to General Relativity as a case study, the paper argues that today's systems have effectively solved induction (finding patterns in data) and are rapidly solving deduction (deriving consequences from given premises), but remain structurally unable to perform abduction: the creative leap of inventing the premises, the axioms, in the first place. The paper proposes that this specific gap, not a general reasoning deficit, is why current AI could not have made Einstein's "jump," and suggests that physically grounded, interactive world models may be the missing ingredient.

## Extended summary

The paper adopts Charles Peirce's classical trichotomy of inference (deduction, induction, abduction, distinguished by which of a rule, a case, and a result is being inferred from the other two) and Einstein's own hand-drawn diagram, from a letter to Maurice Solovine, of scientific discovery as a cycle from Sense Experience (E) to a System of Axioms (A) via a conceptual Jump (J), followed by deduction of testable propositions (S). Section 2 reconstructs the historical context in detail, following Norton (2020): the 19th-century mechanical worldview and its fracture under Maxwell's electrodynamics; the Michelson-Morley null result and the ether crisis; and, crucially, the extraordinary precision of Newtonian gravity at the time (the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass had been confirmed to roughly one part in a billion by Eötvös), with its only known anomaly, the advance of Mercury's perihelion, explained away within the existing framework by hypothesizing an undiscovered planet ("Vulcan") rather than treated as a crisis. Special relativity (1905) is presented as resolving the Maxwell/Newton conflict via two postulates and the Lorentz transformation, with Einstein's radical move being to treat the resulting "fictitious time" as literally real (time dilation) rather than a calculational artifact. General relativity's path is then reconstructed in three phases: Ideation (1907-1912), centered on Einstein's "happiest thought" that acceleration and gravity are indistinguishable; Consolidation (1912-1913), the collaboration with mathematician Marcel Grossmann that identified the Riemann curvature tensor as relevant but abandoned it due to a "fatal error," a mistaken belief that the resulting equations could not reduce to Newton's law in the weak-field static limit, a mistake that delayed the final theory by roughly two years; and Mathematical validation (1913-1915), culminating in a frantic four-week sequence of submissions in November 1915 that produced the final field equations.

Section 3 argues against the "creativity as compression" theory of discovery (Schmidhuber, 2008), which frames discovery as inductive: finding a shorter program that better predicts observations. The paper's central historical argument is that this framework cannot explain General Relativity's invention because there was no statistically significant dataset to compress and no meaningful prediction error to drive an inductive search: Newtonian gravity's residual "loss" was effectively zero, and the one known anomaly was rationalized rather than treated as disconfirming evidence. The paper supports this with modern analogues, citing evidence that Transformer models struggle to reverse-engineer even basic arithmetic rules from data, and that foundation models without the correct inductive bias tend to discover heuristic-fitting "flawed world models" rather than genuine causal structure (Vafa et al., 2025). It also notes that empirical confirmation of General Relativity, the Eddington light-bending observation and later relativistic GPS corrections, arrived only after the theory was formulated, and argues that even a "compress the hypothesis space" framing (minimum description length) fails historically, since the actual search path toward the final theory was, for years, more complex than the Newtonian starting point it eventually replaced, with logical simplicity only apparent in retrospect.

Section 4 examines the limits of deduction. The paper concedes that a modern LLM, initialized with Einstein's 1915 postulates, could plausibly carry out much of the downstream derivation, such as computing the perihelion precession of Mercury, framing this as a verifiable logical task. It further concedes that identifying the underlying conceptual inconsistency, that Maxwell's constant speed of light is incompatible with Newtonian absolute time, is itself close to a deductive "bug-finding" operation a modern system might plausibly perform. However, it argues that identifying an inconsistency is distinct from generating the specific axioms that resolve it, illustrating this with the Grossmann "fatal error" episode: even once the correct mathematical object (the Riemann tensor) had been identified, a mistaken judgment about which physical requirement to trust caused a two-year detour, showing that even the "deductive" search phase required nontrivial judgment about which assumption to revisit, not mechanical proof-search alone. The paper cites recent progress in automated formal mathematics (AlphaProof's silver-medal IMO performance, and subsequent gold-level and verified-research-result systems) as evidence that deduction specifically is becoming mechanized, while explicitly scoping its central claim to the axiom-generation step rather than this downstream derivation capability.

Section 5 develops the paper's central positive proposal: that Einstein's "happiest thought," the equivalence principle, exemplifies Magnani's concept of "manipulative abduction," a two-stage process of first simulating an observation (Einstein's imagined observer inside an accelerating, sealed elevator, releasing an object and seeing it appear to fall) and then abducing the best explanation for that simulated observation (that the simulated acceleration and remembered sensation of gravity are literally the same phenomenon). The paper explicitly distinguishes this from ARC-AGI-style abduction benchmarks, arguing ARC tests only the logical component of inferring a rule from sparse symbolic examples, missing the "manipulative," embodied component that grounded Einstein's specific hypothesis in physical sensation. It then contrasts two lines of current world-model research: passive video-generation models (such as Veo), argued to produce "intuitive physics" only as a byproduct of statistical continuation (a falling apple is generated because falling is the dominant continuation in training data, not because gravity is causally modeled), against action-controllable, agentic world models like Genie, which support counterfactual intervention and are proposed as the more promising, if still insufficient, substrate for genuine "thinking by doing." The paper explicitly restricts this proposal to the physical sciences, noting that mathematics or computer science might ground abduction differently, in the abstract landscape of formal systems rather than physical simulation, without resolving how.

The conclusion applies this framework to two current automated-discovery systems: Sakana's AI Scientist and Google DeepMind's own AlphaEvolve, arguing both remain sophisticated "Chinese Rooms" that recombine existing symbolic concepts or optimize against a fixed objective, rather than closing the abductive gap, since AlphaEvolve specifically relies on a gradient signal that, by the paper's own historical argument, was not present in Einstein's actual situation.

## Learnings

A concrete, checkable diagnostic for evaluating whether an inductive "discovery as compression" account actually explains a specific historical scientific breakthrough is to check whether a statistically significant error signal existed beforehand: in Einstein's case, Newtonian gravity's predictions were accurate to roughly one part in a billion, and its one known anomaly was absorbed into the existing framework via an auxiliary hypothesis (an undiscovered planet) rather than treated as disconfirming. This gives a specific, falsifiable test to apply to any claim that a search- or optimization-based AI system could plausibly have reproduced a particular historical scientific leap, rather than leaving "was there enough data or error signal" as an unexamined assumption.

Being able to detect a logical inconsistency between two established frameworks is a distinct capability from being able to generate the specific new axiom that resolves it. The paper illustrates this with the two-year historical detour caused when Einstein and Grossmann correctly identified the relevant mathematical object (the Riemann curvature tensor) but wrongly discarded it due to a mistaken belief about a different physical requirement; even the "deductive search" phase of constructing a new theory required judgment about which existing assumption to revisit, not only mechanical consequence-derivation from fixed premises.

The paper draws a specific, testable distinction between passive predictive world models (argued to reproduce "intuitive physics" only as a byproduct of statistical continuation in their training distribution) and action-controllable, agentic world models that support counterfactual intervention. Only the latter, it argues, is a plausible substrate for embodied, "thinking by doing" hypothesis generation, since genuinely testing a counterfactual (such as "what if the elevator's cable were cut") requires the capacity to manipulate a simulation, not merely to predict its most likely continuation.

## Verification

None. This is a conceptual and historical argument rather than an empirical study; it does not propose or evaluate a method requiring verification, and its evidentiary basis is secondary historical scholarship (principally Norton, 2020) and citations to others' published results (formal-mathematics benchmarks, world-model architectures) rather than new experiments of its own.

## Field context

The paper positions itself as a direct challenge to Schmidhuber's (2008) "creativity as compression" theory of scientific discovery and to the broader inductive paradigm it associates with prior work rediscovering physical laws from data (Wu and Tegmark's "AI Physicist," sparse-optimization PDE discovery). It treats recent progress in automated formal theorem proving (AlphaProof, and cited successors reaching gold-level IMO performance and producing verified solutions to open research questions) as evidence that deduction specifically, not scientific invention generally, is what is currently being mechanized. It draws on Peirce's classical inference trichotomy and on Magnani's "manipulative abduction" from philosophy of science, and on Harnad's symbol-grounding-problem framing of the "Chinese Room," situating its central claim within an established philosophy-of-mind literature rather than proposing new philosophical machinery. Notably, the paper directly critiques two current automated-discovery agents, Sakana's external AI Scientist and Google DeepMind's own internal AlphaEvolve project, applying the same abductive-gap critique to the author's own lab's flagship discovery system as to an outside one. It also explicitly engages public commentary from Demis Hassabis and Fei-Fei Li on spatial intelligence and world models as AI's next frontier, and positions Genie, another DeepMind project, as the architectural direction the paper's own proposal points toward. Within this corpus, its central worry, that fluency with a domain's formal or symbolic apparatus can outrun genuine grounding in what that apparatus is about, echoes from a different angle the concerns in this corpus's reviews of 24selfcor and 2604.27251, where a model's stated reasoning process is shown to diverge from what actually produced its behavior; here the concern is about invention rather than post hoc justification, but the underlying pattern, symbolic competence without the grounding that competence appears to presuppose, is the same family of concern.

## Critical discussion

The paper's strongest and most concrete claim, that Newtonian gravity presented no statistically significant error signal before 1915, is well documented (the specific precision figures and the Vulcan episode are historically established) and directly undercuts a naive reading of "discovery as compression" for this particular case, making it the paper's best-supported point.

The argument against induction is not, however, fully airtight on its own terms. Schmidhuber's compression-progress framework is about reducing the total description length of everything an agent has observed or been told, which plausibly includes conceptual facts such as the incompatibility between Maxwell's field equations and Newtonian absolute time, not only numerical residuals against Newtonian predictions specifically. The paper's own Section 4 treats this same conceptual inconsistency as a real trigger that a deductive "bug-finding" process could plausibly detect, but does not fully engage the possibility that detecting and wanting to resolve exactly this kind of inconsistency, two separately-described domains that could in principle be unified into one shorter description, is itself the sort of "compression opportunity" the theory it is arguing against is about. This is a genuine tension in the paper's own two-front argument (against induction in Section 3, conceding ground to deduction in Section 4) rather than a decisive refutation of the overall position, but it means the induction/deduction boundary is doing more argumentative work than the paper fully defends.

Similarly, the paper's own historical narrative somewhat blurs its own three-way taxonomy: the 1912-1913 search for the correct field-equation candidate, including the "fatal error" of discarding the Riemann-tensor-based approach, is presented as a deductive, search-over-constraints process, yet it also involved a substantive judgment call about which physical requirement to trust versus reexamine, an activity that shares more with the hypothesis-revision character the paper reserves specifically for the 1907 "happiest thought." The Einstein-Jump-Axioms framework is a useful organizing device, but the paper's own case study shows the boundary between its categories is not always sharp, a point worth naming rather than treating the trichotomy as a clean partition of the historical episode it examines.

The paper's positive proposal, that physically consistent, action-controllable world models are the needed substrate for mechanizing abductive invention, is presented and appropriately hedged as a conjecture about a future architecture ("we propose," "may finally mechanize") rather than as a demonstrated result; no experiment, prototype, or worked toy example is offered showing an interactive world model producing a genuinely novel physical hypothesis through counterfactual intervention. This is not a flaw in the sense of an overclaim, since the paper does not claim to have built or tested such a system, but it does mean the proposal's specific mechanism remains untested. A genuine strength worth crediting is that the paper turns its critical lens on its own lab's flagship discovery-agent project (AlphaEvolve) with the same specificity it applies to an external one (Sakana's AI Scientist), grounding both critiques in a concrete, falsifiable claim (that AlphaEvolve depends on a gradient signal the paper's own historical argument says would not have existed in Einstein's situation) rather than a vague dismissal of either system.

What remains well supported: the historical claim that Newtonian gravity left no significant error signal before 1915 stands on solid documented ground and is a real problem for a naive compression-driven account of this specific discovery; the distinction between deductively verifying a set of axioms and generating those axioms in the first place is a genuine and well-illustrated one, grounded in the concrete Grossmann "fatal error" episode; and the paper's proposed research direction, interactive world models supporting counterfactual intervention as opposed to passive predictive ones, is a clearly labeled, testable hypothesis about where future progress might come from, rather than a claim already backed by evidence.

## Relevance to us

This connects directly to our own emphasis on problem formulation over solving (Arc 3 and B17, the problem-formulation engine turning a hunch into a testable program) and to the tension TAXONOMY.md names between solving and formulation, where the literature "crowds solving" and formulation remains comparatively thin: this paper is, in effect, a detailed, historically grounded argument for exactly that asymmetry in theoretical physics specifically, and it is a useful, independent corroboration of the bet that formulation, not derivation, is where the harder and more distinctively valuable work lies. Its specific mechanism claim, that genuine axiom generation may require an embodied, action-controllable simulation substrate rather than more text or more proof-search capability, bears directly on how we might prioritize between B05 (intervention-capable physics world models) and B06 (physics-native JEPA) relative to further investment in purely text-based or proof-search reasoning: if the paper's argument holds, better chain-of-thought or better formal-proof capability would not by itself close the gap it identifies, since that gap is specifically about generating new premises rather than deriving consequences of given ones, whereas a system that supports genuine counterfactual intervention on a physically consistent simulated scenario would more directly target the mechanism the paper describes. At the same time, this is a conceptual position paper without an experiment or working prototype behind its central proposal, so it should be treated as one well-argued hypothesis to test against rather than as a settled constraint on system design; we should not assume our own approach either satisfies or is exempt from the specific gap it identifies without checking. Separately from its central thesis, the paper is itself a useful data point that the difficulty of problem formulation, not just solving, is recognized as a live, unresolved gap inside a leading industry lab, including by a researcher critiquing his own lab's discovery-agent project on exactly this point, which corroborates rather than merely asserts one of the tensions already in our own framing.
