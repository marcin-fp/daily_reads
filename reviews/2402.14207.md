Citation: Shao, Y., Jiang, Y., Kanell, T. A., Xu, P., Khattab, O., Lam, M. S. Assisting in Writing Wikipedia-like Articles From Scratch with Large Language Models. NAACL 2024. arXiv:2402.14207.

## What this paper is about

Writing a full, well-organized Wikipedia-style article on a topic from nothing is a much harder problem than the retrieval-augmented generation tasks usually studied, because most of the real difficulty happens before any writing starts: figuring out what to even research and how to structure it. This paper studies that "pre-writing" stage directly and proposes STORM, a system that first discovers several distinct perspectives on a topic by surveying related Wikipedia articles, then has an LLM role-play a writer holding each perspective and ask a simulated topic expert a multi-turn series of increasingly specific questions, with every expert answer grounded in retrieved, trust-filtered web sources, and finally synthesizes everything gathered into a structured outline that a separate writing stage expands into a full, cited article. Evaluated against retrieval-augmented baselines on a purpose-built dataset of recent Wikipedia articles and reviewed by experienced Wikipedia editors, STORM produces more broadly-covering and better-organized articles, while the expert review surfaces new failure modes, beyond plain factual hallucination, that the paper argues future grounded-writing systems will need to address.

## Extended summary

The paper first identifies a specific gap in prior Wikipedia-generation research (summarized directly in a comparison table): earlier work either generates only short snippets (a single paragraph), works within one narrow domain, or assumes an outline or reference documents are already supplied, sidestepping exactly the research-and-planning problem this paper targets. To evaluate the harder, full-scope version of the task without help from these assumptions, the paper curates FreshWiki, a dataset of high-quality (ORES B-class or above), heavily-edited Wikipedia articles created or substantially rewritten after the training cutoff of the LLMs tested, specifically to reduce the risk that a model can succeed by having already memorized the target article during pretraining; list articles and articles without subsections are excluded, and the dataset is restricted to plain text (no tables or images) to keep the task tractable. The task is decomposed to mirror the human writing process into a pre-writing stage (produce an outline, a set of multi-level section headings, plus a pool of reference documents) and a writing stage (expand that outline into a full cited article), with two purpose-built automatic metrics for outline quality, heading soft recall (Sentence-BERT cosine similarity between generated and ground-truth headings, avoiding requiring exact string matches) and heading entity recall (the fraction of named entities in the ground-truth headings that the generated outline covers).

STORM's pipeline has four stages. First, perspective discovery: given a topic, an LLM proposes related topics, the system pulls those related topics' actual Wikipedia tables of contents via the Wikipedia API, and an LLM synthesizes N distinct "perspectives" (for example, an event planner focused on logistics versus a layperson wanting basic facts) from that context, always including one fixed "basic facts" perspective to guarantee baseline coverage. Second, simulated conversation: for each perspective in parallel, an LLM plays a Wikipedia-writer role asking a sequence of up to M questions to a simulated topic-expert role, with conversation history feeding back into each subsequent question so later questions can build on earlier answers; critically, every expert answer is grounded by first decomposing the question into search queries, retrieving results, filtering them with a rule-based check against Wikipedia's own reliable-sources guideline, and only then having the LLM synthesize an answer from the surviving trustworthy sources (which are also added to the growing reference pool). Third, outline creation: the LLM first drafts an outline from its own parametric knowledge alone, then revises that draft using all the collected multi-perspective conversations, intentionally combining the model's prior knowledge with newly-researched, topic-specific information rather than relying on either alone. Fourth, article writing: each outline section is generated independently and in parallel, retrieving only the reference-pool documents most relevant to that specific section and subsection via Sentence-BERT similarity (since the full reference pool cannot fit in context), after which the LLM removes duplicated information across the concatenated sections and finally synthesizes a lead summary section, following Wikipedia's own stylistic convention.

The experiments (100 FreshWiki topics with human-written articles under 3000 words, for tractable comparison) compare STORM against three retrieval-augmented baselines of increasing sophistication: Direct Gen (no retrieval, pure parametric generation), RAG (a single topic-based search feeding outline and article generation), and Outline-driven RAG (oRAG, which additionally re-searches using each generated section title before writing that section). On outline quality (Table 3), Direct Gen already achieves surprisingly high heading soft recall, which the paper attributes to LLMs' strong general parametric grasp of how a topic is typically structured, but STORM achieves the highest recall and entity recall under both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 backbones, beating even an augmented "RAG-expand" variant that gives the RAG baseline an extra round of section-title-driven search specifically to test whether more retrieval alone could close the gap; it cannot fully close it. On full-article quality (Table 2, GPT-4 only, since the paper reports GPT-3.5 is not faithful enough to sources when generating cited text), STORM significantly outperforms all baselines on ROUGE scores, entity recall, and LLM-judged (Prometheus-13B, on a rubric co-developed with two experienced Wikipedia editors) Interest Level, Relevance, and Coverage, though Organization scores are close across methods. An ablation removing STORM's outline stage entirely (writing directly from topic and simulated conversations) causes across-the-board deterioration, confirming the outline stage's specific value rather than the conversations alone being sufficient. Separate ablations (removing perspective-guidance, or removing the multi-turn conversational structure in favor of a flat set of pre-generated questions) each degrade outline quality, with removing the conversational structure hurting substantially more, and a companion analysis shows this tracks directly with a large drop in the number of unique reference sources actually collected (STORM's full pipeline: 99.83 average unique sources; without conversation: 39.56), tying the ablation's quality loss to a concrete, countable cause. A citation-quality check (Mistral-7B-Instruct judging whether cited sources actually entail the generated sentence, following a prior citation-recall/precision framework) finds 84.83% of STORM's generated sentences are judged supported by their citations, with a follow-up qualitative analysis (Appendix C.3) finding the main source of unsupported sentences is improper inference or inaccurate paraphrasing rather than outright hallucination of nonexistent content.

Human evaluation recruited 10 experienced Wikipedia editors (each with 500+ edits and over a year of experience) to rate 20 topic-pairs of STORM versus oRAG output (best baseline by automatic metrics), each pair reviewed by two editors on a 1-7 scale across the same five quality dimensions, with reported Krippendorff's alpha inter-annotator agreement ranging from 0.221 (Organization) to 0.388 (Verifiability), figures the paper reports transparently without characterizing them further. STORM articles were rated more organized (25 percentage points more articles rated "good," meaning a 4+ rating, on Organization) and broader in coverage (10 points more) than oRAG, were preferred overall in pairwise comparison (26 of 40 comparisons versus 14 for oRAG), and Organization was the one dimension reaching conventional statistical significance (p=0.005) in a paired t-test; Interest Level and Coverage showed the same direction but did not clear the significance threshold (p=0.077, 0.084), and Verifiability and Relevance showed no meaningful difference. Editors' open-ended feedback surfaced two specific, named failure modes distinct from ordinary factual hallucination: first, a "red herring fallacy" or overspeculation pattern, where the article draws an unverifiable connection between two pieces of retrieved information or between a fact and the overall topic, something the paper explicitly distinguishes from simpler fact-checking since the individual facts involved may each be accurate; and second, source-bias and tone transfer, with 7 of 10 editors independently describing STORM-generated articles as sounding "emotional" or "unneutral," attributed to the system inheriting whatever slant is present in the retrieved web sources rather than Wikipedia's own neutral-point-of-view editorial norms. Editors also directly compared STORM output to the actual human-written Wikipedia article on the same topic and, while several found STORM's output added useful supplementary context, generally judged it less informative overall than a well-revised human article. Finally, a post-hoc usefulness survey found unanimous agreement among the 10 editors that STORM would help them personally with the pre-writing stage of an article, 80% agreement it could help them edit an article on a genuinely new topic, and 70% (versus 10% disagreeing) agreement it could be broadly useful to the Wikipedia community, with somewhat more reservation expressed about that broader community-level claim than about STORM's usefulness to themselves individually.

## Learnings

The paper's most transferable design insight is that giving multiple simulated question-askers distinct, externally-derived perspectives (mined from the tables of contents of related, already-written articles, rather than invented from nothing) produces meaningfully more diverse and specific research questions than either direct topic-based prompting or an undifferentiated pool of questions, and that this diversity converts directly into a measurable outcome: more unique reference sources actually collected (99.83 versus 39.56 without the conversational, perspective-driven structure), which in turn tracks with better outline coverage. This is a concrete, generalizable pattern for any system that needs an LLM to generate a broad set of information-seeking queries about a topic: anchoring the "who is asking" role in externally-sourced structural context (here, other articles' actual organization) rather than only varying the prompt wording appears to produce genuinely different, not just superficially reworded, questions.

A second, more mechanistic learning is the specific value of separating "draft an outline from parametric knowledge alone" from "revise that outline using freshly retrieved research," rather than doing only one or the other: the paper's own outline-generation step deliberately does both in sequence, and the ablation removing the entire outline stage (going straight from research conversations to a written article) causes the largest quality drop of any single ablation tested, indicating that an explicit, separately-verifiable intermediate planning artifact carries real value beyond what the underlying retrieved information alone provides, a specific instance of "give the model an intermediate structured artifact to commit to before the final generation" being worth the extra step.

A third, more cautionary learning, drawn directly from the expert human evaluation, is that as a grounded generation system is given richer, more comprehensive source material, its potential failure modes shift qualitatively rather than simply shrinking: STORM's most-collected-sources configuration produces fewer outright factual hallucinations but introduces a distinct problem, plausible-sounding but unverifiable connective inferences between individually-true facts ("red herring fallacy" or overspeculation), that ordinary fact-checking against individual citations will not catch because each cited fact may itself be accurate. Any system evaluation that only checks whether individual claims are supported by a citation, without separately checking whether the inferential connections drawn between those claims are themselves warranted, will systematically miss this specific class of error.

## Verification

The paper builds verification into the pipeline itself as well as into its evaluation. Within STORM, every simulated expert answer is grounded by decomposing the question into search queries, retrieving results, and applying a rule-based filter against Wikipedia's own reliable-sources guideline before the answer is synthesized, meaning the system has a built-in source-trust check operating during generation rather than only checking the final output after the fact. Citation quality is checked post hoc using an independent model (Mistral-7B-Instruct, distinct from the GPT-4 used for generation) applying a natural-language-inference-style entailment check between each cited source and its corresponding sentence, following a prior citation-recall/precision framework rather than an ad hoc metric, and the paper follows up with a qualitative analysis of the specific unsupported cases rather than stopping at the aggregate percentage, distinguishing improper inference from outright fabrication. The paper is also explicit and self-critical about a specific verification risk in its own automatic evaluation: it states plainly that its LLM-based rubric evaluator (Prometheus-13B) may systematically overrate machine-generated text, and treats the subsequent, independent expert human evaluation as the check on that risk rather than relying on the automatic scores alone; the human evaluation's results (smaller and more mixed effect sizes than the automatic scores, with Verifiability and Relevance showing no significant STORM advantage despite automatic metrics favoring STORM broadly) directly validate that this caution was warranted, since the two evaluation methods diverge in exactly the way the paper worried they might. The human evaluation itself reports inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's alpha) for every rated dimension rather than only reporting mean scores, a genuine transparency practice that lets a reader judge how reliable each specific dimension's rating is, though the paper does not itself flag or discuss the specific implication that its lowest-agreement dimension (Organization, alpha 0.221) is also the one dimension reaching statistical significance in the paired comparison.

## Field context

This paper sits in the retrieval-augmented-generation literature it explicitly surveys (Lewis et al. 2020's foundational RAG work; Semnani et al. 2023's Wikipedia-grounded anti-hallucination chatbot; citation-generation work like Gao et al. 2023, whose citation-recall/precision framework this paper adopts directly) while identifying a specific, underexplored gap, that essentially all prior RAG-for-writing work assumes either a supplied outline or supplied reference documents, sidestepping the research-and-planning problem this paper isolates and names the "pre-writing stage." It also engages the question-asking-in-NLP literature (Qi et al. 2020's reinforcement-learning-based question-informativeness optimization is named as the closest prior work) and expository-writing research (Balepur et al. 2023's paragraph-level Imitate-Retrieve-Paraphrase framework; Shen et al. 2023's emphasis on an author's sensemaking process over sources), positioning STORM's contribution as extending these ideas to the harder, full-article, no-supplied-scaffolding setting. Within this corpus, STORM sits naturally alongside [2312.04854] (MADKE, reviewed earlier in this batch), which shares the underlying idea of grounding multiple distinct simulated viewpoints in a shared pool of retrieved web and Wikipedia content, MADKE for adversarial debate toward a single correct answer, STORM for generative research toward a comprehensive outline, and both papers converge independently on retrieval-grounded diversity as the mechanism for getting more out of multiple simulated perspectives than any one query or persona could surface alone. STORM's genre and stated purpose, generating full Wikipedia-style articles automatically, also puts it in direct, informative contrast with [2211.09085] (Meta's Galactica, reviewed in an earlier batch), whose public demo was withdrawn within three days specifically because it generated confident, fluent, but fabricated Wikipedia-style content with no retrieval grounding at all; STORM's entire architecture, external multi-perspective research, source-trust filtering during generation, independent citation-entailment checking, and expert human review specifically probing for unverifiable-but-plausible content, reads as a direct, considerably more cautious answer to exactly the failure mode that made Galactica's demo untenable, and the fact that STORM's own expert evaluators still surface a residual, more subtle version of that same problem (the "red herring fallacy") is a useful indication of how much of that risk persists even under a much more carefully grounded design.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central comparative claims are well-supported by a genuinely layered evaluation: automatic outline metrics, automatic article-quality metrics (with an LLM judge whose potential bias the authors name explicitly), a citation-entailment check using a separate model, and, crucially, an independent expert human evaluation that the authors treat as the check on the automatic LLM-judge scores rather than as a redundant formality, and the human evaluation's more modest, dimension-specific results (only Organization reaching significance, Verifiability and Relevance showing no advantage) are reported plainly rather than downplayed in favor of the more uniformly favorable automatic scores. The ablation studies are cleanly isolated and each connects a specific design choice to a specific, measurable outcome (fewer unique sources collected without conversational question-asking; broad quality collapse without the outline stage), giving the paper's architectural claims real mechanistic grounding rather than resting on end-to-end comparison alone.

The clearest place for independent caution is the reliability of the human evaluation that carries the paper's most emphasized qualitative claims: the reported Krippendorff's alpha values (0.221 to 0.388 across the five rating dimensions) indicate fairly weak inter-annotator agreement by conventional interpretation, and this is most consequential precisely on Organization, the one dimension whose STORM-versus-oRAG difference reaches statistical significance (p=0.005) and is also the dimension with the lowest reported agreement (0.221) of the five; the paper reports this number transparently but does not discuss what it implies for how much weight the headline "STORM produces more organized articles" claim can bear, and a reader should treat that specific claim with more caution than its p-value alone would suggest. A second, related point concerns sample size: the human evaluation's headline pairwise-preference result (26 of 40 preferring STORM) and per-dimension ratings rest on only 20 topic-pairs, each reviewed by 2 of the 10 recruited editors, a reasonably small base for the strength of the paper's overall claims about STORM's superiority, though this is a common and understandable constraint given the cost of recruiting experienced Wikipedia editors for this kind of careful review. A third point, one the paper's own Limitations section raises directly, is that the "red herring fallacy" and source-bias-transfer problems identified by editors are, by the paper's own account, not solved by anything in the current system, meaning STORM's demonstrated improvement over baselines coexists with a real, editor-documented risk of generating plausible-sounding overspeculation that citation-level fact-checking alone will not catch, a genuine and clearly-named open problem rather than one the paper claims to have resolved.

What remains well supported: perspective-guided, retrieval-grounded, multi-turn question asking produces measurably broader and better-organized research outcomes than direct prompting or single-shot retrieval, confirmed by both automatic outline-coverage metrics and, for organization specifically, statistically significant expert human ratings; an explicit outline-creation stage is a necessary, not merely helpful, component of the pipeline, since removing it causes across-the-board quality collapse in ablation; and the system, while a genuine improvement over the tested baselines and judged broadly useful by the recruited expert Wikipedia editors for their own pre-writing workflow, does not close the gap to well-revised human-authored articles and introduces a specific, real, editor-documented risk (overspeculative but individually-well-cited connections between facts) that goes beyond and is not caught by ordinary citation-level verification.
