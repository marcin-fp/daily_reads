Citation: Kim, K., Kotha, S., Liang, P., Hashimoto, T. Pre-training under infinite compute. arXiv:2509.14786, 2025.

## What this paper is about

Language model pre-training has always been studied under the assumption that web text is essentially unlimited while compute is the scarce resource, and most existing scaling-law research (Chinchilla, inference-scaling laws, etc.) optimizes for that regime. But web text grows only about 3% per year while compute spent on pre-training grows roughly 4x per year, so the opposite regime, a fixed, limited pool of text with effectively unlimited compute to spend on it, is approaching. This paper asks what the best way to train a language model is once compute stops being the constraint and only data is: existing recipes for reusing limited data (repeating it, or making the model bigger) turn out to eventually make things worse rather than better, and the paper works out, empirically, which combination of algorithmic choices, regularization, ensembling multiple independently-trained models, and distillation, actually keeps improving as more compute is thrown at a fixed, small pool of tokens.

## Extended summary

The paper fixes a seed corpus of 200M tokens from DCLM web data and asks how well a model can be made to perform on held-out validation loss given unlimited compute to spend on that fixed pool. The standard data-constrained recipe (repeating tokens across more epochs, per Muennighoff et al. 2023, and/or scaling parameter count, per Kaplan et al. 2020) is shown to overfit: increasing epoch count eventually raises loss rather than lowering it, and increasing parameter count with epoch count separately tuned at each size also eventually raises loss, with a 1.4B model actually performing worse than a 600M model at this token budget. This directly contradicts the functional form assumed in the epoch-scaling law of Muennighoff et al. (2023), which the current paper notes that prior work's own appendix acknowledges by discarding overfitting runs when fitting their law.

The paper's first fix is much heavier regularization than standard practice: a coordinate-descent-style search over weight decay, learning rate, and epoch count jointly at each parameter count finds that the optimal weight decay for the most over-parameterized models is more than 30x the commonly-used default of 0.1. With this regularization properly tuned, loss decreases monotonically as a clean power law in parameter count, holding for parameter-to-token ratios up to 140x larger than the Chinchilla-recommended ratio, a regime where the standard recipe was overfitting badly. Because the loss now decreases monotonically without limit as parameter count grows, the paper argues that the right way to compare compute-unconstrained recipes is not their performance at a fixed compute budget (the traditional scaling-law comparison), but the asymptote of their power-law fit as parameter count goes to infinity, since with genuinely unlimited compute a practitioner would simply keep scaling until the marginal benefit vanishes. The regularized recipe's estimated asymptotic loss is 3.43 (versus an unregularized comparison implied around 3.75), with a reported sensitivity of about ±0.02 loss across three random seeds.

The paper's second, larger improvement comes from ensembling: instead of training one arbitrarily large model, independently training K models of a fixed size and averaging their output logits at inference achieves a lower loss asymptote (3.34, using 300M-parameter members as K goes to infinity) than scaling a single model's parameter count to infinity (3.43), meaning that at sufficiently high total parameter budgets it is better to train several smaller independent models than one correspondingly larger one; even a 3-member ensemble already beats the single-model asymptote. The paper cites a theoretical account (Allen-Zhu and Li 2023) attributing this to a "multi-view" structure in the data, where a single model tends to latch onto one predictive feature while independently-initialized ensemble members tend to pick up different features. Because the optimal hyperparameters for a single model are not the same as the optimal hyperparameters for a large ensemble (more epochs and less weight decay per member turns out to be better as the ensemble grows, intuitively because more per-member overfitting can help each member specialize on different views), tuning specifically for the infinite-ensemble asymptote improves it further, from 3.34 to 3.27. Composing both interventions, taking both member count and per-member parameter count to infinity via a "joint scaling recipe," yields the paper's best estimated asymptote of 3.17, using an experimentally-motivated heuristic for translating single-model-optimal hyperparameters to ensemble-optimal ones (roughly 2x the epochs, 0.5x the weight decay) rather than a full independent hyperparameter search at every scale, since that had proven computationally impractical.

To check whether the 200M-token results generalize, the paper repeats key portions of this pipeline at 400M, 800M, and 1.7B seed tokens, building "data scaling laws" over the fitted asymptotes at each token count. At 200M tokens, the regularized recipe is estimated to be 2.29x more data-efficient than the standard recipe, and the joint (regularized + ensembled) recipe 5.17x more data-efficient; the paper notes that even without any extrapolation, an observed 1.4B model already achieves a 2.09x efficiency gain over the standard-recipe baseline, and an observed 5-member 1.4B ensemble achieves a 3.75x gain, giving a partially non-extrapolated anchor for the extrapolated headline numbers. Fitted data-scaling laws for all three recipes across the four tested token counts show similar decay exponents (0.23-0.24) and asymptotes (1.89-1.96), which the paper interprets as suggesting the relative data-efficiency improvement should persist rather than vanish at much larger token budgets, though it explicitly flags this tiered extrapolation as likely noisy.

Because the ensembling and regularized-scaling asymptotes both rely on arbitrarily large total parameter counts, the paper separately tests whether the resulting efficiency gains can be compressed into a small deployable model. Sequence-level knowledge distillation from an 8-member 300M-parameter ensemble (2.4B total parameters, teacher loss 3.32) into a single 300M student retains 83% of the ensembling improvement over the best single regularized 300M model, achieving 3.36 loss at 8x lower inference cost than the full ensemble, and even slightly beating the extrapolated regularized-recipe asymptote itself. More surprisingly, self-distillation, training a fresh 300M student of the identical architecture on a mixture of the original real tokens and tokens sampled unconditionally from a 300M teacher of the same size, improves loss over the teacher, despite the fact that a naive information-theoretic argument (and a substantial recent literature on "model collapse" from training on recursively self-generated data, e.g. Dohmatob et al. 2024, Shumailov et al. 2024) would suggest this should be impossible or actively harmful. The paper attributes the improvement to the same "implicit ensembling" mechanism (Allen-Zhu and Li 2023) that explains standard ensembling's benefit, and to the specific choice of mixing real and synthetic tokens rather than training purely on model-generated text, which the paper argues is the key difference from the collapse-inducing setups in that prior literature.

The paper then checks whether these validation-loss-based findings hold on two more practically relevant fronts. First, on standard downstream accuracy benchmarks appropriate to this model scale (PIQA, SciQ, ARC-Easy), evaluated only after all recipe selection was finalized on validation loss (a deliberate design choice to avoid overfitting the benchmarks), the same qualitative pattern holds: the unregularized standard recipe does not benefit meaningfully from parameter scaling, while the regularized and ensembled recipes scale smoothly with diminishing returns, and the best ensemble beats the best unregularized model by over 9% average accuracy, with the best distilled 300M model beating the unregularized 300M baseline by around 7%. Second, the paper tests transfer to continued pre-training (CPT), replicating a math mid-training setup (Wang et al. 2025's OctoThinker recipe, continuing Llama 3.2 3B Base on MegaMath-Web-Pro) but restricting to only 4B of the original 73B seed tokens; applying the paper's interventions (smaller batch size, tuned epoching, and ensembling up to 8 epoched models) on this restricted 4B-token budget yields an average GSM8K/MATH/MathQA accuracy (40.58%) that exceeds the reference recipe's own performance using the full, untouched 73B-token budget (39.23%), a reported 17.5x data-efficiency improvement in this specific applied setting.

## Learnings

The paper's central, most broadly transferable finding is a clean, quantified demonstration that heavier-than-standard regularization is what converts an overfitting, non-monotonic data-constrained scaling curve into a clean, monotonically-improving power law, with the specific, surprising magnitude (roughly 30x the commonly-used default weight decay) being directly actionable for anyone training under a similarly small, fixed data budget rather than the usual data-rich regime most published recipes assume.

A second, structurally important learning is the paper's proposed shift in how to compare recipes when compute is not the bottleneck: evaluating a scaling recipe by the asymptote of its power law (the best it could ever do with unlimited parameters or ensemble members) rather than by its performance at a matched, finite compute budget, is a specific, reusable methodological reframing for any future data-constrained scaling study, distinct from the traditional compute-matched comparison that dominates the scaling-law literature this paper otherwise builds on.

A third, genuinely counter-intuitive learning is the demonstrated case of same-size self-distillation improving on the teacher: a student trained on a mixture of the original real data and unconditional samples from a same-architecture, same-size teacher can outperform that teacher, in specific tension with the broader "model collapse" literature on training on recursively self-generated data. The paper's own proposed explanation, that mixing in real data alongside synthetic samples is the critical difference from collapse-inducing setups, is a concrete, testable hypothesis for reconciling this result with the collapse literature rather than treating the two bodies of evidence as simply contradictory.

## Verification

The paper takes several concrete steps to check that its central claims are not artifacts of a specific experimental choice. The overfitting finding for the standard recipe is checked against, and found to directly contradict, the specific functional form of a prior published scaling law (Muennighoff et al. 2023), with the paper noting that the prior work's own appendix independently discards overfitting runs, corroborating rather than merely asserting the discrepancy. The regularized recipe's monotone power-law claim is supported by an explicit hyperparameter search procedure (coordinate descent to a locally-optimal point) described directly, and a stated sensitivity analysis reports the estimated asymptote varies by at most 0.02 loss across three random seeds, giving a concrete (if narrow) uncertainty bound on that specific number. The core "asymptote" methodology is applied consistently across every scaling law fit in the paper (regularized recipe, ensembling, joint recipe, data-scaling laws), and the joint-scaling-recipe result is explicitly cross-checked against a non-extrapolated data point (the observed 1.4B, 5-member ensemble achieving 3.75x data efficiency without relying on any asymptotic extrapolation), which helps ground the more speculative extrapolated 5.17x headline number. The downstream-benchmark generalization check is methodologically careful in a specific, verifiable way: the paper states benchmarks were not evaluated until after all recipe selection had already been finalized using validation loss alone, which is a real, checkable safeguard against implicitly overfitting the recipe search to the eventual downstream metric.

Verification is comparatively thinner in the extrapolation machinery itself. Every asymptote estimate in the paper is obtained by fitting a specific power-law-plus-asymptote functional form to a small number of data points, typically four parameter counts (or four token counts, or five ensemble-member counts) per curve, and then reading off the fitted limit as that variable goes to infinity; the reported ±0.02 seed-sensitivity check addresses run-to-run training noise but does not address the separate, arguably larger risk that the assumed functional form itself could be a poor description of how loss actually continues to behave far beyond the tested range, since no alternative functional forms, goodness-of-fit statistics, or extrapolation-error bounds beyond the seed check are reported in the main text for these fits. The self-distillation "avoids collapse" claim is demonstrated for a single distillation generation (one teacher, one student) rather than the iterated, multi-generation recursive setting that the model-collapse literature the paper cites specifically studies, so the claim that mixing real and synthetic data prevents collapse is verified only for the one-step case actually tested, not for repeated rounds of self-distillation where collapse effects are typically reported to compound.

## Field context

The paper explicitly positions itself against the entire compute-constrained scaling-law tradition (Chinchilla/Hoffmann et al. 2022, Kaplan et al. 2020, and the broader family of inference-time and hardware-precision scaling laws it cites), arguing that regime's core assumption, effectively unlimited data, will invert as compute grows roughly 4x per year against data growing roughly 1.03x per year (citing Sevilla and Roldán 2024, Villalobos et al. 2024), and it identifies a specific, concrete gap in the one directly relevant prior data-constrained scaling study (Muennighoff et al. 2023): that work's own scaling law does not account for the overfitting the present paper documents, and silently discards the runs where it occurs. The paper explicitly revives several classical, pre-LLM-era data-constrained deep learning techniques (heavy regularization, ensembling, and distillation, drawing an explicit parallel to older data-constrained benchmarks like Penn Treebank and ImageNet) and frames its contribution as demonstrating these classical tools compose effectively with modern LLM pre-training once data, not compute, becomes the bottleneck, consistent with what it calls "The Bitter Lesson" framing. Within this corpus, the paper's self-distillation result and its explicit engagement with model-collapse theory connects directly to [2402.07043] (the model-collapse paper reviewed earlier in this batch), since this paper's central self-distillation finding, that training a fresh model on a real-plus-synthetic-data mixture from a same-size teacher improves rather than degrades performance, is a specific empirical case that sits in tension with, and offers a concrete boundary condition for, the collapse-under-recursive-training theory that paper investigates; a natural point for future synthesis is whether the "mix in real data" mitigation this paper credits for avoiding collapse holds up under the multi-generation, iterated retraining setting the collapse literature specifically studies, which this paper does not itself test.

## Critical discussion

The paper's empirical core, that regularization, ensembling, and distillation all measurably improve data efficiency in the compute-unconstrained, small-data regime it constructs, is demonstrated with real methodological care: multiple recipes are compared using a consistent, well-motivated asymptote-based metric rather than a single arbitrary compute budget, key claims are cross-validated against both a non-extrapolated observed data point and an independent literature comparison (the Muennighoff et al. discrepancy), and the downstream-benchmark generalization check is protected against post-hoc overfitting by the stated evaluation-after-recipe-selection discipline.

The most significant limitation is how much of the paper's most striking numbers, the 5.17x data efficiency gain from the joint scaling recipe, the claim that these gains "persist" at higher token budgets, and the exact asymptotic loss values themselves, rest on extrapolating power-law fits from a small number of directly observed points (typically four to five) out to an infinite limit, with the paper's own reported uncertainty analysis (±0.02 loss across seeds) addressing only training-run noise rather than the separate, and likely larger, risk that the assumed power-law-with-asymptote functional form could itself be a poor description of behavior far outside the tested range; the paper is transparent that its "tiered" data-scaling-law extrapolation is "bound to be noisy," which is an honest caveat but does mean the specific multiplier (5.17x) should be read as a plausible, non-extrapolated-lower-bound-anchored estimate (the observed, non-extrapolated 1.4B/5-ensemble result of 3.75x is a real, directly-measured floor) rather than as an established, precisely-pinned figure. A second, related limitation is scale: every experiment in the paper operates at 150M-1.4B parameters and 200M-1.7B seed tokens, several orders of magnitude below the parameter and token counts at which frontier labs actually operate, and below the scale at which the paper's own motivating scenario (a compute-rich future where data becomes the true bottleneck for organizations with access to the entire useful web text) would most directly apply; the qualitative direction of the findings (regularization prevents overfitting under data constraints, ensembling beats parameter scaling in the compute-unconstrained limit) is plausibly scale-invariant given how consistently these patterns replicate across the four token counts tested, but the specific numerical multipliers have not been demonstrated to hold at the token and parameter scales where the underlying motivation (data becoming genuinely scarce relative to compute) would actually bind. A third, narrower limitation is that the self-distillation result, one of the paper's most conceptually surprising findings, is demonstrated for a single distillation generation rather than the iterated, multi-round recursive setting where model-collapse effects are typically reported to compound in the literature this paper directly engages with; the paper's proposed explanation (mixing in real data prevents collapse) is a reasonable, testable hypothesis but is not itself verified under repeated self-distillation rounds within this paper.

What remains well supported after these caveats: for language models trained from scratch on a small, fixed pool of web tokens (in the 200M-1.7B token, 150M-1.4B parameter range actually tested), standard data-constrained recipes based on repeating data or scaling parameters without adequate regularization reliably overfit and eventually worsen loss; substantially heavier weight decay than standard practice restores clean, monotonic power-law scaling in this regime; and, within this same tested range, ensembling independently-trained models of fixed size reliably achieves a better compute-unconstrained loss floor than scaling a single model's parameter count, with most of that ensembling benefit recoverable in a single, much smaller deployed model via distillation.
