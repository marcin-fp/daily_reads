Citation: Holtzman, A., Buys, J., Du, L., Forbes, M., Choi, Y. The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration. ICLR 2020. https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751

## What this paper is about

A language model assigns a probability to every possible next token, and turning it into a text generator requires a decoding rule. For open-ended generation, such as continuing a web article or a story, the two obvious rules both fail. Choosing the most probable continuation (greedy decoding or beam search) produces bland text that falls into repetitive loops. Sampling directly from the model's full distribution produces text that drifts into incoherence. The authors argue that the first failure means maximizing likelihood is the wrong objective for open-ended text, because human-written text is not the most probable text under the model. They attribute the second failure to a long tail of individually unlikely tokens that together carry too much probability.

Their fix is nucleus (top-p) sampling. At each step, keep the smallest set of most probable tokens whose cumulative probability reaches a threshold p, renormalize, and sample from that set. Using GPT-2 Large to continue held-out WebText passages, they compare decoding methods with human text on perplexity, vocabulary distribution, Self-BLEU diversity, repetition rate, and HUSE, a combined human and statistical score. They report that nucleus sampling with p = 0.95 is the closest to human text overall.

## Extended summary

Setup. All experiments use GPT-2 Large, stated as 762M parameters in Section 4.1 (the Figure 1 caption says 774M for Large and 1542M for XL). The authors generate 5,000 passages, each conditioned on the initial paragraph (1 to 40 tokens) of a held-out WebText document from OpenAI's GPT-2 output dataset. A passage stops at an end-of-document token or at 200 tokens. The decoders compared are greedy, beam search (b = 16 in Table 1, with sweeps over width in the figures), stochastic beam search (it appears only as a Table 1 row and the text never describes it), pure sampling, temperature sampling, top-k sampling with and without temperature 0.7 (the combination the authors attribute to Radford et al. 2019 and Fan et al. 2018), and nucleus sampling.

Method. The top-p vocabulary V(p) is the smallest set of tokens whose summed probability is at least p. Probabilities inside V(p) are divided by that set's total mass p′, and every other token gets zero. Top-k differs only in where it cuts: the set always has k tokens, so the renormalizing mass "can vary wildly" from step to step. The authors argue that a fixed k is suboptimal because next-token distributions are sometimes flat across tens or hundreds of plausible tokens and sometimes concentrated on one or a few. A small k makes flat contexts bland. A large k admits inappropriate tokens in peaked contexts, and renormalization then raises their probability. Temperature below 1 moves mass away from the tail, but the cited prior work (Caccia et al. 2018; Hashimoto et al. 2019) finds that it buys quality at the cost of diversity.

Evaluation principle. The authors argue that a good decoder should produce text whose statistics are close to human text, not text that is as probable as possible. Table 1 (Human / Greedy / Beam b=16 / Stochastic Beam b=16 / Pure / t=0.9 / top-k 40 / top-k 640 / top-k 40 with t=0.7 / nucleus p=0.95):

- Perplexity of the generated text under the generating model: 12.38 / 1.50 / 1.48 / 19.20 / 22.73 / 10.25 / 6.88 / 13.82 / 3.48 / 13.13.
- Self-BLEU4 (lower means more diverse): 0.31 / 0.50 / 0.44 / 0.28 / 0.28 / 0.35 / 0.39 / 0.32 / 0.44 / 0.32.
- Zipf coefficient: 0.93 / 1.00 / 0.94 / 0.91 / 0.93 / 0.96 / 0.96 / 0.96 / 1.00 / 0.95.
- Repetition percent: 0.28 / 73.66 / 28.94 / 0.32 / 0.22 / 0.66 / 0.78 / 0.28 / 8.86 / 0.36.
- HUSE (higher is better): not reported for human, greedy, beam, or stochastic beam; 0.67 / 0.79 / 0.19 / 0.94 / 0.08 / 0.97 for the six sampling configurations.

The Table 1 caption calls these "selected parameters" and does not say how they were selected. Self-BLEU uses 1,000 sampled generations, each scored against all 4,999 others. The Figure 8 caption says Self-BLEU was computed on unconditional generations, while the main setup is conditional. A generation counts as repetitive when a phrase of at least two tokens repeats at least three times at the end of the generation, within the first 200 tokens.

HUSE. HUSE trains a discriminator between human and model text on two features: the language model's probability and human judgments of typicality. Each method has 200 generations, each annotated by 20 different annotators, giving 4,000 annotations per decoding scheme. The discriminator is a KNN classifier with k = 13, which the authors chose because it gave higher discrimination accuracy. Applied directly, HUSE gave top-k and nucleus sampling scores near 0, because truncation assigns zero probability to most tokens, even though humans favored those methods. As a proxy, the generations used for HUSE under top-k and nucleus were drawn from an interpolation of the truncated distribution with the original distribution at mass 0.1. Nucleus sampling scored highest (0.97), with top-k second (0.94 at k = 640).

Other findings:

- Search error does not explain maximization's failure (Section 4.3). On average the model assigns lower per-token probability to human text than to beam-search output. Human text rarely stays in the high-probability zone for several consecutive steps, and it does not fall into the repetition loops the model rates as likely.
- Repetition feeds on itself (Figure 4). The probability of a repeated phrase, illustrated with "I don't know." repeated 200 times, rises with each repetition. The authors say this held "for the vast majority of phrases we tested", including randomly sampled phrases, but give no count.
- Beam search looks less repetitive at larger widths, but Appendix A attributes this to average length falling linearly with beam width while the number of distinct trigrams stays constant and far below the human level. The Figure 1 caption adds that at b ≥ 64 both GPT-2 Large and XL prefer to stop immediately after the context.
- Pure sampling has the Zipf coefficient closest to human, followed by nucleus sampling. Pure sampling slightly overuses rare words, which the authors suggest is one reason its perplexity exceeds human perplexity. They read that higher-than-human perplexity as the model "confusing itself" by sampling too many unlikely tokens.
- Settings then in common use (t in [0.5, 1], k in [1, 100]) show high self-similarity, while p in [0.9, 1) matches human Self-BLEU. Every stochastic method becomes repetitive when its parameter over-truncates, and temperatures below 0.9 "severely increase repetition". Reaching human-like Self-BLEU and Zipf statistics requires very high k or t, which the authors say produces unnaturally high perplexity and incoherence. From this they conclude that "only Nucleus Sampling satisfies all the distributional criteria".
- In the qualitative examples (Figure 3 and Appendix B), beam search loops, pure sampling invents words, top-k with t = 0.7 devolves into repetition, and nucleus output is described as imperfect: it confuses whales with birds.

Author caveats and conjectures. The authors conjecture that human text not being the most probable text is an intrinsic property of language, citing Grice's maxims (speakers avoid stating the obvious). On that basis they argue that larger models or better architectures trained with per-word objectives are unlikely to fix the problem. This is offered as a conjecture and is not tested. They say statistical metrics cannot measure coherence, which is why they add human evaluation. They restrict the claims to open-ended generation and note that directed tasks such as translation show milder versions of the same effect with large beams or exact inference. They describe training-time fixes such as unlikelihood training (Welleck et al. 2020) and diverse beam search as complementary. Future work would characterize the model's region of confidence dynamically and add a semantic utility function. Code and all generations are released.

## Learnings

For open-ended generation, a model's own likelihood is a poor quality score. GPT-2 Large gave its maximum-probability outputs a perplexity near 1.5 against 12.4 for human text, and greedy output ended in a loop 74 percent of the time. The workable target is to match human text statistics, not to maximize probability. The caveat is scope: this is continuation of web text by a 2019 base model, and the authors themselves say directed tasks behave differently.

A candidate set sized by cumulative probability adapts to how peaked each next-token distribution is, where a fixed count cannot. In the paper's own Table 1, however, a large fixed k (640) came close to nucleus sampling on every axis. The durable lesson is "truncate the tail, and check several human statistics at once", more than "p beats k".

Any one-parameter decoding family can be tuned to hit a single scalar statistic such as perplexity. The informative test is whether one setting matches several statistics at once and also survives human judgment. Likelihood-based evaluations like HUSE also break on truncated distributions, which assign zero probability to most tokens, so the evaluation itself has to be patched.

A repeated phrase makes further repetition more likely, which creates a positive feedback loop. Any pipeline that feeds a model its own output back as context inherits this pull toward loops.

## Field context

The authors place the work in a 2018 to 2019 line of open-ended generation research. Prior studies had found that maximization-based decoding yields grammatical but low-diversity text (Kulikov et al. 2019; Holtzman et al. 2018; Fan et al. 2018). Top-k sampling had been adopted by Fan et al. (2018), Holtzman et al. (2018), and GPT-2 (Radford et al. 2019), and temperature sampling has a long history going back to Ackley et al. (1985). They cite results that text GANs fail to beat language models once quality and diversity are judged jointly (Caccia et al. 2018; Tevet et al. 2019; Semeniuta et al. 2018). For directed generation, they cite quality losses with large beams in translation (Koehn and Knowles 2017) and with exact inference (Stahlberg and Byrne 2019). They present unlikelihood training (Welleck et al. 2020) as a training-time complement and borrow their metrics from Hashimoto et al. (2019) for HUSE and Zhu et al. (2018) for Self-BLEU. The timeline matters: v1 appeared about two months after the GPT-2 release that the introduction cites, and v2 accompanied ICLR 2020.

What follows is my inference from other reviews in this corpus, not the authors' framing. This is the source of the top-p knob that later papers treat as a default setting. reviews/2402.07043.md runs its Llama2 experiments with top-p 0.9 and temperature 0.9 and argues that exactly this kind of truncation cuts the tail of Zipf-distributed data, which harms models later trained on the generated text. The two results fit together, because they optimize different things. Truncation improves the quality of each individual sample here, and the same truncation removes coverage there when samples are recycled as training data. reviews/2504.09389.md reports that raising temperature increases originality while eventually degrading quality, which matches this paper's picture of high temperature trading coherence for diversity, though it uses a different metric. reviews/2210.02410.md likewise finds plain beam search the least diverse decoder in a captioning comparison. Several later reviews locate diversity loss upstream of decoding, in post-training or preference data: reviews/2510.01171.md, reviews/2605.26492.md, and reviews/2605.27878.md. This paper diagnoses a base model plus a decoding rule. A truncation rule can only remove probability mass, so it cannot restore modes that post-training has already collapsed. reviews/24semant.md is an example of nucleus and top-k sampling used routinely as the sampling front end of a later method.

## Critical discussion

The case against maximization-based decoding is strong. The effects in Table 1 are large: greedy output ends in a loop 73.66 percent of the time against 0.28 percent for human text, and beam perplexity is 1.48 against 12.38. The qualitative examples agree with the numbers, and Appendix A handles the obvious confound in the beam-width trend by showing that the apparent drop in repetition comes from shorter outputs. The argument against search error is also sound in its core. The model assigns higher per-token probability to beam output than to human text, so a better search for high-probability text would move further from human text, not closer. This is the most secure result in the paper.

The claim that nucleus sampling is "currently the best available decoding strategy", and that "only Nucleus Sampling satisfies all the distributional criteria", goes beyond what Table 1 shows. Top-k with k = 640 matches the human repetition rate exactly (0.28, against 0.36 for nucleus) and ties nucleus on Self-BLEU (0.32). It is slightly further from human on perplexity (13.82 against 13.13, human 12.38) and on the Zipf coefficient (0.96 against 0.95, human 0.93), and its HUSE is 0.94 against 0.97. The text gives two reasons for setting high-k top-k aside. One is that k above 100 is "rarely used in practice", which is a statement about practice rather than quality. The other is that high k produces "high variance in likelihood, hinting at" incoherence, which the text does not quantify and which rests on figures I could not assess. The design argument about flat and peaked distributions is illustrated (Figure 5) but not measured. For example, no statistics are reported on how nucleus size varies across steps. What the evidence clearly shows is that nucleus sampling beats the settings in common use at the time: top-k 40 has HUSE 0.19, and top-k 40 with t = 0.7 has HUSE 0.08 and 8.86 percent repetition. Its margin over a well-tuned top-k is small and unquantified.

The HUSE comparison has two measurement problems. First, for top-k and nucleus the generations annotated for HUSE came from a mixture that puts mass 0.1 back on the untruncated distribution. The HUSE winner is therefore a smoothed variant that readmits some of the tail the paper blames for incoherence, not the method as defined in Section 3.1. Pure sampling and temperature sampling were scored without this adjustment, so the HUSE column is not like-for-like across rows, and greedy, beam, and stochastic beam get no HUSE at all. Second, there is no uncertainty anywhere. HUSE rests on 200 generations per method with no confidence interval, the KNN neighborhood size was chosen for higher discrimination accuracy on the evaluation data, and no Table 1 metric carries a variance or a seed spread. The gap between 0.97 and 0.94 cannot be read as a real difference from what is reported.

The distributional evidence also has a built-in weakness that the authors partly acknowledge. The Figure 6 caption notes that sampling, top-k, and nucleus can all be calibrated to human perplexity, so "closest perplexity" alone cannot discriminate between methods. The informative evidence is joint closeness across metrics at one setting, and there nucleus and top-k 640 are close to each other. The unreliable-tail mechanism is plausible, but it is inferred from pure sampling's higher-than-human perplexity and its slight overuse of rare words. The paper never directly measures how much aggregate mass the tail carries per step, and it runs no ablation that isolates tail tokens as the cause of incoherence. Perplexity is also computed by the same model that generates, so it measures agreement with the model's own distribution, not coherence as a reader would judge it.

The scope is narrow relative to the headline. One model (GPT-2 Large) and one domain (WebText continuation) are tested. "Long-form" here means at most 200 tokens after a context of 1 to 40 tokens, and coherence is measured only indirectly, through perplexity and typicality ratings. The Grice-based claim that scale will not fix degeneration is labeled a conjecture, and nothing in the paper tests it across model sizes beyond a caption remark about GPT-2 XL at large beam widths. Several details are missing or inconsistent:

- Stochastic beam search appears in Table 1 with near-human numbers (perplexity 19.20, Self-BLEU 0.28, repetition 0.32) but is never described and has no HUSE.
- The model size is given as 762M in the text and 774M in a caption.
- Self-BLEU is reported on unconditional generations, while the main setup is conditional.
- The typicality annotation protocol is not described in the text.
- The repetition feedback loop rests on Figure 4 and an uncounted "vast majority of phrases we tested".

What remains supported: for open-ended continuation with this class of model, likelihood-maximizing decoding degenerates badly, and the model prefers that degenerate text to human text, so better search will not fix it. Untruncated sampling drifts above human perplexity. Truncating the low-probability tail with a cutoff that adapts to the distribution produces text that matches human statistics on several axes at once, and it scored best on HUSE in this setup, with the interpolation caveat above. Nucleus sampling is clearly better than the low-k and low-temperature defaults of its time. The narrower claim that it beats a well-tuned top-k is plausible but not established by this paper.

## Relevance to us

For B16 (diversity-preserving scientific generation), this is the decoding-level baseline. Any intervention we claim preserves diversity in hypothesis or conjecture generation should be compared against a tuned truncation sampler at matched budget, which is a B01-style fairness point. It should report several distributional statistics jointly rather than a single diversity score, and the paper's own template (match the reference distribution on several axes, then add human judgment) transfers directly. The finding that model likelihood prefers generic, repetitive text also warns against ranking candidate conjectures by the model's own probability or confidence, because that ranking would favor the "generically safe" themes CONTEXT worries about. Read alongside reviews/2402.07043.md, it adds a second caution for B21-style synthetic data loops: the truncation that makes each sample look better also cuts the tail of whatever we later train on. The limits are real. This is a 2019 base model on web text with no scientific content, and for instruction-tuned models the main diversity loss appears upstream of decoding, where truncation cannot help.
