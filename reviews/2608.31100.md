## What this paper is about

Most agent benchmarks test a model as a frozen policy: how good is it right now? S3Gym instead asks whether an agent can get better by using its own experience — trying things out (Self-Testing), deciding for itself which of its own actions were good or bad without being told the ground truth (Self-Judging), and turning that judgment into improved future behavior (Self-Improvement). It does this across seven text-based games with programmatically verifiable outcomes, comparing three ways of incorporating experience (raw history in context, a compressed natural-language summary, or actual parameter fine-tuning), while deliberately hiding the environment's real reward from the agent during exploration so the paper can separately measure whether the agent's own self-assessment is accurate and whether accurate self-assessment actually helps.

## Extended summary

**Design.** Each self-improvement cycle has an Exploration Phase (permissive game configurations; the agent acts, and jointly emits a self-judged score for its own action, without seeing the environment's true verifier-computed reward) followed by a held-out Evaluation Phase (stricter configurations, disjoint seeds, and no evaluation trajectories fed back into experience). The environment's real reward is retained by the benchmark for later analysis but withheld from the agent throughout exploration, which is what lets the authors compare model self-judgment against ground truth without contaminating the experiment. Three experience-incorporation pathways are compared under matched seeds, budgets, and decoding: History ICL (score-annotated trajectories appended directly to context, up to a 100,000-token limit), Summary Memory (the model compresses judged trajectories into retained strategies, mistakes to avoid, and concrete next-cycle directions), and parameter Training (high-scoring actions become SFT positives, low-scoring ones filtered or corrected, then applied via a parameter update — treated as an auxiliary pathway "because it introduces optimization variables absent from context-level improvement").

**Games.** Seven text-based games (Chess, Minesweeper, Nullify, Tetris, Snake, Plants-vs-Zombies, Trust Evolution) chosen to span latent-rule induction, constraint satisfaction, numerical transformation, spatial planning, resource allocation, survival, and multi-agent strategy, each with a distinct, more permissive exploration configuration and a stricter evaluation configuration (e.g., Chess exploration uses 12 pieces on a 15x15 board, evaluation uses 22 pieces on the same board; Minesweeper exploration grants two extra lives, evaluation ends on the first mine).

**Context-level results (7 proprietary models: GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, o3-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash/Pro, GPT-5.5, Gemini-3.5-Flash).** Self-improvement potential depends jointly on model, pathway, and game — no single model or pathway dominates everywhere. Summary Memory is not uniformly better than raw History ICL: it substantially helps models that struggle to use long raw trajectories directly (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Flash's Minesweeper AUC+ rises from 0.000 to 7.794, its PvZ AUC+ from 24.4 to 238.5 under summarization), but it can also discard state-specific evidence needed for reactive, detail-dependent games (GPT-5.5's PvZ AUC+ falls from 548.5 under History ICL to 33.2 under Summary Memory; Gemini-3.5-Flash's Trust Evolution AUC+ falls from 200.0 to 55.1). The pattern the authors draw out: summaries help when experience compresses into a stable, reusable policy rule (Nullify's arithmetic planning, Trust Evolution's opponent-conditioned strategy), and hurt when success depends on reconstructing precise current-state details (Minesweeper's exact board geometry, PvZ's lane timing and sun budget, Snake's exact obstacle layout).

**Parameter training (Qwen3-8B only, 20 checkpoints).** Effects are highly uneven: substantial and sustained improvement on Trust Evolution (0 to a max of 30, above baseline at 18 of 19 updated checkpoints); limited or transient gains on Chess and Snake; no measurable improvement at all on Minesweeper, Nullify, or Tetris (flat at zero throughout); and severe, persistent negative transfer on Plants-vs-Zombies (23 at baseline, collapsing to 6 at every subsequent checkpoint) — direct evidence that internalizing self-judged exploration trajectories via fine-tuning can overwrite previously effective behavior rather than only adding to it.

**Is Self-Judging reliable? (RQ2, 98 runs, 116,117 transitions).** Step-level event agreement between the model's self-score and the environment's actual reward ranges from near-random (0.496 on Chess) to fairly high (0.881 on PvZ), but high agreement doesn't imply good calibration: PvZ has the highest event agreement and simultaneously the worst normalized mean absolute error (0.882), meaning the model can correctly sense whether an action was locally good or bad while badly misjudging by how much. Chess and Trust Evolution are the clearest failure cases (near-random agreement plus high over-confidence), which the authors attribute to rewards that depend on hidden dynamics, opponent behavior, or exact multi-object state prediction rather than an immediately observable local consequence.

**Does accurate self-judgment predict actual improvement? (also RQ2).** Essentially no: blockwise correlation between judgment quality (event agreement or calibration error) and the subsequent evaluation-checkpoint score change is close to zero across the whole benchmark (ρ(agreement, gain) = -0.010; ρ(-error, gain) = -0.018), and the gap in average score gain between the best- and worst-judgment-quality tertiles is likewise small and slightly negative. A parallel run-level analysis using a normalized above-baseline-area metric finds only weak correlations (Pearson r = -0.23, Spearman ρ = -0.11) between event agreement and sustained improvement. The paper's own reading: recognizing good and bad actions locally is necessary but not sufficient — an agent also has to turn that recognition into a reusable, executable rule, and that second step is where most of the observed failures concentrate (illustrated with paired successful/failed summary examples: a summary that specifies a concrete arithmetic planning procedure or opponent-conditioned rule succeeds, while a "reasonable but too coarse" high-level reminder that doesn't resolve exact state details fails).

## Learnings

The experimental design choice to withhold the true verifier reward from the agent during exploration while still recording it for later analysis is a clean, reusable pattern for any study of self-supervised or self-judged improvement: it lets you separately answer "is the agent's own judgment accurate" and "does accurate judgment actually produce improvement" as two distinct questions, rather than conflating them into a single end-to-end improvement number that can't distinguish a judgment failure from a judgment-to-behavior translation failure.

The near-zero correlation between self-judgment accuracy and subsequent improvement (Section 6.2) is a specific, well-quantified instance of a more general and important caution for self-improving-agent designs: accurate local credit assignment is not sufficient for improvement, and a system could have a perfectly calibrated critic yet still fail to translate that signal into better future behavior if it cannot compress the recognized pattern into an executable rule.

The Summary-Memory-versus-History-ICL split (compact-rule tasks favor summarization; state-precision tasks favor raw history) is a concrete, generalizable design heuristic for choosing between memory-compression strategies in any agent system: the right question is not "does summarization help" in the abstract, but whether the task's success condition can be captured by a stable policy rule independent of the exact current state, or whether it inherently requires reconstructing precise state details that a natural-language summary would discard.

## Verification

Central and carefully layered: the reward-withholding design is itself a verification mechanism (it prevents the ground-truth signal from leaking into the agent's judgments during exploration), Self-Judging reliability is measured against the environment's own executable verifier rather than against another LLM judge, and the judgment-improvement coupling analysis uses two independent statistical approaches (blockwise correlation with tertile gaps, and a separate run-level Pearson/Spearman analysis) that reach the same conclusion, which is a meaningful form of internal replication for a null/negative result. The qualitative case studies (paired successful and failed summaries with the specific trajectory evidence and outcome) ground the quantitative Table 7 pattern in concrete, checkable examples rather than leaving the game-level trend as a bare statistic. What is not verified to the same standard: the parameter-training pathway (RQ1), the paper's most consequential and highest-stakes finding (severe negative transfer on PvZ, no improvement on three of seven games), rests on a single model (Qwen3-8B) with no reported second model or training seed to check whether the specific pattern of which games succeed or fail would replicate.

## Field context

S3Gym extends the KORGym game-based evaluation paradigm (the same lead author's own prior benchmark) from measuring static LLM reasoning capability to measuring experience-driven self-improvement specifically, and positions itself against a wave of concurrent 2026 adaptive-agent benchmarks it tabulates directly (Table 1): PostTrainBench (automated post-training pipelines on held-out tasks), SEA-Eval, SEAGym, PAST-Bench, ContinualSkillBench, FinEvo-Bench, Social Gym, and AI4AIBench. Its stated differentiator is coupling explicit Self-Judging (comparing model-generated scores against verifier ground truth) with Self-Testing under executable verification, which none of the compared benchmarks do simultaneously per the paper's own comparison table. Within this batch, S3Gym's parameter-training pathway (RQ1: converting self-judged exploration trajectories into SFT updates, with an explicit shared "adoption" concern about whether an update should be trusted) sits close to BCIT's problem ([2608.26730.md](2608.26730.md), reviewed earlier in this batch): both papers examine what happens when an autonomous system converts its own accumulated interaction experience into a parameter update, though BCIT proposes and evaluates a specific gating mechanism to prevent bad updates from being applied, while S3Gym instead measures, without any such gate, how often naive training on self-judged experience produces exactly the kind of negative transfer (Plants-vs-Zombies) that a mechanism like BCIT's non-compensable hard-conflict veto is designed to catch — the two papers' findings are complementary rather than overlapping, with S3Gym providing an independent empirical motivation for why a gating mechanism like BCIT's might be needed.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central methodological contribution — separating self-judgment accuracy from improvement outcome via reward-withholding, then testing whether the two are actually correlated — is executed carefully and yields a genuinely informative negative result (near-zero judgment-improvement coupling) that is corroborated by two independent analyses (blockwise and run-level) rather than resting on one statistic. The qualitative summary case studies meaningfully support the quantitative game-level pattern (compact-rule tasks favor summarization) by showing concretely what a successful versus failed summary looks like, which is more informative than the aggregate ∆NABA numbers alone.

Two things temper how far the results can be generalized. First, the parameter-training pathway — arguably the paper's most striking finding, since severe negative transfer on Plants-vs-Zombies and zero improvement on three of seven games are strong, specific claims about a real failure mode — is evaluated on a single model (Qwen3-8B), with no second model or repeated training run to establish whether this particular pattern of which games succeed, stagnate, or degrade is a property of training-based self-improvement in general or an artifact of this one model's specific behavior and hyperparameters (`narrow-evaluation`). Second, the main context-level evaluation uses only 3 strict-mode episodes per checkpoint, and several of the games the paper itself identifies as having "sparse or highly discrete scores" (Chess, Minesweeper, Nullify) are exactly the ones where a 3-episode sample would be most susceptible to noise; no confidence intervals, variance, or repeated-seed estimates are reported for the Avg./Max./AUC+ metrics in the main results table, so some of the finer-grained cross-model rankings discussed in the text (e.g., which model "leads" a specific brittle game) should be read as suggestive rather than statistically established (`missing-uncertainty`).

What remains well supported after these caveats: the core qualitative claims — that self-improvement potential depends jointly on model, pathway, and task structure rather than being a fixed property of model capability; that Summary Memory is a selective rather than unconditional improvement over raw history; and that accurate self-judgment does not by itself predict improvement — are each backed by either a large-sample statistical analysis (the RQ2 judgment-coupling result, N=116,117 transitions) or a consistent, multi-game qualitative pattern with concrete supporting examples (the History-vs-Summary split), rather than resting on a single anecdote or a small-sample point estimate. The specific magnitude of gains or losses for any one model-game-pathway cell, especially in the single-model training pathway, is better read as a documented case than as an established general rate.
