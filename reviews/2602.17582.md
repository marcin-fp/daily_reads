## What this paper is about

This is a community whitepaper, produced and endorsed by the American Physical Society's Division of Particles and Fields, arguing that experimental particle physics — a field that already generates hundreds of petabytes of data annually and throws away more than 99% of it at the detector level — is uniquely positioned to benefit from a deep, end-to-end integration of AI across the entire lifecycle of an experiment: designing accelerators and detectors, deciding in real time what data to keep, running facilities with less human intervention, and turning raw data into physics results far faster than today's years-long analysis cycles. Rather than proposing a specific technical method, the paper proposes a funding and organizational structure — a new national-scale, DOE/NSF/university/industry collaboration on the order of 120+ full-time-equivalent staff — to pursue four "Grand Challenges" it defines, aimed both at informing U.S. funding agencies and policymakers and at giving the field's next generation of major facilities (HL-LHC, DUNE, the EIC, and eventually a Higgs factory or muon collider) a coordinated path to becoming "AI-native."

## Extended summary

The paper opens by grounding its motivation in the community's existing scientific priorities (the 2023 P5 report and the 2023 Nuclear Science Long Range Plan), arguing that flagship and future facilities — HL-LHC, DUNE, the EIC, Belle-II/LHCb, Mu2e, dark-matter direct-detection experiments, IceCube-Gen2, ALICE 3, and eventually a Higgs factory or 10 TeV-scale collider — each face specific data-volume, latency, or precision bottlenecks that AI methods could address. It then organizes its vision around four "Grand Challenges" spanning the experimental lifecycle: (1) Accelerated Experimental Design, using differentiable simulation and active learning to co-optimize accelerators, detectors, and even project cost/schedule estimates (illustrated with DUNE Phase II, FCC-ee, and a proposed muon collider as target cases); (2) Intelligent Sensing and Instrumentation, moving away from traditional hardware triggers (which the paper says discard roughly 99.99% of raw data) toward AI-driven, trigger-less continuous readout that decides what to keep in real time, with particular emphasis on time-critical multi-messenger astrophysics alerts; (3) Autonomous Experiments, automating detector/facility operations, calibration, and data-quality monitoring to reduce the hundreds of person-hours currently required, with specific illustrative targets of up to 50% less downtime, 10x faster calibration cycles, and roughly a 10x reduction in required personnel effort for calibration/DQ tasks; and (4) From Data to Discovery, using multimodal foundation models, AI-accelerated simulation and reconstruction, neural simulation-based inference, and agentic workflows to compress today's multi-year analysis cycles by a targeted factor of 100-1000x and enable searches across much larger theoretical parameter spaces than hand-picked benchmark scenarios allow.

The paper states these Grand Challenges were synthesized from a structured community-input process: more than 100 contributions from roughly 150 individuals across 50 institutions (7 national labs, 43 universities), collected between December 2025 and January 2026 via APS DPF-organized forums, explicitly described as "a sampling of the field" rather than an exhaustive survey. A cross-cutting section (3.5) names recurring themes that didn't map cleanly onto the four Grand Challenges: neutrino interaction modeling via simulation-based inference, SMEFT/EFT and parton-distribution-function pipelines linking theory and experiment, GPU-accelerated Monte Carlo event generation, federated AI-ready Open Data with common representations, and AI-assisted detector/materials co-design.

The organizational proposal (Section 4) asks for a jointly-managed core effort of at least 120 full-time-equivalents over 5 years (with roughly double that number of total contributors), modeled explicitly on existing U.S. LHC operations programs that each manage on the order of $40M/year jointly funded by DOE and NSF, plus private-foundation and industry partnerships. The paper is explicit that this scale estimate is "an estimated scale, reflecting the scope and ambition of the Grand Challenges, rather than a detailed bottom-up effort calculation." It draws on the field's decades of experience running large multi-institution collaborations (U.S. CMS: 2 DOE labs + 51 universities; U.S. ATLAS: 5 DOE labs + 42 universities; U.S. ALICE: 2 DOE labs + 11 universities) and cites existing national-scale AI/software efforts (HEP-CCE, IRIS-HEP, A3D3, IAIFI) as organizational precedents. Section 5 outlines a workforce-development component tied to the DOE's "Genesis Mission" goal of training 100,000 scientists over the next decade, estimating the proposed collaboration could contribute roughly 2,100 PhDs and 20,000 undergraduates toward that target. Sections 6-7 (AI Technologies for HEP; Advanced AI Cyberinfrastructure, not detailed in this summary) describe the shared technical building blocks — multimodal foundation models, differentiable/fast simulation, inference-as-a-service, FAIR-aligned federated data, and integration with DOE Leadership Class Facilities and the emerging "American Science Cloud" — needed to support the four Grand Challenges across institutions. The Conclusion explicitly frames the document as "the foundation of a living community vision, rather than a static document," intended for periodic revision, and names several topics (sustainability, responsible AI use, international engagement, outreach) it says are out of scope for this particular document.

## Verification

None, in the sense the paper-review skill applies to empirical claims. The paper reports no experiments and cites no pilot studies within its own text to substantiate its headline quantitative targets (e.g., "up to 50% less downtime, 10x faster calibration cycles," "reduce ... by factors of 100-1000"); these are stated as goals the proposed collaboration would pursue, not as measured outcomes of any existing deployment described in the document. The one process the paper does describe with some rigor is its own community-input methodology (Section 3's account of soliciting and aggregating more than 100 contributions from roughly 150 individuals across 50 institutions over a defined December 2025-January 2026 window), which is transparently scoped and explicitly caveated as "a sampling of the field" rather than a comprehensive survey — an appropriately modest characterization of what that process can and cannot establish.

## Field context

The paper positions itself explicitly as a synthesis and extension of several recent community reports it names directly: the Snowmass 2021 Computational Frontier Machine Learning topical group report, the A3D3 institute's 2023 "AI to Accelerate Science and Engineering Discovery" workshop report, IAIFI's report on generative AI in the physical sciences, and the 2025 NSF workshop report on the future of AI and the mathematical/physical sciences — situating this whitepaper as the particle-physics-specific, funding-oriented successor to that broader cross-disciplinary discussion, and grounding its science priorities directly in the community's existing P5 (Particle Physics Project Prioritization Panel) and Nuclear Science Long Range Plan processes rather than proposing a new scientific agenda independent of those. Within this corpus, the paper's four-Grand-Challenge structure (design, sensing, operations, analysis) is a domain-specific instance of the same "AI across the full research/experimental lifecycle" framing this batch's other survey (2605.18661, on AI for academic research broadly) applies to the general research process — both documents organize AI opportunities by lifecycle stage and both flag verification/trust as the central open problem, though this physics whitepaper's version of that concern (Section 3.4's emphasis on uncertainty quantification, interpretability, and avoiding hallucinated LLM operations-assistant outputs; the explicit need for "correctness" verification in agentic analysis workflows) is stated as a design requirement to be engineered rather than as an empirically-demonstrated failure mode, since — unlike 2605.18661's survey of already-published AI-for-research systems — this document proposes infrastructure that does not yet exist at the scale described.

## Critical discussion

As a funding-advocacy and roadmap document rather than a study reporting results, the appropriate critical question is not whether its evidence supports its claims (it presents no experimental evidence of its own to evaluate) but whether its quantitative targets and organizational scale estimates are grounded, and whether the document is transparent about the difference between the two. On the second question the paper is commendably direct in places: it states outright that its 120-FTE staffing estimate is "an estimated scale ... rather than a detailed bottom-up effort calculation," and it distinguishes its four Grand Challenges (synthesized from community input) from an "exhaustive review of ongoing R&D," which the Audience note at the top of the document explicitly disclaims. This kind of self-aware scoping is a genuine strength relative to advocacy documents that present aspirational figures without any such caveat.

On the first question, several of the document's most attention-grabbing numbers are presented with more specificity than the surrounding text can support with in-document evidence: "up to 50% less downtime, 10x faster calibration cycles, ... reducing the required personnel effort for calibration and data quality tasks by a factor of ten" (Section 3.3), and "reduce the time required to design, refine, and evaluate an analysis by factors of 100-1000" (Section 3.4) are stated as targets of the proposed program without citing a specific pilot deployment, prototype result, or prior published benchmark within this document that achieved comparable gains at comparable scale — they read as aspirational planning figures (reasonable inputs for a funding proposal) rather than as extrapolations from demonstrated evidence, and a reader should not mistake the precision of the stated percentages for an evidentiary basis the document does not provide here. This is consistent with the genre (a whitepaper aimed at funding agencies and policymakers, per its own stated Audience section) rather than a flaw specific to this document, but it does mean the numbers should be read as negotiating positions and illustrative targets rather than validated projections.

A second, related point: the community-input process that shaped the four Grand Challenges, while transparently described, is self-selected and modestly sized relative to the field it claims to represent (roughly 150 individuals from 50 institutions, out of a field the document itself says spans "nearly every U.S. Ph.D.-granting university" and involvement from "hundreds of experts" at individual large detectors) — the paper's own framing ("a sampling of the field," noting "a longer period for input would have resulted in many more contributions") is appropriately modest about this limitation, and the prioritization into exactly four Grand Challenges necessarily compresses a much broader and more heterogeneous set of community proposals (acknowledged directly in Section 3.5's discussion of cross-cutting themes that didn't fit the four-challenge framing) into a smaller, more fundable-sounding narrative structure — a reasonable and disclosed simplification for a document whose explicit purpose is to be legible to funding agencies, but one that means the four Grand Challenges should be read as an organizing narrative choice rather than as an empirically-derived taxonomy of the field's AI needs.

What remains well supported, on its own terms as a policy and organizational document rather than an empirical one: the paper's account of the field's existing large-scale collaborative infrastructure (U.S. CMS, ATLAS, ALICE operations programs; HEP-CCE, IRIS-HEP, A3D3, IAIFI as organizational precedents) is a factual description of existing, verifiable institutional arrangements rather than a projection, and provides a genuinely relevant precedent for the proposed governance model; the diagnosis of the underlying data-scale problem motivating the whole document (multi-exabyte datasets, sub-1%-retention triggering, years-long analysis cycles) is a well-established and uncontroversial characterization of the field rather than a contested empirical claim; and the paper's explicit framing of itself as "a living community vision" requiring "periodic updates" rather than a finished technical proposal is an honest characterization of the document's actual epistemic status.
