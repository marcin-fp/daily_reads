## What this paper is about

When people ask whether a language model can produce genuinely novel output, most existing measurements capture only one half of the question: either how different the text is from the model's training data, or how good it seems to a human or LLM judge, but not both together. A model can produce text that is highly original yet nonsensical, or text that is fluent and well-liked yet lifted nearly verbatim from something it memorized. This paper proposes measuring novelty as the harmonic mean of originality (the fraction of a generation's word sequences that never appeared in the model's own training data) and task-specific output quality, explicitly designed so a generation only scores well if it is both different from the training data and actually good. Applying this metric across three open-weight model families and three creative tasks, the paper traces which levers, model scale, post-training, improving the base model, or inference-time prompting tricks, actually move the needle on genuine novelty, and by which of the two ingredients.

## Extended summary

The paper's central methodological contribution is combining two previously separate measurement traditions. Originality is measured the standard way prior memorization-analysis work has done it: the fraction of higher-order n-grams (n=4, 5, 6) in a generation that do not appear anywhere in the model's own pre- and post-training corpora, computed using the WIMBD and Infinigram APIs, which index the actual training data (the Pile, Dolma, Dolmino, and various instruction/preference-tuning mixtures) for the specific open-data model families studied. Quality is measured via LLM-as-a-judge scoring against task-specific rubrics (0-5 scale), since large-scale human evaluation across all the paper's model/task/intervention combinations would be impractical. The paper explicitly argues that neither measure alone is sufficient: an originality-only metric (like the prior "Creativity Index") would rank rare-but-nonsensical text highly, while a quality-only metric (like Chatbot Arena-style human preference) risks a naive judge favoring fluent but memorized text without realizing it is copied. The two are combined via harmonic mean specifically because it penalizes either dimension being low, so a generation cannot compensate for poor quality with high originality or vice versa.

Three open-ended creative tasks are used: story completion (TinyStories, completing a story's opening line), poetry writing (CoPoet, generating a single line to a content/literary-device instruction), and creative tool use (MacGyver, proposing an unconventional but valid physical solution using given objects), each scored by a task-specific LLM-judge rubric. To validate the LLM-judge quality scores, the authors collected three human annotations each for 100 examples per task via Upwork (after a pilot-based screening process), finding inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's alpha) of 0.68 (CoPoet), 0.64 (MacGyver), and 0.59 (TinyStories), broadly consistent with reported agreement levels for creative tasks elsewhere in the literature. They then tested several candidate LLM judges (Claude 3.7/4 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o-mini, o3-mini, GPT-5-mini) under three prompting configurations (single run, in-context examples of human ratings, 5-sample averaging) against these human annotations via Spearman correlation, finding o3-mini with 5-sample averaging achieves the highest correlation (0.50 for CoPoet, 0.52 for both TinyStories and MacGyver), which is the configuration used for all subsequent experiments.

The main experiments evaluate base and instruction-tuned models from three families (OLMo 1B/7B; OLMo-2 1B/7B/13B/32B; Pythia 6.9B/12B and Pythia-Deduped 1B/2.8B/6.9B/12B, the latter trained 1.5 epochs versus Pythia's 1 epoch on a deduplicated version of the same corpus), comparing them against a "baseline" of the human-written references in each dataset (computed with the same originality/quality pipeline, treated as a reference point for "average human writing" rather than a gold standard, since the tasks are open-ended enough that many valid answers exist). Three consistent patterns emerge, each isolated to a specific one of the two novelty ingredients. First, scaling within a model family (1B to 7B) reliably increases novelty, but the improvement is driven mainly by higher output quality (e.g., +19% on TinyStories, +39% on MacGyver for OLMo-1B to 7B), not more originality, and the gains plateau at larger scales (7B to 32B shows mixed, smaller effects across tasks), though the top 10% of generations continue to improve with scale even when the average does not. Second, post-training (SFT+DPO for OLMo, SFT+DPO+RLVR for OLMo-2) reliably and substantially increases novelty over the corresponding base model at every scale tested, again primarily through higher quality, with a smaller, consistent boost to originality as well; the effect is strongest on CoPoet, which the authors attribute to that task's format closely matching typical instruction-tuning data. Third, and distinctly, improving the underlying base model at a fixed parameter scale (OLMo to OLMo-2 at 1B and 7B; Pythia to Pythia-Deduped at 6.9B and 12B) also increases novelty, but this time the improvement is driven by higher originality rather than quality, a genuinely different mechanism than either of the first two levers. The paper explicitly contrasts its finding that alignment (post-training) increases both originality and quality with a prior finding (Lu et al. 2024a) that RLHF reduces "creativity index," attributing the discrepancy to that prior work computing originality against a large general internet corpus rather than, as this paper does, the model's own specific training corpus.

The second half of the paper investigates whether novelty can be elicited at inference time without changing the model itself. Raising sampling temperature (tested from 0.5 to 2.0 on OLMo-7B and Pythia-12B) shows a U-shaped effect: originality climbs steadily with temperature, but quality eventually degrades enough that net novelty declines past some task-dependent optimal temperature, meaning temperature must be tuned per task and cannot be fixed at a high value to reliably chase novelty. Providing base models with in-context examples specifically selected for their own high novelty (from a held-out pool, top-10% novelty-scored) produces only small, inconsistent effects, a statistically significant improvement for OLMo-7B on two of three tasks but a small decrease for OLMo-2-7B on all three. For instruction-tuned models, two more elaborate prompting strategies, directly asking the model (via chain-of-thought) to produce rare and high-quality output, and "denial prompting" (iteratively extracting the high-level concepts used in prior generations via GPT-4o and explicitly telling the model not to reuse them across three rounds), each produce modest, inconsistent effects across tasks: both increase novelty somewhat on TinyStories and CoPoet by trading a quality decrease for a larger originality increase, but reduce or barely affect novelty on MacGyver (attributed to the technique producing degenerate outputs on that specific, more constrained reasoning task). The paper's overarching conclusion from this section is that no inference-time method tested comes close to matching the effect size of scale, post-training, or base-model improvements; all of them primarily just trade one ingredient of novelty for the other rather than genuinely expanding the underlying novelty frontier.

The Limitations appendix is candid about the metric's scope: n-gram originality cannot detect close paraphrases of memorized content that avoid exact n-gram overlap; LLM-judge quality scores are constrained to a coarse 1-5 integer scale limiting fine-grained comparison; and the analysis is restricted to open-data models specifically because computing originality requires an indexed, accessible training corpus, which frontier closed-weight models do not provide. The paper proposes a specific mitigation for this last limitation: since the metric only needs an aggregate unseen-n-gram fraction per generation, model providers could in principle compute and publish this statistic themselves using their own internal (proprietary) training-data index without ever exposing the underlying data, enabling cross-provider novelty comparison without requiring open data.

## Learnings

The paper's central, most transferable methodological contribution is demonstrating, with a concrete worked example (temperature scaling), why originality and quality must be measured and combined rather than either one alone: originality increases monotonically with sampling temperature, so an originality-only metric would report ever-increasing novelty as temperature rises, when in fact quality eventually collapses and net novelty (by the paper's own harmonic-mean measure) turns over and declines. This is a specific, quantified demonstration that a single-axis proxy metric can be systematically misleading exactly in the regime (aggressive sampling for "creativity") where a practitioner is most likely to reach for it, and is a directly reusable caution whenever a system is being tuned to maximize a proxy for a two-sided property like this.

A second, more mechanistically specific learning is that different levers for improving generation quality operate through genuinely different components of novelty, not interchangeably: scaling and post-training both raise novelty primarily by raising quality while leaving originality roughly flat, whereas improving the underlying base model architecture/training recipe at fixed scale raises novelty primarily by raising originality while quality moves comparatively little. This is a useful diagnostic distinction for anyone deciding how to invest limited resources toward more novel model output: the answer to "how do I get more original output" is not the same lever as "how do I get more usable output," and conflating them (as a single aggregate novelty number without this decomposition would) risks investing in the wrong intervention.

A third, more cautionary learning concerns the limits of prompting as a lever at all: across three distinct inference-time interventions (novel in-context examples, explicit novelty requests, iterative denial prompting), the paper finds consistently small and inconsistent effects relative to the model-level interventions (scale, post-training, base-model quality), with most gains in one dimension paid for by losses in the other rather than a genuine expansion of the achievable frontier. This is a specific, tested counterpoint to the intuitive assumption that clever prompting alone can substitute for a better underlying model when the goal is genuinely novel (not just superficially different) output.

## Verification

Verification of the paper's central measurement, LLM-judge quality scoring, is treated as a first-class methodological problem rather than an assumption: a real, paid, screened human-annotation study (with a pilot-based rejection process for low-quality annotators) is conducted specifically to validate that the LLM-judge scores track human judgment, and the paper reports the actual correlation values (Spearman, around 0.50-0.52 for the best-performing judge configuration) transparently rather than only asserting the judge is reliable. The paper also tests several distinct judge models and prompting configurations (single-run, in-context calibration, multi-sample averaging) specifically to find the best-validated setup rather than defaulting to whichever judge was most convenient, and explicitly reports the inter-annotator agreement among the human raters themselves (Krippendorff's alpha 0.59-0.68) as context for interpreting how much ceiling the LLM-judge correlation could realistically reach, since even human raters do not agree perfectly with each other on these inherently subjective creative-quality judgments. Statistical significance is checked directly for reported novelty differences via paired-samples t-tests rather than reporting raw percentage differences without a significance test, and the paper is careful to flag which specific comparisons do and do not clear the significance threshold rather than treating every reported delta as equally trustworthy. The originality half of the metric is verified against the actual, complete training corpora of the specific open-data models studied (rather than an approximate or partial proxy corpus), which the paper explicitly identifies as a methodological advantage over prior work using a general internet reference corpus, directly explaining a specific discrepancy with a previous paper's contrary finding about alignment and creativity rather than leaving the disagreement unexplained.

## Field context

This paper builds directly on, and explicitly extends, two established measurement traditions it distinguishes carefully: n-gram-based originality/memorization analysis (McCoy et al. 2023; Merrill et al. 2024; the Creativity Index of Lu et al. 2024a, which the paper identifies as its closest prior work and specifically improves on by adding a quality dimension) and LLM-as-judge-based creativity scoring inspired by the Torrance test tradition (Zhao et al. 2024; Chakrabarty et al. 2024), which the paper notes has been shown to diverge from expert human ratings specifically on originality judgments, motivating its choice to measure originality programmatically against training data rather than via LLM judgment at all. Within this corpus, the paper's central concern, distinguishing genuinely novel model output from confident, fluent regurgitation of memorized training content, is directly and practically relevant to [26mira] (Deep Principle's AI Scientist system, reviewed in an earlier batch), whose entire value proposition rests on an LLM-based agent squad generating "grounded, testable hypotheses" for scientific discovery; this paper's methodology offers a concrete, checkable way to ask whether such a system's generated hypotheses are actually original relative to its training corpus (including the scientific literature it has read) or are higher-quality restatements of existing ideas, a question 26mira's own evaluation does not directly address and this paper's framework is specifically built to answer. The paper's finding that post-training and base-model quality improvements shift the novelty frontier through different mechanisms (quality versus originality respectively) is also a useful, independently-derived data point for [2402.07043] (the model-collapse theory paper, reviewed in an earlier batch), whose central concern is how the statistical properties (specifically, the heavy-tailed structure) of AI-generated text degrade under recursive training; this paper's originality metric, the fraction of a generation's content that is genuinely unseen relative to training data, is essentially measuring a related but distinct property (how much a specific generation resembles previously-seen material) that would be a natural instrument for empirically tracking exactly the kind of tail-narrowing synthetic-data degradation that paper's theory predicts, though neither paper cites the other.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central methodological argument, that originality and quality must be measured jointly rather than separately, is convincingly demonstrated with a concrete, quantitative counterexample (the temperature-scaling U-curve) rather than only argued abstractly, and the three main findings about scale, post-training, and base-model improvement are each supported by a decomposition into which specific ingredient (originality or quality) drives the effect, a genuinely more informative level of analysis than reporting only the aggregate novelty number would provide. The human-validation study for the LLM-judge component is a real methodological strength relative to papers that simply assert an LLM judge is reliable without independent human comparison.

The clearest limitation, one directly quantifiable from the paper's own reported numbers, is that the LLM-judge quality score, half of the paper's central novelty metric, only moderately correlates with human ratings even in its best-validated configuration (Spearman correlation around 0.50-0.52), and the human raters themselves only moderately agree with each other (Krippendorff's alpha 0.59-0.68); together these numbers mean a meaningful fraction of the variance in the paper's quality measurements, and therefore in its downstream novelty conclusions, is not fully explained by either the automatic judge or by inter-human consensus on what counts as high-quality creative output. This does not invalidate the paper's directional findings, which are large and consistent enough (e.g., the scaling and post-training effects) to plausibly survive this level of measurement noise, but it does mean the more marginal, inconsistent findings (particularly the small, task-dependent effects of the inference-time prompting interventions in Section 4) should be read with real caution given how much closer those effect sizes are to the measurement's own validated correlation ceiling. A second, explicitly self-disclosed limitation is the paper's restriction to open-data models: because computing n-gram originality requires an accessible, indexed training corpus, the paper's specific quantitative findings about scale, post-training, and base-model effects on novelty have only been demonstrated for OLMo, OLMo-2, and Pythia, model families that lag meaningfully behind the frontier closed-weight models (GPT-4-class, Claude-class) that the paper's own motivating examples (scientific discovery, creative-writing assistance) most directly concern; the paper proposes a reasonable path to extending its method to those models (providers self-reporting aggregate originality statistics) but this remains a proposed future direction rather than something demonstrated in the paper itself, so the practical generalizability of the specific numerical findings to the models people actually use for the motivating use cases is, by the paper's own account, still an open question.

What remains well supported: originality and quality are genuinely separable, sometimes divergent properties of LLM-generated text that a single-axis metric can conflate in specific, demonstrable ways (the temperature U-curve being the clearest case); model scale, post-training, and improving the underlying base model each measurably shift the novelty frontier for the open-data model families tested, and do so through distinguishably different mechanisms (quality-driven versus originality-driven); and inference-time prompting interventions, across three distinct methods tested, produce meaningfully smaller and less consistent gains than any of these model-level interventions, a specific, comparative finding rather than only an assumed one.
