## What this paper is about

Real-world LLM agents rarely see their entire toolset at once — with enterprise MCP servers or large API catalogs, an agent typically retrieves only a relevant-looking subset of tools at each step, and that retrieval can itself be unreliable (missing the right tool, or handing back a broken, stale, or misleadingly-similar one). PlanBench-XL is a benchmark built specifically to stress-test this setting: 327 multi-step retail tasks spread across 1,665 tools, where an agent must iteratively retrieve tools it doesn't yet know exist, use their outputs as evidence to figure out what to retrieve next, and — in an optional "blocking" mode — cope with some of the tools it needs being silently broken, explicitly erroring, or replaced by something that merely looks similar. Across ten frontier LLMs, even the best model (GPT-5.4) drops from 51.90% accuracy with no blocking to 11.36% when only the hardest recovery path remains, and detailed trajectory analysis finds the dominant failure mode is not that agents fail to find the right tool, but that they drift off a productive path after partial progress and then fail to notice or return to a useful tool they had already retrieved.

## Extended summary

The benchmark is built from a datatype-grounded generation pipeline: an LLM (GPT-5.2, used as both the generator and the quality filter throughout) first proposes and filters a set of domain-specific "datatypes" (e.g., person_name, refund_status), then constructs tools by pairing input/output datatype sets and generating plausible tool functionality, names (using 5-10 aliases per datatype to mimic real-world naming inconsistency), and descriptions. A backend database is populated with concrete, non-inferrable values so that tool execution is genuinely necessary to obtain them. Tools are filtered to require deterministic dependency (no redundant inputs), tool-grounded information access (no pure-reasoning shortcuts a model could compute itself), domain realism, and non-trivial information gain. Each executable tool is paired with five "noisy" variants (deprecated, condition-limited, stale, unreliable, or non-authoritative) that are semantically similar but explicitly describe their own unreliability, yielding 925 noisy tools alongside 185 executable ones (1,665 tools total including additional structural variants). Queries are generated by first identifying solvable (initial-datatype, target-datatype) pairs via an explicit state-graph reachability check (code-based, not LLM-judged), then instantiating and verbalizing them into natural language, with ground-truth answers derived by executing one valid tool-call sequence; only queries whose shortest solution requires at least five distinct tool calls are retained (average solution length 5-9, average ~25 turns to solve in the easiest setting).

At runtime, an agent interacts through exactly three actions (retrieve, tool-call, answer) against an environment-maintained latent state tracking discovered tools and obtained datatypes. The retriever supports three modes aligned with "bi-directional anticipation": input-conditioned (forward, from available evidence), output-conditioned (backward, from the desired outcome), and input-output-conditioned retrieval. An optional retrieval-time blocking module identifies path-critical tools and, when they would be retrieved, silently substitutes them with an explicit-failure tool (returns an error), an implicit-failure tool (returns a plausible-looking but wrong value with no error signal), or a semantically-misleading tool (looks related but performs a different function) — while guaranteeing, by construction, that at least one alternative feasible solution path always remains.

Ten proprietary and open-source LLMs are evaluated (GPT-5.4, GPT-5.4-Mini, Gemini-3.1-Pro, Gemini-3.5-Flash, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, three Qwen3 sizes, two Llama-3 sizes) using seven metrics spanning task completion (Accuracy, Executed-Ground-Truth-Datatype Precision), exploration behavior (Average Turns, Mean Explored Datatypes, Search-to-Call Ratio), and execution quality (Invalid Tool Call Rate, Untrusted Input Rejection Rate). In the default (no-blocking) setting, results split sharply: Gemini-3.1-Pro leads at 77.06% accuracy and 91.47% EGT Precision, most other models stay below 60%, and the smallest open-source models (Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) score 0%. Exploration breadth (Mean Explored Datatypes) correlates strongly with accuracy (Pearson r=0.902) but is not sufficient by itself — Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct matches GPT-5.4's exploration breadth (19.20 vs. 20.65) yet trails badly in accuracy (18.96% vs. 51.90%) — and the relative frequency of backward (output-conditioned) retrieval correlates even more directly with success (r=0.800), with weaker models showing forward/backward retrieval ratios above 14-16 (i.e., almost never searching backward from the goal). Execution reliability also matters: EGT Precision correlates with accuracy at r=0.781, and Invalid Tool Call Rate anti-correlates at r=-0.443.

Under retrieval-time blocking, all four models tested in the ablation degrade as the fraction of disabled feasible paths increases, with GPT-5.4 losing over 20 points; among block types, implicit (silent) failures are consistently the most damaging, and the paper's Untrusted Input Rejection Rate metric shows agents are more likely to reuse a silently-failed tool's fabricated output as input to later calls (11.99% average) than to reuse an explicit error (9.67%) or a misleading tool's output (9.89%). Restricting recovery to only the single longest remaining valid path (versus the shortest) causes a further sharp drop — GPT-5.4 falls from ~30% under the standard block setting to just above 10%. An "enforced exploration" intervention that inserts continuation prompts whenever a model tries to terminate incorrectly yields only modest gains (most models improve by less than 5 points) and never closes the gap to the no-block accuracy, suggesting the bottleneck is not simply insufficient inference-time budget. Longer minimal solution-path lengths are independently associated with lower accuracy, even without blocking.

A detailed trajectory-level error analysis annotates each valid tool call as a "progress call" (produces evidence needed by at least one feasible solution path) or "non-progress call." Across four analyzed models, the dominant failure category is Irrecoverable Drift (the model makes at least one progress call, then a non-progress call, and never recovers) — 72.4% and 71.3% of failures for GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3.5-Flash respectively — while Weak Recovery (regaining progress after drifting) accounts for only 3.0% of failures on average. Critically, before 78.0% (default) and 71.1% (block) of failed non-progress calls, the model had already retrieved a tool that could have advanced the solution; failed calls show a strong recency bias toward recently-retrieved tools (74.1%/63.6%) even when an older, more useful tool (retrieved more than two windows earlier in 44.7%/43.2% of cases) was available; and even when a genuinely useful tool re-appears in the recent retrieval context after drift (42.5%/53.4% of cases), the model still often fails to select it — indicating the bottleneck is tool selection and working-memory prioritization, not merely retrieval coverage or memory loss. Under blocking, semantically-misleading alternatives are rarely invoked (at most 3% of invoked block alternatives, never for two of the four models), showing agents can usually reject superficially-related-but-wrong tools by inspecting descriptions; explicit failures stop direct value propagation entirely (0% Value Reused) but do not stop the agent from continuing to search from the failed branch; implicit failures are uniquely damaging because their fabricated outputs are propagated as grounded evidence into later tool calls in 42.2% of cases on average (up to 75.5% for Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct), versus 0% for explicit failures. Termination behavior after navigation failure is highly model-specific and stable across settings: GPT-5.4 predominantly surrenders explicitly (77.3%/80.6% of default/block failures) despite every task being solvable by construction; DeepSeek-V4-Flash and especially Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct instead commit hallucinated wrong tool values (58.8-81.7% of failures); Gemini-3.5-Flash mostly exhausts its search budget without converging (90.8%/85.1%).

Robustness checks include non-parametric bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples over the 327 queries) for every metric on every model, generally narrow relative to observed performance gaps; a seed-variation check (3 additional seeds) for two models under blocking showing under 3% variation; and, notably, a single-step retrieval sanity check built from the same tool library, on which a weak model (Qwen3-8B, which scores 0% on the full benchmark) achieves 85.95% accuracy and 92.43% ground-truth-tool retrieval, supporting the claim that the benchmark's difficulty comes from multi-step planning and execution rather than an unreasonably weak retriever. A small human-annotation quality check (5 annotators, all paper co-authors per the Ethics Statement, rating 50 of 1,665 tools and 25 of 56 datatypes on a 1-5 Likert scale) reports average scores of 4.32 and 4.56. Authors' stated limitations: the benchmark is currently instantiated only in the retail domain (though the generation pipeline is designed to be domain-general); the blocking mechanism captures representative but not fully open-ended real-world tool failures; and the retriever is intentionally simplified (grounded in the underlying datatype structure) rather than modeling the noisy documentation, imperfect ranking, and dynamic catalogs of real deployed retrieval systems. ## Learnings

The dissociation between tool discovery and tool exploitation — most non-progress tool calls occur even though a useful, already-retrieved alternative was sitting in the agent's own retrieval history, with a documented recency bias toward recently-retrieved tools over older-but-more-useful ones — is a specific, transferable diagnostic for evaluating any long-horizon retrieval-augmented or tool-using agent: retrieval coverage/recall and downstream exploitation of what has already been retrieved are dissociable failure modes that require different fixes (better retrieval versus better working-memory prioritization and re-ranking of previously-seen candidates), and a benchmark or error analysis that measures only end-task accuracy or retrieval recall will miss this distinction entirely.

The finding that backward, output-conditioned retrieval (searching from the desired outcome rather than only forward from current evidence) correlates more strongly with task success than forward exploration breadth alone (Pearson r=0.800 vs. r=0.902 for a related but distinct forward-exploration metric, with weak models showing forward/backward retrieval ratios above 14-16) is a specific, transferable design principle: agents and their training or scaffolding should be explicitly evaluated and incentivized for bidirectional search rather than assuming forward-only exploration will naturally discover what backward reasoning would surface.

The three-way distinction among explicit-failure, implicit-failure, and semantically-misleading tool corruption, together with the specific finding that implicit (silent) failures are uniquely damaging because their fabricated outputs get propagated as grounded evidence into later tool calls (Value Reused in 42.2% of cases versus 0% for explicit failures), is a transferable robustness-evaluation lesson: an explicit error signal is a comparatively easy failure mode for an agent to act on, but a tool that fails silently with a plausible-looking wrong value is a fundamentally harder and more consequential failure mode that cannot be caught by current agents' output-content inspection alone.

## Verification

This benchmark's empirical validation is substantially more rigorous than typical for the genre. Every metric for every one of the ten evaluated models is reported with non-parametric bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples), and the resulting radii are generally narrow relative to the performance gaps being interpreted; a seed-variation check independently confirms low sensitivity to the specific random blocking configuration. Most notably, a dedicated single-step retrieval sanity-check experiment isolates retriever quality from downstream planning ability by testing a weak model (Qwen3-8B, which scores 0% on the full multi-step benchmark) on trivial one-tool-call tasks built from the same tool library, finding 85.95% accuracy and 92.43% ground-truth-tool retrieval — genuine, targeted evidence that the benchmark's difficulty is not an artifact of an unreasonably noisy or incomplete retriever. The trajectory-level failure taxonomy (progress vs. non-progress calls, four failure categories, three termination-behavior categories) is applied systematically and quantitatively across models rather than illustrated only through anecdote, though the paper does not report an inter-annotator agreement check for the underlying progress/non-progress call labeling itself, which appears to be determined algorithmically against the solvability graph rather than by human judgment, reducing (but not eliminating) this concern.

The main gap is on the benchmark-construction side. The entire tool library, tool names, tool descriptions, natural-language queries, and reference-answer derivation are generated and filtered by a single LLM (GPT-5.2), with the only independent quality check being a small, non-blinded annotation study (5 annotators, who are themselves the paper's co-authors per the Ethics Statement, each rating 10 tools and 5 datatypes, for 50 of 1,665 tools and 25 of 56 datatypes total). This leaves the realism and naturalness of the auto-generated content across the full 1,665-tool library validated only by code-level solvability checks (rigorous for logical correctness) plus a narrow, self-annotated spot check (limited evidence for surface-level realism at scale). Separately, using a GPT-family model (GPT-5.2) to construct the entire benchmark that then evaluates other GPT-family models (GPT-5.4, GPT-5.4-Mini) as two of the ten test subjects introduces a same-family construction relationship the paper does not discuss or explicitly control for, though the fact that Gemini-3.1-Pro substantially and consistently outperforms both evaluated GPT models is suggestive, if not conclusive, evidence against a severe generator-family advantage in practice.

## Field context

PlanBench-XL positions itself via a detailed seven-trait comparison table (Table 1) against seventeen prior tool-use and agentic-planning benchmarks (ToolBench, RestBench, APIBench, ToolRet, API-Bank, EscapeBench, MCP-Universe, MCPBench, ACEBench, BFCL v4, Tool Decathlon, LiveMCPBench, ToolGym, AgentNoiseBench, OpaqueToolsBench, WildAGTEval), arguing it is the first to jointly combine tool retrieval, implicit sub-goal discovery, bi-directional exploration, unreliable/noisy tools, long horizons, and scalable generation in one benchmark, where prior work covers only some of these traits at a time. Within this corpus, the closest connection is [reviews/2606.07297.md](2606.07297.md) (SWE-Explore), reviewed earlier in this collection: both benchmarks independently isolate and name a "finding the right resource is not the same as using it correctly" bottleneck in entirely different domains — SWE-Explore's central finding is that coding agents reliably localize the correct file but systematically miss the specific evidence-bearing lines within it (high HitFile, low line-level recall), while PlanBench-XL's Takeaway 9 finds agents frequently have already retrieved a useful tool yet fail to select or return to it, drifting instead toward recently-retrieved but less useful alternatives. These are two independently-constructed benchmarks in unrelated domains (code exploration versus tool-use planning) converging on the same general diagnostic principle: for current LLM agents, the harder and more persistent bottleneck is often the transition from having relevant information or resources available to actually acting on them correctly, rather than the initial discovery or retrieval step itself.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central claims are backed by unusually thorough empirical rigor for a benchmark paper: bootstrap confidence intervals and seed-robustness checks support the statistical reliability of the main accuracy comparisons; a targeted single-step retrieval sanity check provides genuine, specific evidence that the benchmark's difficulty is not an artifact of retriever weakness; and the trajectory-level failure taxonomy is applied systematically and quantitatively, with concrete supporting statistics (e.g., the 78.0%/71.1% figures on how often a useful tool was already retrieved before a non-progress call) rather than only qualitative illustration.

Two limitations qualify how the results should be read. First, the entire benchmark's tools, descriptions, and queries are generated and filtered by a single LLM (GPT-5.2) with only a small, self-annotated (co-author) quality-validation sample covering roughly 3% of tools and 45% of datatypes; this is a real, if partial, gap in independently verifying that the auto-generated content is realistic and free of systematic artifacts across its full scale, beyond the code-level solvability checks that verify logical (not linguistic or realism) correctness. Second, because the same GPT-family model constructed the benchmark while other GPT-family models (GPT-5.4, GPT-5.4-Mini) are among the ten evaluated systems, there is an unaddressed possibility that the benchmark's phrasing, naming conventions, or tool-description style could be more familiar to GPT-family models than to others; the paper does not discuss or test for this, though Gemini-3.1-Pro's clear lead over both evaluated GPT models in the reported results argues against this being a severe effect in practice. The retriever's intentional simplification (grounded in exact datatype structure rather than realistic noisy embedding-based retrieval) is explicitly disclosed by the authors as a deliberate realism-controllability trade-off rather than an oversight, and is a reasonable design choice given the paper's stated goal of isolating planning and exploitation behavior from retrieval-system quality specifically.

What remains solidly supported after these caveats: massive-tool, long-horizon planning is genuinely difficult even for the strongest tested frontier model, and this difficulty is not explained away by weak retrieval (per the isolation experiment); retrieval-time blocking, and silent/implicit failures specifically, cause severe and statistically well-supported degradation across every tested model family; the dominant failure mode is drift-and-non-recovery after initial partial progress rather than a complete inability to make any progress, and this drift is substantially a tool-selection failure rather than a pure discovery failure; and simply adding more test-time interaction budget provides only modest relief, pointing to a structural adaptive-replanning limitation rather than an easily-fixed sampling-budget constraint.
