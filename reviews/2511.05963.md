Citation: Teoh, J., Tomar, M., Ahn, K., Hu, E.S., Pearce, T., Sharma, P., Krishnamurthy, A., Islam, R., Lamb, A., Langford, J. Next-Latent Prediction Transformers Learn Compact World Models. arXiv:2511.05963, 2025.

## What this paper is about

Transformers predict text one token at a time and can look back at their entire history via attention, which makes them powerful but also removes any built-in pressure to compress everything they've seen into a compact internal summary the way older recurrent networks had to. The paper argues this is why transformers often learn brittle "shortcuts" that fit training data well but generalize poorly, for instance forming an internally incoherent map of a city's streets even while perfectly predicting the next turn in a taxi ride. The authors propose a simple additional training signal, Next-Latent Prediction (NextLat), that trains a transformer's internal representations to be predictable from one time-step to the next (in addition to the usual next-token objective), and prove this forces those representations toward "belief states," the theoretically minimal, sufficient summary of everything relevant that happened so far. The method requires no architecture changes and leaves both training parallelism and inference behavior of the base transformer intact, yet in their experiments it produces more compact, more generalizable internal representations and, as a side benefit, unlocks a faster inference trick.

## Extended summary

Building on the reinforcement-learning notion of a belief state (a sufficient statistic of the past for predicting the future), the paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 3.2) shows that if a transformer is trained to satisfy two conditions jointly, standard next-token prediction, and a "transition consistency" condition where a small auxiliary model can predict the transformer's next hidden state from its current hidden state plus the next token, then the transformer's hidden states must converge to a belief state. Standard next-token training alone does not guarantee this, because self-attention lets the model look up arbitrary past tokens directly rather than being forced to summarize them. NextLat operationalizes the second condition by jointly training the transformer with a lightweight MLP "latent dynamics model" that regresses the next hidden state from the current one plus the next token (a Smooth L1 loss, with a stop-gradient to prevent representational collapse), optionally rolled out over several steps for a richer signal, plus a complementary KL self-distillation term that keeps the predicted latent's implied token distribution aligned with the model's own true output distribution. Crucially, the paper proves belief-state convergence already holds with a rollout horizon of just one step; longer horizons only add extra gradient signal, not a stronger guarantee, which distinguishes NextLat from a competing recent method (JTP) whose belief-state guarantee the paper's own appendix shows requires the prediction horizon to be at least as long as the data's underlying "observability horizon," a condition that is often impractically large for long-context language data. Because the latent dynamics model can be unrolled recursively in latent space alone (without re-invoking the full transformer), it also enables what the paper calls variable-length self-speculative decoding: unlike multi-token-prediction methods, which can only draft as many tokens ahead as they were explicitly trained to predict, NextLat's latent rollout can draft further than its training horizon, since belief-state convergence does not depend on that horizon.

The empirical evaluation spans four settings against three main baselines (standard next-token GPT training, multi-token prediction MTP, and a newer joint multi-token-prediction method JTP, plus Belief State Transformers, BST, where computationally feasible). On a synthetic Manhattan taxi-ride dataset where the true "world model" (street map) is known and directly visualizable, NextLat produces internal maps that are qualitatively closer to the true map than any baseline, the highest sequence-compression score (identical continuations for trajectories converging on the same intersection, 0.71 vs. 0.64-0.65 for GPT/MTP), the lowest effective latent dimensionality (52.7, over 3x smaller than GPT's 160.1), and the strongest generalization to out-of-distribution routes even when random detours are injected. On the Countdown arithmetic-reasoning game, NextLat with only a one-step training horizon already outperforms MTP and JTP trained with the same shallow horizon by more than 35% relative improvement, and specifically avoids a documented failure pattern called the "regretful compromise," where a model realizes only at its very last equation that it cannot reach the target and is forced to output an invalid final step; NextLat's final-equation validity (54.8%) far exceeds the next-best baseline (42.3%). On the Path-Star graph lookahead-planning task, designed specifically to expose transformers' tendency to exploit local shortcuts rather than genuinely plan ahead (the "Clever Hans cheat"), NextLat maintains near-100% solve rates across all tested graph sizes while BST begins failing on the largest graph and MTP/JTP perform substantially worse throughout, despite NextLat being trained on a comparatively small, fixed sample of 200k sequences. On TinyStories, frozen-representation linear probes trained to predict tokens many steps ahead show that BST, MTP, and JTP's added token-level objectives measurably degrade both next-token accuracy and far-ahead predictive information in the learned representations, while NextLat preserves next-token performance and shows the strongest predictive information up to 20 tokens ahead.

Scaling up to a more standard language-modeling setting (1.3B-parameter transformers trained on 100B tokens of FineWeb-Edu), the results are more mixed: NextLat with horizon 2 shows only a modest, not fully consistent, average accuracy gain over standard GPT training across a battery of zero-shot multiple-choice benchmarks (59.21% vs. 58.82%), and the paper explicitly states larger model sizes might be needed to see clearer gains at this axis. However, NextLat does more reliably preserve next-token perplexity better than MTP or JTP (which both measurably degrade it), consistent with the TinyStories probing results, and the speculative-decoding results here are the paper's clearest scaled-up win: across Wikipedia, Books, Code, and Math domains, NextLat's average number of accepted draft tokens per drafting step far exceeds its own training horizon (evidence its latent dynamics remain coherent well beyond where they were explicitly supervised), translating into substantially larger inference speedups (up to 3.3x, versus roughly 1.7-1.9x for MTP/JTP even when those baselines are given a larger, more expensive training horizon of 4).

A separate mechanistic investigation examines an A5 permutation state-tracking task, a problem class formally known to be inexpressible by fixed-depth, fixed-precision transformers (it sits in a higher computational-complexity class, NC1, than what such transformers can express, TC0). Trained on 12-token sequences, a 2-layer NextLat transformer itself fails to generalize to 36-token sequences, just as a same-sized GPT model does, and just as a GPT model trained directly on 36-token sequences also fails. Strikingly, the small MLP latent-dynamics model NextLat co-trains alongside the transformer, despite having far fewer parameters and being trained entirely in parallel with no sequential computation, successfully generalizes to 36-token sequences with over 95% accuracy. The authors interpret this as evidence that NextLat may offer a way to partially route around the fundamental parallelism-versus-expressivity tradeoff of transformers, letting a transformer that can only see a bounded context train a smaller recurrent module that inherits and generalizes a sequential computation the transformer itself cannot perform, though they are explicit that this is an intriguing empirical observation raising an open theoretical question rather than a proven general result.

## Learnings

The paper's most conceptually important and broadly transferable idea is a specific, formally proven route to injecting a "recurrent" inductive bias, an incentive to compress history into a compact, consistently-evolving state, into an architecture (the transformer) that has no such incentive by default, without touching that architecture at all or sacrificing its parallel-training efficiency; this is a general recipe (a lightweight, self-supervised auxiliary loss enforcing next-latent-state predictability) that is not tied to any particular downstream task and is directly reusable by anyone training transformers for planning-, world-modeling-, or long-horizon-coherence-sensitive applications.

A second, more mechanistic and immediately useful learning is the demonstrated connection between how compactly a model represents its internal state and how well it avoids shortcut-driven, myopic failures on tasks that specifically require lookahead (the Path-Star "Clever Hans cheat" and the Countdown "regretful compromise" are both concrete, named, previously-documented failure patterns that the paper shows a compactness-inducing training objective measurably alleviates); this gives a specific, testable diagnostic (does the model exhibit these particular named failure signatures?) for anyone trying to determine whether a transformer trained on a similar lookahead-planning task is learning genuine forward planning or exploiting local shortcuts.

A third, more forward-looking and genuinely surprising learning is the A5 state-tracking result: a transformer whose own architecture provably cannot express a certain class of computation can nonetheless, through this kind of self-supervised co-training, produce a much smaller auxiliary model that does generalize to that harder computation class. This is a specific, well-scoped empirical phenomenon (not a general claim, and the paper is careful to frame it as an open question rather than a settled result) worth remembering as a concrete existence proof that a model's own architectural expressivity ceiling does not automatically bound what a co-trained, differently-structured module derived from its representations can achieve.

## Verification

The paper's core theoretical claim (belief-state convergence) is backed by an explicit theorem with a proof sketch in the main text and a full formal proof (by backward induction) in the appendix, and the paper is careful to state precisely what the theorem does and does not guarantee, for instance explicitly noting that the guarantee holds already at a training horizon of one and that longer horizons only improve empirical signal rather than strengthening the theoretical result, a distinction it also uses to make a specific, checkable contrast with a competing method (JTP) whose own belief-state guarantee is shown (via a cited theorem in the appendix) to require an often-impractically-large minimum prediction horizon. The empirical claims are checked against a reasonably strong and relevant set of baselines (standard next-token GPT, multi-token prediction MTP, the more recent JTP, and Belief State Transformers BST where computationally tractable) across four qualitatively distinct task families (a fully-known synthetic world model, a challenging arithmetic reasoning game, a lookahead-planning stress test, and standard-scale language modeling), rather than a single favorable benchmark, and several results are corroborated by more than one independent signal (e.g., the TinyStories linear-probe analysis and the direct sequence-compression/latent-rank metrics on the Manhattan taxi task both independently point toward the same conclusion, that NextLat's representations carry more genuinely predictive, longer-range information). The paper is also unusually transparent about negative or inconclusive results rather than only positive ones: it explicitly reports that language-modeling-scale accuracy gains are modest and inconsistent across tasks, and a dedicated appendix section candidly documents an unexplained rise in one of NextLat's own training losses near the end of training that the authors tried five different fixes for without success, stating plainly that "the cause of this behavior remains unclear."

Verification is comparatively thinner in a few places. The paper's most mechanistically striking and theoretically provocative result, the A5 state-tracking experiment showing a co-trained latent dynamics model generalizing beyond the expressivity limits of its parent transformer, is demonstrated only on a small, synthetic task with 2-layer transformers and sequence lengths up to 36 tokens; the paper explicitly frames the broader implication as an open research question ("can a transformer... co-train... an RNN that generalizes to sequences of length ≫ T?") rather than a general, proven claim, which is appropriately careful, but it does mean this specific, headline-worthy phenomenon has not been checked at any larger scale or on any other task, so its generality beyond this one narrow synthetic setting is genuinely unknown rather than merely unproven. The design of the NextLat loss itself (the specific choice of Smooth L1 over MSE, the use of stop-gradients, the KL self-distillation term) is explicitly stated by the authors to be guided by "small-scale ablations... and empirical intuition" rather than a systematic search, and the paper states directly that it remains unclear whether these specific design choices are even necessary at larger model and data scales, an honestly-flagged gap between what has been validated (small-to-moderate scale) and what the method's practical relevance ultimately depends on (whether these benefits persist or compound at the scale of production language models).

## Field context

The paper positions NextLat directly against two contemporaneous belief-state-learning methods for transformers it compares head-to-head throughout, Belief State Transformers (BST), which achieves a similar theoretical guarantee but at quadratic (O(T^2)) gradient cost and by training two full transformer encoders (making it, by the paper's own measurement, over 3x slower to train and requiring 10% subsampling of training pairs just to fit in memory), and JTP, which is more computationally efficient than BST but whose belief-state guarantee the paper shows theoretically requires a minimum prediction horizon tied to the data's "observability horizon" that is often impractically large for long-context text. NextLat's specific claimed contribution relative to both is achieving the same class of theoretical guarantee at a training cost close to standard next-token prediction and independent of the chosen prediction horizon. The paper also explicitly connects its approach to the "self-predictive representation learning" tradition already well-established in reinforcement learning (citing specific prior methods this borrows its core mechanism from) and to a lineage of successful latent-dynamics-based world models outside the language domain (MuZero, Dreamer, Genie), framing its contribution as bringing an already-validated RL representation-learning idea into transformer-based sequence modeling, an area it argues has been comparatively underexplored for this specific technique. It further situates itself within an active debate the paper cites directly about whether transformer language models implicitly learn coherent internal world models at all, taking a clear position by using the specific incoherent-street-map phenomenon documented in prior work as its primary motivating example and demonstrating a concrete, measurable fix for that specific failure. Within this corpus, this paper's broader concern, understanding and shaping what a transformer's internal representations actually encode during autoregressive generation rather than only observing its output-level accuracy, is thematically adjacent to [2508.17627] (the Reasoning Dynamics/RCP paper, reviewed earlier in this batch), which similarly probes internal transformer states (there, semantic convergence during chain-of-thought reasoning) to understand and intervene on generation behavior; the two papers approach a related underlying question, what a transformer's hidden states are actually doing beyond the next-token prediction they are nominally trained for, from different angles (training-time representation shaping here, versus inference-time behavioral analysis there).

## Critical discussion

The paper's theoretical contribution is a genuine strength: a precisely-stated theorem with an explicit proof, a clean and checkable distinction from the closest competing method's weaker guarantee, and a training-time cost that the paper directly and favorably measures against that competing method rather than only asserting. The breadth of empirical validation, spanning a fully-known synthetic world model, a hard combinatorial reasoning task, a purpose-built shortcut-detection benchmark, and standard-scale language modeling, with multiple independent metrics corroborating the same underlying story at each stage, is also a real strength that goes beyond a single favorable benchmark result.

The clearest gap is between the abstract's framing, that NextLat "demonstrates significant gains over standard next-token prediction and other baselines in downstream accuracy," and the specific result at the one setting most directly relevant to real-world language-model deployment (1.3B-parameter, 100B-token pretraining), where the reported average accuracy gain (59.21% vs. 58.82%) is modest and the paper itself states is "not consistent across tasks," with the clearer, more reliable win at that scale being inference speed rather than accuracy. This is not a case of the paper hiding the result, since the relevant table and the honest caveat about needing larger models are both present in the main text, but the abstract's general "significant gains... in downstream accuracy" language does not clearly signal that this particular claim is strongest in the smaller, more controlled synthetic settings (world modeling, Countdown, Path-Star) and comparatively weak and uncertain at the language-modeling scale that most readers would likely care about most. A second, related limitation is that the paper's most theoretically provocative and headline-friendly empirical result, a small co-trained model exceeding its parent transformer's provable expressivity ceiling on the A5 task, is demonstrated only in a narrow, small-scale, synthetic setting, and the paper's own framing of it as an open question rather than an established phenomenon is appropriately cautious, but this does mean the finding's generality, whether it holds for larger transformers, other NC1-hard problems, or realistic language tasks, is genuinely untested rather than merely unproven, and readers should not extrapolate this specific finding beyond the narrow setting where it was shown. A third, more minor point, self-disclosed by the authors, is that several specific design choices in the NextLat loss (Smooth L1 vs. MSE, stop-gradient placement, the KL self-distillation term) rest on small-scale ablations and intuition rather than a systematic study, leaving open whether the particular recipe validated here remains the right one, or even necessary at all, at substantially larger model and data scales.

What remains well supported after these caveats: the central theoretical claim, that combining next-token prediction with a one-step latent-transition-consistency objective provably drives transformer hidden states toward belief states, independent of any multi-step prediction horizon, is proven rigorously and distinguished clearly and correctly from a weaker guarantee in a directly comparable prior method; in every controlled, small-to-moderate-scale experimental setting tested (world modeling, arithmetic reasoning, lookahead planning, and representation-probing on TinyStories), NextLat produces measurably more compact and more genuinely predictive internal representations than the tested baselines, translating into concrete, non-trivial gains on tasks specifically designed to expose shortcut learning; and the variable-length self-speculative decoding capability this induces provides a real, substantial, and consistently-observed inference speedup at the one language-modeling scale tested, independent of whether the accuracy gains at that same scale turn out to be as strong.
