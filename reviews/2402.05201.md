Citation: Matthew Renze and Erhan Guven. The Effect of Sampling Temperature on Problem Solving in Large Language Models. Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2024, pp. 7346-7356, 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05201

## What this paper is about

Practitioners set the sampling temperature of LLM APIs mostly by folklore: low for "precise" tasks, high for "creative" ones. The authors test whether temperature actually changes problem-solving accuracy. They build multiple-choice exams from ten public benchmarks, run nine API models released between late 2022 and April 2024 with five one-shot prompt styles, and sweep temperature from 0.0 to 1.0 (to 1.6 for one model), answering every question ten times. They report no statistically significant change in mean accuracy between 0.0 and 1.0 across models, prompts, and domains, a rapid collapse above 1.0 for GPT-3.5, and they recommend temperature 0.0 for problem solving because it is reproducible at no measured accuracy cost.

## Extended summary

Temperature is defined in the usual way: the next-token distribution is a softmax of logits divided by a constant tau, so low tau concentrates mass on the most likely token (greedy at 0) and high tau flattens it. The introduction frames the knob as an exploit-explore trade-off in solution space. Footnotes motivate the study: OpenAI's GPT-3.5 API allowed 0.0 to 1.0 with default 0.7, GPT-4's allowed 0.0 to 2.0 with default 1.0, with no stated rationale, and the GPT-4 technical report describes its benchmark temperatures as a "best-guess". The authors note that a few prior empirical studies did find temperature effects on code generation and on an engineering exam.

Models (Table 1): Claude 3 Opus, Command R+, Gemini 1.0 Pro, Gemini 1.5 Pro (Preview), GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4, Llama 2 7B Chat, Llama 2 70B Chat, and Mistral Large, all chosen because their APIs expose temperature. Prompts (all single request-response with one example problem and solution): Baseline (answer only, in the form Answer("C")), Domain Expertise (the system prompt names the domain expertise), Self-recitation (recite relevant knowledge first), Chain-of-Thought (think step by step), and Composite (all of the above plus a self-criticism step; Figure 10 shows the template). Multi-step agents, few-shot prompting, and fine-tuning are excluded.

Data (Table 2): ten MCQA problem sets, namely ARC Challenge test (science), AQUA-RAT (math), HellaSwag validation (common-sense completion), LogiQA English (logic), LSAT-AR, LSAT-LR, and LSAT-RC (law), MedMCQA validation (medicine), SAT-English, and SAT-Math; seven of the ten come from AGIEval. A 1,000-question exam samples 100 per set and a 100-question exam samples 10 per set; the smaller one exists for cost reasons.

The full factorial design in Algorithm 1 was not run. The executed slices were:

- GPT-3.5 with CoT on the 100-question exam, temperature 0.0 to 2.0 in steps of 0.1, to find the usable range. Accuracy fell rapidly above 1.0 and text was incoherent by 1.6, so the sweep stopped there and all other sweeps used 0.0 to 1.0.
- The other eight models with CoT on the 100-question exam, 0.0 to 1.0.
- GPT-3.5 with each of the five prompts on the 100-question exam, 0.0 to 1.0.
- GPT-3.5 with CoT on the 1,000-question exam, 0.0 to 1.0, for the per-domain analysis.

All other sampling parameters (top-p, top-k, repetition penalty) were held fixed; their values are not given. Each question was answered ten times per condition. Per-question accuracy is the fraction of ten attempts correct, and the reported accuracy is the mean over questions. Because per-question accuracy is bimodal around 0 and 1, the authors test for a temperature effect with the Kruskal-Wallis test across the eleven temperature groups (df 10). They also compute six text-similarity measures of responses (Jaccard, bag-of-words, TF-IDF, Levenshtein, BLEU, SBERT).

Results:

- GPT-3.5, CoT, 1,000 questions: H(10) = 10.439, p = 0.403.
- By model (Table 3, CoT, 100 questions), every p-value exceeds 0.05. Claude 3 Opus 0.998, Command R+ 0.998, Gemini 1.0 Pro 0.689, Gemini 1.5 Pro 0.978, GPT-3.5 0.996, GPT-4 0.956, Llama 2 70B 0.961, Mistral Large 0.980, and Llama 2 7B 0.072 (H = 17.086). The text says most models show a "gradual (non-significant) decrease" with temperature. Llama 2 7B did no better than random guessing: 39 percent of its answers were misformatted and 36 percent were well-formed but wrong. Its low p-value is attributed to all-or-nothing behavior at 0.0 against more random behavior from 0.1 to 1.0.
- By prompt (Table 4, GPT-3.5, 100 questions), all p-values are 0.996 or higher. CoT outperformed the other four prompts and was used for all single-prompt runs; no numbers or test for that comparison appear in the text.
- By exam (Table 5, GPT-3.5, CoT), LSAT-AR (H = 37.874, p < 0.001) and SAT-Math (H = 21.276, p = 0.019) are significant, and ARC Challenge (p = 0.089) is treated as non-significant. The other exams range from p = 0.413 (AQUA-RAT) to 0.993 (MedMCQA). Dunn-Bonferroni post hoc tests on the two significant exams attribute the effect to all-or-nothing responses at 0.0 against more random responses at 0.1 to 1.0: individual problems' accuracy varied significantly between temperatures while the average stayed similar.
- Text similarity decreases steadily with temperature and drops quickly above 1.0, tracking the accuracy collapse. The authors say this holds across models, prompts, and domains (Figures 7 to 9 use TF-IDF). The curves are in figures whose values are not in the extract.

The authors conclude that temperature changes between 0.0 and 1.0 have no statistically significant effect on MCQA problem-solving performance, and recommend 0.0 for problem solving because it maximizes reproducibility without compromising accuracy and avoids the drop above 1.0. They further state that temperature up to 1.0 does not cause hallucination that leads to wrong MCQA answers, and that higher temperatures do not improve MCQA solution-space search.

Stated limitations: a subset of models; only single-cycle one-shot prompts (agents might use temperature better); a subset of problems and domains; small test sets (1,000 and 100) that "may have introduced bias"; the range above 1.0 explored only for GPT-3.5; other sampling parameters fixed; and only a subset of model-prompt-exam combinations run. Proposed future work includes open-ended tasks, since "the limited effects of sampling temperature in our experiments may have simply resulted from the constraints imposed by the structure of MCQA problems", creative or lateral-thinking tasks, extending the range until accuracy reaches zero (noting that response length, and hence cost, grows sharply at high temperature), and error analysis of problem subtypes, because averaging may hide temperature-sensitive problems.

## Learnings

On closed-form multiple-choice questions, raising temperature from 0 to 1 changed the wording of responses far more than their final answers. Text similarity between responses fell as temperature rose, while no change in mean single-sample accuracy was detected for any of nine API models available in early 2024. The caveat is statistical: this is a failure to detect under a weak test, not demonstrated equivalence, and the multi-sample regime where temperature usually earns its keep was not measured.

Mean accuracy can stay flat while per-question correctness moves. On LSAT-AR and SAT-Math the post hoc tests found individual questions whose accuracy changed across temperatures with the average unchanged, and at temperature 0 each question was right on all ten attempts or on none. A single-run score on a small benchmark therefore mixes item-level sampling luck with capability, and the fewer the questions, the worse the mix.

When the same items are scored under every condition, the analysis should be paired and aimed at the quantity of interest. Here an unpaired rank test was dominated by between-question variance, since questions are mostly always right or always wrong, and it responded to the changed distribution shape at temperature 0 rather than to any change in the mean. That is how two "significant" domains appeared, which the authors then attribute to that shape change.

## Verification

The paper does not check reasoning. Grading compares the chosen option, which the prompt requires in the form Answer("X"), against the benchmark key. The parsing procedure is not described, and misformatted outputs evidently count as wrong (39 percent of Llama 2 7B's answers). The chain-of-thought, recitation, and self-criticism text is never evaluated, so a correct letter reached by a wrong argument counts as correct. The reproducibility argument for temperature 0 rests on the text-similarity curves and the all-or-nothing pattern at 0. It is not a direct measurement of answer agreement across runs or API calls.

## Field context

The authors frame the work against prompt-engineering guidance that recommends temperatures without evidence, vendor defaults that changed without explanation, and "best-guess" temperatures in the GPT-4 report. They position it as a systematic answer to "what temperature for problem solving", citing earlier studies that found temperature effects in code generation (Xu et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2023, dynamic temperature sampling) and on an engineering licensing exam (Pursnani et al. 2023). The paper does not reconcile its null result with those studies beyond noting them. The temperature softmax itself is traced to Boltzmann machines (Ackley et al. 1985) and distillation (Hinton et al. 2015).

What follows is my inference from other reviews in this corpus, not the authors' framing. reviews/1904.09751.md shows that in open-ended continuation the decoding rule is decisive: low temperatures sharply increase repetition, and untruncated sampling drifts into incoherence. This paper is the closed-answer counterpart. When the output that is scored is a single letter at the end of a chain, temperature at or below 1 mostly changes the path and not the endpoint, while both papers see incoherence once the distribution is flattened too far. reviews/2504.09389.md finds that raising temperature on open-ended creative tasks increases originality until quality collapses. Together with this paper's rising lexical variability and collapse above 1.0 for GPT-3.5, that suggests a common picture: temperature buys surface variation cheaply up to a model-specific point and then destroys quality. The two papers use different metrics and model families, so this is an analogy, not a replication. reviews/2510.01171.md locates diversity loss in post-training, modeling the aligned policy as a temperature-sharpened version of the base model. That is one reason sampling temperature on an already aligned model may move answers so little. The variation this paper averages away is what several reviewed methods use. reviews/24semant.md samples at temperature 1 and treats disagreement across samples as a confabulation signal, and reviews/2510.00931.md and reviews/2510.01143.md depend on diverse parallel samples for aggregation. None of these multi-sample uses is tested here. On evaluation practice, reviews/2410.05229.md reports accuracy swings of up to about 15 points across resampled variants of the same GSM8K questions, and reviews/2509.26574.md finds that requiring 4 of 5 runs to be correct leaves every model except GPT-5 (high) at zero on its full physics challenges. Both are consistent with this paper's item-level instability under a flat mean. reviews/2510.04950.md, another MCQA prompt-perturbation study, uses paired tests over repeated runs and reports differences of a few points as significant. That is the kind of analysis this paper's design could have supported. All nine models here were released by April 2024 (Table 1), before the long-chain reasoning models studied in reviews such as reviews/2604.10739.md.

## Critical discussion

The paper's practical core is credible as far as it goes. Across nine API models and five prompt styles, moving temperature between 0 and 1 did not produce a large, obvious change in mean MCQA accuracy, while one model collapsed above 1. For a practitioner choosing between 0 and 0.7 on a multiple-choice task, that is useful. The stronger claims made from it are less well supported.

A non-significant Kruskal-Wallis test is being read as "no effect". The paper reports no effect sizes, no confidence intervals on accuracy, no equivalence margin, and no power analysis, and the accuracy levels themselves appear only in figures that the extract does not preserve. The authors also write that most models show a "gradual (non-significant) decrease" with temperature. A decline in the same direction for most of eight models is weak evidence of a small real effect that per-model omnibus tests cannot resolve. The test is an omnibus comparison of eleven groups and is not tuned to a monotone trend. My rough estimate, assuming near-bimodal per-question outcomes (standard deviation close to 0.5): on the 100-question exam an unpaired comparison of two temperatures would detect only differences of roughly 15 to 20 accuracy points with reasonable power, and on the 1,000-question exam roughly 5 to 6 points. The claim therefore rules out large effects. It does not rule out the few-point effects that matter when ranking models.

The test also does not match the design. The same questions are answered at every temperature, so the data are repeated measures, yet Kruskal-Wallis treats the temperature groups as independent. The huge between-question variance (easy questions always right, hard ones always wrong) then swamps any within-question temperature effect. A Friedman test, a paired bootstrap, or a mixed-effects logistic model with question effects and temperature as a continuous predictor would test the stated hypothesis directly and with more power. The mismatch runs both ways. The two significant exams, LSAT-AR and SAT-Math, are explained by the authors' own post hoc analysis as a distribution-shape change at temperature 0 (all-or-nothing answers), not a change in mean accuracy, and the same explanation is given for Llama 2 7B's low p-value. The instrument is thus insensitive to modest mean shifts and sensitive to a shape change that is irrelevant to mean accuracy. The authors report the shape explanation honestly, but it undercuts the use of the test as the arbiter in both directions.

The quantity measured is narrower than the claims drawn from it. Accuracy is the mean of single-sample correctness. Temperature matters most where several samples are drawn and aggregated (self-consistency, best-of-N, pass@k), and the paper already has ten attempts per question per temperature but reports neither pass@10 nor majority-vote accuracy. The statement that higher temperatures "do not appear to improve MCQA solution-space search" is therefore not tested by the metric used. The per-question shifts found on LSAT-AR and SAT-Math are exactly the variation a multi-sample aggregator would exploit or suffer from. Likewise, the claim that temperature up to 1.0 "does not cause the LLM to hallucinate in ways that lead to incorrect MCQA solutions" rests on final-letter accuracy alone. No reasoning trace is checked, and hallucination is never measured.

The generality claims rest on one-factor-at-a-time slices, not a crossed design. The model axis was tested only with CoT on the 100-question exam (ten questions per domain). The prompt axis was tested only on GPT-3.5, and the domain axis only on GPT-3.5 with CoT. The range above 1.0 was tested only on GPT-3.5. Interactions, such as a different model with the Baseline prompt on SAT-Math, were not measured. Two of ten domains were significant, yet the abstract says the results generalize across problem domains. Llama 2 7B performed at chance, so its null adds nothing to the model count. The Discussion's "inevitable drop-off in performance that occurs beyond a temperature of 1.0" contradicts the authors' own fifth limitation, which says other models may behave differently above 1.0. Temperature 1 means sampling each model's unmodified distribution, and how peaked that distribution is differs by model and post-training, so there is no reason to expect coherence to break at the same temperature for every model. One further untested confound, offered as a hypothesis: all ten problem sets are public benchmarks, and memorized items would be answered stably at any temperature for reasons unrelated to reasoning.

Several reporting gaps limit reuse. The values of the "fixed" top-p, top-k, and maximum-length settings are not given. That matters because truncation interacts strongly with temperature (reviews/1904.09751.md), so the same temperature sweep under top-p 1.0 and under top-p 0.9 are different experiments. The text does not say which pairs of responses the similarity metrics compare. The CoT advantage is asserted with no numbers or test. Counts in the method are inconsistent: "ten models" appears where nine are listed, and Algorithm 1 specifies 16 temperatures while the sweeps used 11 (0.0 to 1.0) or 17 (0.0 to 1.6, cut from a planned 0.0 to 2.0).

What remains supported: for these nine API models on closed-form MCQA with one-shot prompts and single-sample scoring, temperatures between 0 and 1 did not produce a large change in mean accuracy, output wording became measurably more variable as temperature rose, and GPT-3.5 degraded to incoherence and zero accuracy between 1.0 and 1.6. Choosing 0 for single-shot MCQA evaluation is a defensible default on this evidence. That small effects are absent, that the result holds for open-ended or multi-sample problem solving, and that temperature does not affect hallucination are not established here.

## Relevance to us

This bears on evaluation hygiene more than on any mechanism we are building. For budget-matched baseline comparisons (B01) and for scoring physics benchmarks, it supports treating temperature within 0 to 1 as a second-order confounder of mean single-sample accuracy on closed-answer items, at least for the API models of early 2024. A baseline run at 0.7 against ours at 0 is not unfair on that basis alone. The paper's own item-level result, together with the consistency criterion in reviews/2509.26574.md and the resampling variance in reviews/2410.05229.md, argues against reporting a single temperature-0 run on a small physics set. We should report repeated runs, per-question agreement, and paired comparisons, and we should analyze them with a paired or mixed-effects test rather than an unpaired omnibus test. Temperature 0 as the reproducibility default suits the users who want results regenerable later, with the caveat that this paper does not measure run-to-run determinism of the APIs themselves.

For diversity-preserving scientific generation (B16), the paper adds little. It shows that temperature raises lexical variability without moving closed-answer accuracy, but it never asks whether that variability yields distinct correct candidates, and hypothesis generation is open-ended, which is exactly the regime the authors flag as untested. reviews/2504.09389.md and reviews/2510.01171.md are the closer evidence there.
