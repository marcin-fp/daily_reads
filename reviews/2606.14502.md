## What this paper is about

This is a large narrative survey from Tencent Youtu Lab and academic collaborators arguing that LLM-based AI is moving through two parallel transitions at once: on the "thinking" side, from fast one-pass chatbots to slow, deliberate "Thinking LLMs" that reason before answering; and on the "acting" side, from fragile tool-calling agents to systems embedded in a persistent, stateful "workspace" (files, terminals, browsers, logs, permissions) equipped with reusable "skills." The paper's central thesis is that it is specifically the combination of **persistent workspace + reusable skill** — not a bigger model or more tools alone — that turns an LLM from something that answers questions into something that can reliably finish real work, and it walks through how this reframes what training data and evaluation need to look like.

## Extended summary

The survey is organized into four parts plus an open-challenges/future-directions section, built around roughly 1,200 cited works.

**Part I (Cognitive Core)** traces the Chatbot Era — scaling laws (Kaplan, Chinchilla), parametric knowledge compression, in-context learning, Mixture-of-Experts efficiency, RLHF-based alignment, and multimodal expansion — as producing fluent but fundamentally "System-1," single-pass generators whose knowledge is compressed, static, and hard to audit. It then traces the Thinking LLM Era (o1, DeepSeek R1) as internalizing what were previously external scaffolds (Tree-of-Thought search, Self-Refine-style reflection, multi-path exploration) into a single model's output via reinforcement learning, producing long Chain-of-Thought traces, inference-time scaling (a striking cited example: a 1B model with enough inference-time compute reportedly surpassing a 405B model on math benchmarks), and phenomena like reasoning-length "overthinking" and DeepSeek-R1's contested "Aha moment." It traces the RL algorithm lineage from PPO through GRPO (removing the value model), DAPO, Dr. GRPO, CISPO, GSPO, and SAPO, noting that the simplest reward design (rule-based answer matching) has empirically outperformed more elaborate process-reward approaches in practice.

**Part II (Tool-Augmented Execution)** covers the Agent Era's ReAct-style observe-plan-act-observe loop and its four canonical sub-capabilities (perception, planning, memory, tool use), citing evidence that current agents remain fragile: WebArena's GPT-4 achieving only 14% success, and a synthesized four-pattern failure taxonomy (premature ungrounded action, over-helpfulness with plausible-but-wrong details, distractor-induced context pollution, fragile execution under load). It then defines the OpenClaw Era via an explicit boundary table (Table 4) distinguishing an "environment-action-feedback loop" (episodic state, tool calls as isolated transactions, evaluation by action/trajectory success) from a "persistent workspace for task hosting" (durable files/sessions/logs, workspace operations, evaluation by final-state verification, task closure, and auditability) — arguing this is a qualitative architectural shift, not merely "more tools." This section also surveys emerging reliability research (agents succeeding once but failing on repeated runs due to stochastic execution), and a cluster of very recent (2026-dated) security/governance work specific to "OpenClaw"-branded systems (ClawGuard, ClawSafety, ClawKeeper, SemaClaw, "Taming OpenClaw," "Don't Let the Claw Grip Your Hand") addressing prompt injection, capability/identity/knowledge poisoning, and runtime permission enforcement.

**Part III** develops the paper's core thesis: a workspace provides durable state, memory, and consequence (files, terminals, logs that persist and can be inspected, rolled back, and audited), while a skill provides reusable operational knowledge (a directory-level package — centered on a `SKILL.md` file plus scripts, examples, dependencies, and checks — that is more than a prompt and more than a single tool). The paper argues this combination shifts human-AI interaction from instruction (correcting one response at a time) to delegation (granting bounded authority, monitoring an inspectable "work episode," and auditing a final state). Notably, this section includes an explicit, self-critical limitations subsection: skills go stale as underlying tools/APIs/UIs drift ("skill brittleness"); skills can overfit to the specific environment where they were authored and misfire when auto-retrieved into a different context ("negative transfer"); persistent workspaces accumulate stale files, failed artifacts, and corrupted caches that can reduce rather than improve reliability ("workspace contamination"); skills present a supply-chain attack surface (malicious or over-permissive skills); and the entire paradigm shifts the bottleneck from prompt design to costly system operations (versioning, sandboxing, verification infrastructure).

**Part IV** reframes data and evaluation across the same four eras (Table 6-7): chatbot-era data is static instruction-response pairs judged by answer correctness/preference; Thinking-LLM-era data is reasoning traces and process-reward annotations (PRM800K, Math-Shepherd) judged by step-level correctness; Agent/OpenClaw-era data becomes full state-action-observation trajectories (tool traces, UI states, file diffs, permission logs) judged by task closure, state verifiability (external evidence like test results or file diffs, not the model's own claim), execution reliability, efficiency, reproducibility (fixed initial states, snapshot/restore), and trajectory-level safety. The paper argues this creates a harder data problem: high-quality trajectories cannot be passively scraped, are expensive to annotate, and age quickly as UIs and APIs change, while simulation environments face a realism-versus-safety-and-reproducibility trade-off.

The **Open Challenges** section names long-horizon reliability (errors compounding across tool calls, skill-composition interface mismatches), safety/governance (permission boundaries, audit trails, sandboxing as operational rather than purely linguistic concerns), human-AI collaboration ethics (deskilling risk, blurred error responsibility, data-sovereignty and enterprise-asset boundary questions), and memory/persistent state (arguing the conventional short-term/long-term memory distinction is too coarse, proposing instead a forms/functions/dynamics framework). The **Future Directions** section proposes "self-evolving AI ecosystems" — a stack of prompt engineering, context engineering, and "harness engineering" (citing SWE-agent's argument that the agent-computer interface is itself a decisive design object), "beyond-gradient learning" (systems improving by editing their own tests, wrappers, and skills rather than only updating model weights), and governed multi-agent orchestration with explicit authority scopes and accountability — closing with the principle that "every consequential action should be treated as evidence, and every useful piece of evidence should become a governed update." The Conclusion reiterates the two-dimensional (cognitive-core x tool-execution) framing with Workspace + Skill as the connecting mechanism. No new experiments, benchmarks, or models are introduced by the paper itself; no funding or acknowledgments section is present in the extracted text beyond a project webpage reference.

## Learnings

The two-dimensional decomposition — cognitive-core evolution (reasoning capability: Chatbot to Thinking LLM) as an axis orthogonal to tool-execution evolution (acting in environments: Agent to OpenClaw) — is a genuinely useful organizing lens distinct from a single linear "generations" narrative: it clarifies that a system's reasoning strength and its execution-harness maturity are separable properties that can be strong or weak independently (a capable reasoner wrapped in a fragile, stateless tool harness, or a robust persistent-workspace harness driven by a comparatively weak model), a distinction often collapsed in casual discussion of "agent progress."

Table 4's explicit, checkable boundary criteria between "Agent era" and "OpenClaw era" systems (organizing abstraction, unit of action, state model, knowledge reuse, task objective, evaluation focus, failure recovery, safety boundary) function as a reusable classification rubric that can be applied to any concrete agent system, rather than a vague qualitative label: the operational test the paper implies is whether a system treats its environment as an external oracle queried per step (episodic, no persistent state) or as a durable place it inhabits (files, sessions, and logs that survive and can be inspected across the whole trajectory).

The three-stage evaluation reframing (final-output scoring, to process/reasoning judgment, to task-state/workspace-closure verification) is a specific, transferable lens for understanding why a given benchmark's metric choice tracks a fundamentally different construct: an accuracy-style benchmark (e.g., MMLU-style final-answer scoring) cannot distinguish systems that differ mainly in their ability to sustain a correct, verifiable trajectory through a real, stateful environment, which requires execution-based state-diff verification (as in SWE-bench's test-pass criterion or OSWorld's custom execution-based checking scripts) rather than answer-matching.

## Verification

As a narrative survey, the paper does not run its own experiments; its evidentiary backbone is the selective citation of other papers' benchmark results (e.g., WebArena's reported 14% GPT-4 success rate, low OSWorld success rates) to support its periodization narrative. No systematic-review methodology is disclosed anywhere in the sections read — no stated search protocol, database coverage, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or explicit account of how roughly 1,200 citations were selected from the presumably much larger pool of related work in this extremely fast-moving area — which is common practice for narrative surveys in this space but means a reader cannot independently assess how representative or exhaustive the cited evidence base is, nor how the specific placement of the Agent-to-OpenClaw boundary was arrived at beyond the authors' own qualitative synthesis.

## Field context

The paper explicitly positions itself against prior agent-architecture surveys it cites (Wang et al.'s Profile-Memory-Planning-Action framework, Xi et al.'s cognitive-science framing, the CoALA cognitive-psychology mapping, Buyya et al.'s six-dimension POMDP framing), arguing that these treat perception, planning, memory, and tool use as separate ingredients rather than unifying them under a workspace-centered account of what changed between early tool-calling agents and later persistent-workspace systems. Within this corpus, several already-reviewed papers provide concrete, quantified instances of ideas this survey discusses only qualitatively: [reviews/2604.14228.md](2604.14228.md) ("Dive into Claude Code") independently reverse-engineers the internal harness architecture of a real production coding agent, offering an empirical grounding check on this survey's abstract "workspace + skill + harness engineering" claims from a source the survey itself does not cite. [reviews/2606.11926.md](2606.11926.md) (Arbor), reviewed earlier in this same batch, is a concrete working instance of the survey's "self-evolving ecosystem" concept in Section 6.2 — its hypothesis tree literally implements "a task trajectory becomes a memory" and "a failure becomes a reusable, distilled insight" with measured downstream gains, rather than as a forecast. [reviews/2606.13681.md](2606.13681.md) (EvoArena/EvoMem), also reviewed earlier in this batch, directly and empirically engages the survey's Section 6.1.4 claim that "the conventional short-term/long-term distinction is too coarse for modern AI agents": EvoMem's patch-based memory design and its measured chain-level accuracy gains are a quantified answer to a problem this survey raises only as an open challenge. [reviews/2605.26302.md](2605.26302.md) (Agent Lifespan Engineering / AgingBench) similarly gives concrete, mechanism-level evidence for this survey's qualitative "workspace contamination" and memory-decay concerns (Section 4.2, Section 6.1.4).

## Critical discussion

The survey's central organizing contributions — the two-dimensional cognitive-core/tool-execution framing, the explicit Agent-vs-OpenClaw boundary table, and the Workspace + Skill thesis — are useful and, notably, more self-critical than many single-thesis position papers: Section 4.2's dedicated limitations discussion (skill brittleness and environmental drift, skill overfitting and negative transfer, workspace contamination and state inconsistency, security/supply-chain risk, governance overhead) candidly documents real, currently-unsolved problems with the very paradigm the paper is arguing for, rather than treating Workspace + Skill as a settled solution.

Two concerns temper how much weight the "OpenClaw era" framing specifically should carry. First, a substantial share of the evidence base supporting this era's existence and governance concerns (Table 5) consists of very recent, same-vintage sources clustered around a single named system and its immediate derivative ecosystem (OpenClaw, SemaClaw, Sema Code, ClawGuard, ClawSafety, ClawKeeper, ATBench-Claw, "Taming OpenClaw," "Don't Let the Claw Grip Your Hand" — nearly all dated 2026, several sharing an obvious naming lineage); the paper does not discuss whether this reflects a broad, independently-converged industry-wide transition or largely documents one product family's architecture and its immediate satellite literature, elevated in the periodization to the status of a distinct "era" alongside the much more broadly-attested Chatbot, Thinking-LLM, and Agent eras. Second, no literature-selection methodology is disclosed, so a reader cannot assess how the roughly 1,200-citation evidence base was assembled or how representative it is of the field as a whole, beyond trusting the 20 authors' collective domain judgment — a common and often unavoidable limitation for surveys in a fast-moving area, but one this paper does not name as a limitation of its own presentation the way it does for the Workspace + Skill paradigm itself. Relatedly, some of the survey's headline "current agents are fragile" evidence (WebArena's 14% GPT-4 success rate) is drawn from benchmark runs using models that are now several generations behind the frontier systems (GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.7) discussed elsewhere in the same paper, and the survey does not reconcile whether this specific fragility evidence still holds for current frontier agents or is being carried forward from a now-dated benchmark snapshot.

What remains solidly supported after these caveats: the two-dimensional framing and the explicit, checkable Agent-vs-OpenClaw classification criteria (Table 4) are genuinely useful organizing tools regardless of whether "OpenClaw" specifically becomes the field's dominant name for this transition; and the survey's own candid limitations analysis of Workspace + Skill (brittleness, negative transfer, contamination, supply-chain risk, governance cost) is a balanced, well-reasoned account of real open problems rather than an overclaimed solution narrative.
