Citation: Robbie Goldfarb, Andrew B. Hall, Jillian Fisher, Kathryn Salam, and Matt Wilde. Distilling Expert Judgment at Scale. Whitepaper (authors affiliated with Forum AI, Stanford University, and University of Washington; no venue, date, or URL stated in the document).

## What this paper is about

When AI models answer questions in high-stakes areas (health, defense, finance, politics), the useful question is whether their judgment is sound, and neither crowd raters, off-the-shelf LLM judges, nor narrow benchmarks such as refusal rates measure that well. The authors describe a pipeline for turning the reasoning of domain experts into written evaluation standards, collecting expert-labeled gold sets under those standards, and then tuning an LLM judge until it reproduces the expert labels. They test it on how AI models handle news and political content, for two dimensions: the quality of cited sources and the neutrality of the response. They report that two independent experts agree well when applying the standards, that the tuned judge matches the adjudicated expert labels with F1 around 0.8 to 0.97, and that rubrics invented by coding agents without expert input agree with experts much less.

## Extended summary

The paper is a methodology whitepaper with one validation study. Section 2 argues from the cognitive psychology of expertise that expert judgment is reliable in environments with learnable regularities and feedback (Kahneman and Klein 2009; Shanteau 1992), that unstructured expert prediction fails in domains without these properties (Tetlock 2005), and that structured procedures outperform unstructured clinical judgment (Meehl 1954; Dawes, Faust and Meehl 1989; Grove et al. 2000). The authors read this literature as a case for translating expertise into explicit procedures rather than for relying on intuition. They add an institutional argument: model developers cannot credibly evaluate their own products, and regulators rarely have the expertise to design and validate evaluation criteria.

The general method (Sections 3 and 4) has these parts.

- Experts are selected for evaluative judgment, not only domain knowledge, and complete calibration exercises on pre-scored and ambiguous cases before joining a panel. Panels are assembled to span professional and intellectual viewpoints. Structured interviews with senior practitioners (the text names former intelligence directors, hospital leaders, and executives of major financial institutions) total "several hundred hours" across domains and use an elicitation process adapted from cognitive task analysis (Klein 1998), grounded in concrete AI outputs and probing cues, comparisons, and counterfactuals.
- The authors report that experts from different backgrounds follow a broadly similar sequence: decide what kind of response the input warrants, check how the model handles the input's premises, look for domain-specific quality markers, then judge overall epistemic adequacy. A small group of internal experts turns the interview transcripts into editorial standards, each a categorical or ordinal scale plus a free-text rationale field, refined until external experts can apply them consistently.
- Test prompts come from three sources (real-world questions from forums and social media, expert-written adversarial prompts, and synthetic prompts that fill coverage gaps and create ideological companion pairs) and are characterized by domain, task type, and cognitive complexity (Table 1: nine policy domains; task types informational, perspective role-play, policy deliverables, and charged framings; complexity factual, interpretive, analytical, normative, and strategic). About 65 percent of the test set is held fixed across evaluation cycles; the core set is locked before each run. Standards and methodology are published (no URL given); test sets are withheld so that vendors cannot optimize for them.
- Gold sets are labeled by expert raters "largely distinct" from the standard authors (examples given: tenured scholars, journalists who have covered a beat for decades). Disagreements are adjudicated by a senior expert into one consensus label, and persistent disagreement patterns trigger revision of the standard. Deep disagreement classed as "reasonable pluralism" is escalated to a larger group, with majority vote if they cannot agree.
- Judges are built as simply as each task allows, with no fixed architecture. Intermediate results pass between steps as typed structured objects rather than prose, and steps that need no judgment (aggregating scores, applying deduction rules, enforcing constraints) are deterministic code. Calibration is an AI-assisted loop that targets the lowest-agreement standards; after each change the judge is re-scored on the gold set and a human inspects the execution trace. The standards are frozen once the labeling protocol is final, so calibration only moves the judge toward the labels. Iteration stops when agreement plateaus or reaches the human-human level.
- Two validation tests: inter-expert reliability via Krippendorff's α with target α ≥ 0.67, with rare classes oversampled; and judge versus adjudicated expert labels on a held-out test set via precision, recall, and F1, with target F1 > 0.70.

Application to AI news reporting (Section 5). The expert network draws on intelligence analysis, foreign policy, journalism, law, economics, and academia, plus a bipartisan advisory board; panel sizes and composition are not given. Three dimensions were defined (source quality, factual accuracy, neutrality). Factual accuracy, which requires claim extraction then claim verification, is described as at an earlier stage and not reported. The extract does not name the frontier models whose responses make up the test sets, the number of prompts, or any model-level results.

Source quality. Each citation is assigned one of six types (primary, scholarly, think tank, journalistic, commercial content, informal) and a binary state-controlled flag (RT, CGTN, Xinhua flagged; BBC, NPR, PBS, Deutsche Welle not flagged). An undisclosed expert formula combines them into a 0 to 100 response score. Two experts who did not help write the standards labeled sources independently, with senior adjudication; validation set n ≈ 230 (used for judge calibration), held-out test n ≈ 150. Table 2 (α with 2,000-resample item bootstrap 95% CI): source type 0.82 [0.76, 0.87] validation and 0.83 [0.76, 0.89] test; state-controlled 0.93 [0.80, 1.00] validation and 0.69 [0.52, 0.83] test. The authors attribute the wide test interval to the flag being rare. Table 3 (judge versus adjudicated labels on test): source type n = 146, macro precision/recall/F1 0.74/0.80/0.75 and weighted 0.86/0.79/0.81; state-controlled n = 147, macro 0.98/0.92/0.95 and weighted 0.97/0.97/0.97. The abstract's "F1 = 0.81/0.97" are the weighted figures. No intervals are given for F1.

Comparison with uncalibrated agents (Section 5.3, called "preliminary"). The expert-guided judge ("using Claude Code") is compared with two alternatives: Codex and Claude Code were each given a minimal task description plus example prompts, responses, and citations, but no gold labels, and asked to build a rubric for a continuous source-quality score, with one build phase and a single refinement iteration. Both produced their own 1 to 5 scales, so the comparison uses taxonomy-free metrics on the same test set: source ordering (Spearman ρ, whether sources are ranked as experts rank them), bad-source detection (Cohen's κ, where "bad" means commercial content, informal, state-controlled, or "out-of-core competency"), response-level agreement (Spearman ρ), and response-level MAE, with response scores averaged over the citations in a response. The metric definitions are partly cut off in the extract. Table 4 (bootstrap 95% CI): source ordering ρ 0.76 [0.67, 0.85] expert-guided, 0.27 [0.14, 0.39] Claude, 0.59 [0.49, 0.67] Codex; bad-source κ 0.79 [0.70, 0.86], 0.03 [-0.09, 0.14], 0.30 [0.17, 0.42]; response ρ 0.37 [0.26, 0.48], 0.14 [0.02, 0.26], 0.16 [0.04, 0.27]; response MAE 12.8 [11.4, 14.2], 28.9 [26.7, 31.1], 27.6 [25.5, 29.8]. The authors conclude that capability alone does not recover expert standards, that the gap on problematic sources suggests tacit distinctions hard to infer from descriptions and examples, and that targeted ablations of the expert contribution are future work.

Neutrality. Experts converged on a flowchart that first classifies the prompt (purely factual, directed, loaded, normative "should" questions, open-ended questions that may invite "nuance" framing) and routes it to type-specific named rules, each with pass/fail examples and substitution or removal tests. The verdict is binary ("approximate neutrality"). Stated principles include: faithfully executing a directed prompt is neutral and refusing a requested perspective is a failure; loaded premises must be corrected without overcorrecting; normative questions need balance without steering; "nuance" framing must be backed by genuinely complex content; asymmetric attribution and false equivalence are failures. Responses that fail get a secondary political-lean label, currently limited to U.S. left or right, with a none/other class. The authors say these are not the only or best definitions of neutrality. Human agreement (Tables 5 and 6): neutrality α 0.79 [0.66, 0.90] on validation (n ≈ 100) and 0.72 [0.60, 0.83] on test (n ≈ 160); political lean α 0.68 [0.55, 0.80] on validation (n ≈ 60) and 0.65 [0.51, 0.78] on test (n ≈ 100), just below the 0.67 target, which the authors attribute to the three-way label. Judge (Tables 7 and 8): neutrality n = 159, macro F1 0.85 and weighted 0.86; political lean n = 99, macro precision/recall/F1 0.72/0.76/0.73 and weighted 0.81/0.78/0.79. The authors state that lean performance is strongest on explicit left and right and slightly weaker on none/other; per-class numbers are not shown.

Author-stated limitations (Section 6): only two of three dimensions are validated; expert consensus is not objective truth, and whether a different equally qualified panel would give different targets is open; adjudicating every item to one label collapses ambiguity that may be meaningful, and they plan to annotate ambiguity and test whether models recognize when certainty is unwarranted; the expert network, standards, and taxonomy must be rebuilt for each domain, and the political benchmark is a proof of concept.

## Learnings

A judge that reproduces expert labels well at the item level can still track the expert's overall score poorly. Here the calibrated judge reaches weighted F1 0.81 on per-citation source type, yet its response-level score correlates with the experts' response-level score at only Spearman ρ 0.37. If a product reports the aggregate, validate the aggregate, not only the per-item labels.

The judge-building rules are a reusable discipline: pass typed structured objects between steps, put every non-judgment step (aggregation, deduction rules, constraints) in deterministic code, reserve LLM calls for steps that need judgment, have a human read the execution trace at each calibration iteration, and freeze the standards so that calibration moves the judge toward the labels and never the labels toward the judge. None of these choices is ablated in the paper, so their individual value is asserted rather than measured.

Rater disagreement is treated as a diagnostic of the standard, not only as noise to be averaged away. Persistent disagreement on one standard is read as an ambiguous definition or badly placed category boundaries and triggers a revision, while deep disagreement is escalated separately as reasonable pluralism. The authors concede that forcing one adjudicated label per item then discards ambiguity that may itself be informative.

Two coding agents given the same minimal brief built rubrics that differed widely in agreement with experts (source-ordering ρ 0.59 for Codex versus 0.27 for Claude Code; bad-source κ 0.30 versus 0.03). A label-free, agent-invented rubric is therefore a high-variance baseline, and a single run per agent says little about which agent is better at the task.

## Verification

The paper is about verifying AI outputs, although the checks cover source type and framing, not truth. What is checked is each citation's outlet type and state-control status, a binary neutrality verdict on the prompt-response pair, and, for non-neutral responses, the direction of lean. The verdict is issued by an LLM judge calibrated on expert gold labels; the aggregation into a 0 to 100 source score and other non-judgment steps are deterministic code. Ground truth is an adjudicated expert consensus, with a senior expert resolving two-rater disagreements and majority vote for unresolved pluralism cases.

Validation has two layers: expert-expert reliability (Krippendorff's α, target 0.67) and judge-versus-consensus agreement on held-out items (precision, recall, F1, target 0.70), on roughly 150 citations, 160 neutrality pairs, and 100 lean items. Calibration is AI-assisted, and a human inspects the trace of every iteration. There is no abstention path: neutrality is binary, and ambiguity is resolved into a single label by design, which the authors list as a limitation. The source-quality check does not ask whether a cited source supports the claim it is attached to. Factual accuracy (claim extraction, then verification against external evidence) is the dimension that would test truth, and it is explicitly not yet validated. Test sets are private and locked before each run, so the reported judge agreement cannot be re-measured externally.

## Field context

By the authors' own positioning, the work sits between three existing ways of supervising AI outputs at scale, all of which they consider inadequate for expert domains: crowdsourced rating, uncalibrated LLM-as-judge, and narrow instruments such as political-compass scores and refusal-rate benchmarks. They also position it against developer self-evaluations of sensitive-content handling and against regulator-designed evaluation. Their theoretical support comes from the expertise and decision-science literature (Kahneman and Klein; Shanteau; Tetlock; Chi, Glaser and Farr; Ericsson et al.; Dreyfus and Dreyfus; the Meehl clinical-versus-statistical line), from cognitive task analysis for elicitation (Klein 1998), and from content-analysis reliability (Krippendorff). A reviewer observation: the reference list contains no machine-learning work at all, with no LLM-as-judge validation studies, political-bias benchmarks, or developer self-evaluations cited by name. The paper's relation to that literature is therefore asserted, not documented. The document is undated; it was in this corpus's 2026-05-06 dump.

Within this corpus, the closest method match is reviews/26madrag.md, which also claims LLM-judge agreement at or above human-human agreement. Its review notes the same asymmetry present here: the judge is scored against a consensus target while humans are scored against each other. reviews/26scales.md also validates an LLM applying structured rubrics against a human consensus, but its annotators were the authors themselves, whereas here the gold-set raters are separate from the standard authors, which is a cleaner separation. reviews/26sycoph.md studies a related political construct (perspective sycophancy) with a GPT-4o judge validated at κ 0.63 against human labels, and reviews/2606.04075.md reports κ 0.55 for an LLM judge against legal annotators. This paper's judge-expert numbers are higher, but they come from narrower categorical tasks and use a different metric (F1 rather than κ). reviews/2609.13009.md, from a different domain (physics benchmarks) and using a different method (expert re-audit of rejected answers), found that automated graders and reference answers, not models, account for most recorded failures. That is independent support for this paper's premise that automated evaluation diverges from experts unless experts audit it, though it does not bear on this paper's specific judge.

## Critical discussion

The central comparative claim is stronger than the experiment behind it. The abstract says expert-calibrated judges outperform uncalibrated frontier models and that "encoding expert reasoning is the critical step that prompt engineering alone cannot replace," and Section 2.2 says "iterative prompt engineering does not close the gap." But the Section 5.3 baselines got a minimal task description, no gold labels, and a single refinement iteration. The expert-guided arm got the expert-written standards, the expert formula, calibration against the n ≈ 230 labeled validation set, and an open-ended AI-assisted refinement loop that ran until agreement plateaued. The expert arm is itself iterative prompt engineering with label feedback. The experiment therefore shows that standards plus labeled calibration beat label-free, one-pass rubric invention. It does not isolate "encoding expert reasoning," and it does not test iterative prompt engineering. The missing controls are straightforward: the same backbone given the written standards but no calibration, and the same backbone calibrated on the labels without the standards. The authors call the comparison preliminary and list ablations as future work (L108), which is honest, but the abstract and conclusion (L142) do not carry that hedge (weak-baseline, missing-control, overclaim).

The bad-source κ gap (0.79 versus 0.30 and 0.03) is partly definitional. "Bad" is defined by the expert taxonomy as commercial content, informal, state-controlled, or out-of-core competency, and the baseline agents were never told these categories count as bad. The extract does not say how each agent's 1 to 5 scale was converted into a bad-source flag, or how 1 to 5 scores were mapped onto the 0 to 100 range used for MAE, and the metric definitions are partly lost in extraction. "Out-of-core competency" also appears nowhere in the two source-quality standards described in Section 5.2. The authors' reading, that expert standards encode distinctions agents cannot infer, is consistent with the result, but in part the result reduces to "a system told the expert definition matches the expert definition better" (incomplete-reporting).

The claim that judges reproduce expert consensus "at levels comparable to inter-expert agreement" (L142) is not shown on a common scale. Section 3.4.3 says judge calibration is scored with the same Krippendorff's α so that human-human and human-judge reliability can be compared directly, but Section 5 reports only precision, recall, and F1 for the judge. F1 against an adjudicated consensus label and α between two individual raters are different quantities, and the consensus is a less noisy target than either rater, so parity cannot be read off Tables 2 to 8. The headline F1 values are support-weighted. For the state-controlled flag, which the authors say is rare in the test set, weighted F1 0.97 mostly reflects the majority class, and the macro 0.95 rests on a positive count that is not reported. No F1 carries a confidence interval, and each baseline agent was run once, so the bootstrap intervals in Table 4 capture item sampling but not run-to-run variation in the rubric an agent invents (missing-uncertainty).

The most product-relevant number is also the weakest. The response-level source-quality score, the thing a published evaluation would report, correlates with experts at ρ 0.37 [0.26, 0.48] even for the expert-guided judge, with MAE 12.8 on a 0 to 100 scale. The paper frames this as gains that "persist" over the baselines, which is true, but it does not explain why good per-citation agreement yields modest response-level agreement (range restriction, the averaging rule, or the formula). For political lean, the human α of 0.65 misses the paper's own threshold. The statement that the judge "preserves uncertainty in gray-area cases rather than overconfidently forcing ambiguous examples into partisan labels" (L134) would need per-class results or a confusion matrix, and neither is shown.

Reporting is thin in ways that limit what the numbers mean. The extract does not name the models whose responses were judged, the LLM behind the judge beyond "using Claude Code," the number of prompts, the class balance of the neutrality test set, the size or political composition of the panels, or the source-score formula. Rare classes were deliberately oversampled for Test 1 (L61), so it is unclear whether the test sets match the deployment distribution, and precision in deployment may differ from what is reported. Test sets are private by design. The paper's own argument that evaluators need "visible independence" (L27) applies here too: every validation number is self-reported by the organization that builds and operates the judge, on data no one else can inspect. The rationale for keeping test sets private (preventing vendor optimization) is reasonable, but the cost is that independent replication is not possible. The evaluation covers two dimensions of one domain, U.S.-centric political news, with test sets of about 100 to 160 items and two expert raters per item. The generalization to healthcare, defense, and finance is a design intention, not a result (narrow-evaluation). Finally, the literature in Section 2 cuts both ways. The Meehl line shows mechanical rules beating unstructured experts, and Tetlock shows poor expert political prediction. The authors resolve this by arguing for structured procedures, but the claim that the Kahneman-Klein conditions (learnable regularity plus feedback) "map precisely" onto neutrality judgments is asserted with "arguably" and "may," not demonstrated.

What remains supported: on a private held-out set of about 150 citations, two external experts apply the six-way source-type standard with α 0.83, and a label-calibrated LLM judge reproduces the adjudicated labels with weighted F1 0.81 (macro 0.75). On about 160 political prompt-response pairs, experts apply a flowchart-based binary neutrality standard with α 0.72, and the judge reaches F1 about 0.86. A judge built from expert standards plus gold-label calibration agrees with experts far better than rubrics that coding agents invent in one pass without labels. The pipeline description (separating standard authors from gold-set raters, freezing standards during calibration, deterministic non-judgment steps, trace review) is a transparent and sensible recipe. The stronger claims (that expert reasoning, rather than labeled calibration, is the decisive ingredient; that judge reliability matches human reliability; that the approach generalizes across domains) are not established by this document.

## Relevance to us

The paper supports, from an unrelated domain, the construction rule we already hold for verification: an LLM should orchestrate and judge only where judgment is unavoidable, with typed objects between steps, deterministic code for everything checkable, and a human reading the trace. It adds a concrete protocol we lack for the parts of physics checking that no deterministic procedure covers, such as whether an approximation is justified or whether a derivation smuggles in an assumption. Elicit expert standards from concrete model outputs, have separate experts label a gold set, measure expert-expert α first, and only then calibrate a judge against held-out adjudicated labels. For a B12-style graded stack, this is a reasonable minimum bar for any LLM-judged layer. The paper's own numbers also carry the warning: per-item categorical agreement was good, while the holistic response score reached only ρ 0.37.

Two cautions carry over. First, the source-quality check classifies outlet type and says nothing about whether a source supports the claim it is attached to. In our citation cluster, that is the easy layer; claim-evidence support corresponds to their unvalidated factual-accuracy dimension. Second, the missing control is the one we would need to run ourselves (B01): a frontier model given the written standards but no label calibration. If most of the gain comes from the standards, it is cheap for others to copy once published. If it comes from the expert-labeled gold set, that is the hard-to-copy asset, which matches this paper's choice to publish standards and withhold test sets.

The authors' planned direction, preserving and annotating expert ambiguity instead of forcing one adjudicated label and testing whether models recognize when certainty is unwarranted, points at the calibration and abstention signal our interviews emphasized. Their current binary, fully adjudicated design is the opposite of our "undecided is fine" rule, and a physics version would need an explicit contested or undecidable label from the start.
