Citation: Villaescusa-Navarro, F., Bolliet, B., Villanueva-Domingo, P., Bayer, A.E., et al. The Denario project: Deep knowledge AI agents for scientific discovery. arXiv:2510.26887, 2025.

## What this paper is about

This paper introduces Denario, a publicly-released, modular multi-agent system built to act as an AI research assistant across essentially any scientific discipline: given a description of a dataset or problem, it can generate a research idea, check whether that idea is already known in the literature, design a detailed research plan, write and execute the code to carry it out, produce plots, draft a full scientific paper, and even referee that paper, either as separate on-demand tools or chained together into a fully autonomous, end-to-end research pipeline. Rather than testing the system on a synthetic benchmark, the authors demonstrate it by generating complete draft papers across thirteen genuinely different scientific fields (from astrophysics to biology to pure mathematics), have domain experts score the outputs, and candidly discuss where the system succeeds, where it fails outright, and what ethical and philosophical questions a tool like this raises for how science gets done.

## Extended summary

Denario is built from six modules, each a distinct multi-agent subsystem implemented using one of two open-source orchestration frameworks (AG2, via the authors' own prior CMBAgent system, and LangGraph): an Idea module (a "propose-critique" loop between an idea-maker agent and an idea-hater agent that iteratively refines a research idea, with prior work cited showing such multi-agent idea generation is judged more novel than either single-agent or human-generated ideas); a Literature module (checking novelty against Semantic Scholar or FutureHouse's Owl agent, kept deliberately separate from the pipeline so a human decides whether an idea is truly original); a Methods module (producing a detailed research plan); an Analysis module (writing and executing code, producing plots and a results report, using a "Planning & Control" strategy borrowed from robotics, where a plan is decomposed into subtasks executed and monitored by dedicated engineer and researcher agents with automatic retry and package-installation handling on failure); a Paper module (drafting a full LaTeX paper across four progressively-refined versions, including automated figure captioning and, optionally, literature-grounded citation insertion via a Perplexity-based agent); and a Review module (an LLM-based referee that reads the rendered PDF and produces a structured critique and a 0-9 quality score). These modules can be run independently or chained end-to-end from a single natural-language input description, and the system supports a wide range of interchangeable underlying LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash, Claude 4/4.5, etc.) chosen per-task based on the authors' own informal trial-and-error rather than a systematic model comparison.

The paper's central empirical content is thirteen end-to-end generated papers, one each in mathematical physics, planetary science, medicine/health services research, chemistry, two in astrophysics, biology, digital health, biophysics, neuroscience, materials science, and one interdisciplinary case combining deep learning and quantum physics applied to cosmological data, most produced with little to no human intervention beyond writing the initial input description. For each, the paper reports the actual prompt given, discusses what Denario produced, and offers the authors' own qualitative assessment of strengths and weaknesses; one case (a materials-science paper on graphene-water interfaces) is explicitly highlighted as a "human-in-the-loop" example where the input prompt was iteratively refined using feedback from a domain expert. In the featured mathematical-physics example, given raw latent-space data from a physics-informed neural network trained on Burgers' equation, Denario independently applied PCA and subspace-similarity analysis to conclude the network had learned a 3-dimensional solution manifold varying smoothly with viscosity; the authors note the system also exposed a mistake in their own prompt (it correctly recognized that an index the authors had mislabeled as "different initial conditions" was actually different viscosity values) rather than uncritically reproducing what the prompt implied, which they read as evidence of some robustness against being led into an incorrect framing.

Beyond the showcased successes, the paper documents two detailed, explicitly negative case studies as "severe failure modes." In a cyclic-peptide structure-generation task, after repeated failed attempts to implement a genuine numerical solver, the system's paper-writing agent produced a complete paper claiming success, with plots purporting to show valid results, when in fact no correct solver existed in the code and the paper's own description of the method was vague; the authors describe this as effectively "hallucinated" success and stress that verifying underlying code and data, not just reading the resulting narrative, is essential when evaluating agent-generated science, exactly as it would be for suspect human-generated work. In a pure-mathematics case (an open problem about representing simplicial complexes as nerves of ball collections), five independently generated papers were analyzed in detail and found to exhibit only the surface form of mathematical writing (titles, "main theorem" language, superficially plausible-sounding definitions) without any actual proofs, coherent logical structure, or correctly-used citations; one invented technical condition turned out to be trivially true or false depending on interpretation, and the authors conclude the system can currently imitate the rhetorical form of mathematics without its actual logical substance.

For evaluation, the authors report on two forms of check. First, an "exhaustive validation" is illustrated with two examples: the peptide failure case above (where multiple internal agents, including the reviewer, failed to catch the fabricated result), and a positive counter-example where a domain-expert team member independently reimplemented, without access to Denario's own code, the calculations behind one of the astrophysics papers and reproduced closely matching results, offered as evidence that at least in that one case an independent human following the paper's description could arrive at the same scientific conclusion. Second, for overall "evaluation" (assessing paper quality and interest rather than strict correctness), the authors had in-house domain experts assign each of the featured papers a single subjective score from 0 to 10, reporting a distribution skewed above the "normal paper" midpoint of 5, with several papers scored 8 or 9, and three below average; the authors explicitly caveat this scoring as informal and not intended as a rigorous evaluation criterion.

The Discussion section (Sec. 6) further lists self-identified, ongoing limitations: citation quality (relevant but often not the most relevant papers, and an expert reader can reportedly spot a Denario paper from its citation choices alone), shallow interpretive depth relative to an experienced human researcher (the authors compare current output quality to "a good undergraduate or early graduate student"), occasional LaTeX/formatting errors, and code execution failures from API or package version drift. A long Ethics section (Sec. 7.1) raises specific, concrete concerns: that easy paper generation could be used to inflate publication counts or citation numbers; that widespread reliance on such tools without validation risks trusting incorrect or discarded results uncritically; a specifically observed "homogenization" pattern, where the system struggles to propose genuinely novel research questions or notice serendipitous findings, tending instead toward research that confirms or extends its training-data literature; unequal computational access shaping which researchers and fields benefit; open questions about how to attribute authorship and responsibility for AI-assisted or AI-generated work; risk of a "deluge" of low-quality papers under misaligned publishing incentives; substantial environmental cost from LLM training and inference; and the risk of deliberate, large-scale generation of misleading literature for political or commercial ends. A further section (Sec. 7.2-7.4) situates these developments within philosophy-of-science frameworks (Lakatos's research programmes, Quine's holistic epistemology, Kuhn's normal-science/paradigm-shift distinction, Feyerabend's epistemological anarchism), arguing that LLM-driven science could plausibly push toward either genuine paradigm-shifting pluralism or a homogenizing, self-reinforcing "perpetual normal science," depending on how such tools are designed, deployed, and incentivized.

## Learnings

The paper's most transferable systems-design insight is the specific value of a dedicated propose-critique agent pair for idea generation (idea-maker paired against a separately-instructed idea-hater agent that must find weaknesses before an idea proceeds), a concrete, reusable pattern for any multi-agent system aiming to avoid uncritically accepting a first plausible-sounding output, directly motivated here by, and consistent with, prior findings the authors cite that multi-agent idea generation is judged more novel than single-agent generation.

A second, more cautionary and highly transferable learning comes from the paper's own documented failure modes rather than its successes: the cyclic-peptide case demonstrates a specific, concrete mechanism by which an agentic pipeline can produce a confidently-written, plot-supported paper describing a result that was never actually computed, because the paper-writing stage is not architecturally forced to verify that the underlying code actually implements and executes the claimed method; this is a directly reusable warning for anyone building or evaluating similar end-to-end agentic research pipelines, that a separate, code-execution-grounded verification step (not just a text-level "reviewer" agent reading the narrative) is necessary to catch this specific class of failure.

A third, more conceptual learning from the pure-mathematics case study is a sharp, well-illustrated distinction between imitating the surface form of a domain's discourse (correct-sounding jargon, standard section structure, plausible-looking definitions) and actually participating in that domain's substantive practice (real proofs, genuine logical necessity, correctly-contextualized citation); this distinction, demonstrated concretely here for mathematics, is a useful general lens for assessing LLM-generated content in any domain where formal rigor, rather than merely fluent prose, is the actual criterion for validity.

## Verification

The paper takes a genuinely candid approach to reporting its own system's failures rather than only its successes, devoting a dedicated subsection to two specific, detailed negative case studies (the fabricated peptide-solver result and the pure-mathematics imitation-without-substance case) described in enough concrete detail that a reader can judge the nature and severity of the failure directly, rather than being told only that "limitations exist." The one reported independent-reproduction check (a domain expert reimplementing an astrophysics analysis from scratch, without access to Denario's own code, and obtaining closely matching results) is a genuine, meaningful verification step precisely because it withholds the system's own implementation from the checker, guarding against merely re-running the same code and getting the same numbers by construction. The domain-expert scoring exercise (Fig. 11) at least provides a distributional, rather than purely anecdotal, summary of output quality across the featured papers, and the authors are explicit that this score is subjective and not a rigorous evaluation criterion, an appropriately hedged framing rather than an overstated quantitative claim.

Verification is comparatively thin in a few specific, consequential places. The domain-expert quality scores in Figure 11 do not report who scored which paper, whether scorers were drawn from the paper's own author list or genuinely external domain experts, whether scoring was blinded to any details of how each paper was produced, or any measure of inter-rater agreement; given that many of the featured domains map closely onto the professional expertise of the paper's own 36 co-authors, it is plausible that a meaningful share of this evaluation was performed by people with a direct stake in the system's success, a conflict-of-interest risk the paper does not address or rule out. The single independent-reproduction check, while methodologically sound in its own right, is performed for only one of the thirteen featured end-to-end papers, so it establishes that this kind of verification is possible and that at least one output survived it, but does not establish what fraction of Denario's outputs would survive similarly rigorous, code-independent reproduction if it were applied systematically. Relatedly, the paper does not disclose how the thirteen featured example papers were selected from the presumably much larger and more varied set of runs the authors performed while developing and testing the system (which, based on the failure-modes section, clearly includes many unsuccessful or unusable outputs); without a stated selection protocol, a reader cannot tell whether these thirteen examples are a representative sample of typical Denario output or a curated set of the system's more successful runs, which matters directly for interpreting how the mostly-above-average score distribution in Figure 11 should be read.

## Field context

The paper explicitly and extensively positions itself within a lineage of automated-research systems it traces back decades before the current LLM wave, citing the Adam and Eve robot scientists (which physically executed genomics and drug-discovery experiments) and the Automated Statistician as important early precedents, and situates itself among a cluster of very recent LLM-based systems it distinguishes itself from directly: Sakana AI-Scientist, Google's AI co-scientist, AI-cosmologist, Curie, and Agent Laboratory, as well as more narrowly-scoped agentic tools like AutoWEKA, ResearchAgent, and AstroAgents. Denario's specific claimed differentiation is breadth (explicitly demonstrated across roughly a dozen distinct scientific disciplines within a single unified, modular architecture, rather than one field or one pipeline stage) combined with public availability and a deliberately modular design that supports both fully autonomous end-to-end use and human-in-the-loop use of individual modules. Within this corpus, Denario is a direct, concrete instantiation of exactly the multi-agent, full-lifecycle "AI co-scientist" category of system that [2510.09901] (the "Autonomous Agents for Scientific Discovery" survey, reviewed earlier in this batch) taxonomizes at a more abstract level; reading the two together, Denario's own self-reported qualitative assessment that its output quality resembles "a good undergraduate or early graduate student" and its documented pure-mathematics failure case (fluent form without logical substance) offer a concrete, hands-on illustration of exactly the "Knowledge Closure" limitation that survey argues constrains LLM-based reasoning engines, that an agent confined to recombining and restating patterns in its training data struggles specifically at the kind of genuinely novel, rigor-demanding reasoning pure mathematics requires. The paper's own "homogenization" concern (Sec. 7.1) and its Kuhnian worry about AI-driven science collapsing into "perpetual normal science" (Sec. 7.3.2) are also directly, empirically consistent with the large capability gap for genuinely open-ended, unpublished research reasoning documented quantitatively in [2509.26574] (CritPt, reviewed earlier in this batch), which found even the best frontier models solve only 5.7-12.6% of genuinely novel physics research challenges; Denario's live-system experience and CritPt's controlled benchmark numbers offer two independently-derived but mutually reinforcing pictures of the same underlying gap between fluent, well-formatted scientific output and genuinely novel, rigorously-grounded scientific reasoning.

## Critical discussion

The paper's greatest strength, relative to many system-demonstration papers in this space, is its candor: rather than only showcasing polished successes, it devotes real space to two detailed, damaging failure cases (fabricated results in the peptide task, and mere stylistic mimicry without mathematical substance in the pure-math task), explicitly recommends that agent-generated science be checked with the same rigor as suspect human work, and raises a wide-ranging, specific, and largely self-critical set of ethical concerns (homogenization, incentive misalignment, authorship ambiguity, environmental cost, malicious use) rather than a perfunctory ethics-statement checklist. The one genuine independent-reproduction check, performed without giving the reproducer access to the system's own code, is a methodologically meaningful piece of evidence rather than a circular self-check.

The clearest limitation is the evaluation methodology underlying the paper's central positive claim, that Denario can produce papers a domain expert would rate as reasonable or good: the scoring exercise behind Figure 11 does not disclose whether the scoring experts were independent of the 36-author development team, whether scoring was blinded, or how consistent different experts' scores were with each other, all of which matter directly given how plausible it is that scorers with domain expertise closely matching a co-author's own specialty were themselves involved in the project. This is not necessarily evidence of a biased result, but the paper's own framing ("we asked the domain experts in our team to evaluate them") suggests at least some scorers were project-internal, and without more detail a reader cannot rule out that the reported quality distribution partly reflects evaluator investment in the project's success rather than a purely arm's-length assessment. A second, related limitation is that the paper does not disclose how the thirteen featured papers were selected out of what was very likely a much larger and much more mixed set of attempts (the failure-modes section alone documents at least two additional, non-featured negative case studies encountered during development), so the true, representative success rate of an arbitrary Denario run on an arbitrary new research problem remains unknown from this paper alone; the thirteen examples demonstrate what the system can do under at least some conditions, but not how often it does so. A third, more moderate point is that the single reported independent-reproduction check, while methodologically sound, covers only one of the thirteen examples, so while it is genuine evidence that this kind of verification can succeed for at least one output, it does not by itself establish what fraction of Denario's broader output would similarly withstand independent, code-blind reproduction.

What remains well supported after these caveats: Denario is a real, working, publicly-released, and genuinely broadly-applicable multi-agent system capable of producing complete, plausible-looking draft scientific papers, with real generated code, plots, and text, across a demonstrated range of at least a dozen distinct scientific disciplines, at a small fraction of the time and monetary cost of human-led research; the system's own documented failure modes (fabricated results without a working underlying implementation; fluent stylistic imitation without logical substance in a domain requiring formal rigor) are genuine, specific, and non-hypothetical phenomena the authors themselves observed and chose to report rather than omit; and the broader ethical and epistemic concerns the paper raises about homogenization, validation burden, and incentive misalignment in a world where such tools become widespread are substantive, well-articulated, and largely unresolved rather than dismissed.
