## What this paper is about

If a language-model assistant learns a fact about you — your cardiologist's name, an allergy — where should that fact live? The dominant "write it into the weights" recipe is a per-user LoRA adapter, but this paper shows that approach quietly breaks a fact into every unrelated conversation the model ever has, because a LoRA edits shared functions (the Q/K/V/O projections), not an addressed location. Borrowing the brain's trick of keeping a fast, local memory trace (the hippocampus) separate from the slow, shared skill of using it (the neocortex), the paper writes each user's facts as surgical edits to specific rows of a content-addressable memory table (the "Engram" architecture) and keeps the general reasoning skill in one adapter shared across all users — and shows this combination recalls facts as well as a per-user LoRA while reasoning over them far better, contaminating nothing, and costing about 33,000x less collateral damage to unrelated text.

## Extended summary

The paper builds on Engram (Cheng et al. 2026), a sparse-memory architecture in which, at a few designated transformer layers, the recent tokens' suffix N-gram is hashed by K deterministic multiplicative-XOR heads into addresses in a large embedding table; the retrieved rows are projected and gated by an attention-style scalar, then added to the residual stream only at the position whose N-gram matches. Because the address is a deterministic function of token IDs (known before the forward pass), the table is a genuine content-addressable store rather than "more parameters." Since no Engram-pretrained weights are public, the author trains four "Mini-Engram" replicas from scratch (178M to 1.22B parameters, on a single GPU) by grafting the Engram mechanism into Karpathy's nanochat GPT-2-style backbone, and reproduces Engram's two signature findings from the original paper (a factual-recall-specific sensitivity to suppressing the lookup, and effective added depth via a LogitLens probe) at appropriately smaller magnitude for the smaller scale.

Section 3 establishes the core motivating problem with a controlled comparison on a fixed base (Mini-Engram-d20, 1.22B): training a per-user LoRA (rank-64 on Q/K/V) to hold a fact raises held-out-text loss on text unrelated to that user by +1.784 bits/byte on average (17/20 users worse), while writing the identical fact as an Engram-row edit changes that same held-out loss by only +0.00005 — a ~34,000x difference in this specific comparison (~33,000x as a 3-seed mean), attributed to the fact that a LoRA "cannot write a fact without bending a function every input shares," whereas an Engram row is read only when its specific trigger fires. A second, separate problem is that a per-user LoRA recalls its own facts well but reasons over them poorly (a "recall-without-reasoning" gap the paper attributes to the adapter memorizing a flat trigger-to-answer mapping from a handful of examples rather than acquiring generalizable reasoning); this damage is visible on a base LM but is masked on strong instruction-tuned bases (Qwen2.5-3B/7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B) whose own reasoning already carries indirect questions, illustrating that base strength controls whether the underlying damage is observable at all rather than whether it exists.

Section 4 describes how a fact is written: its trigger's suffix N-gram deterministically hashes to a small, fixed set of rows (about 16 for the paper's N=3, K=8 configuration), and one of three increasing-cost strategies (a closed-form matrix-vector solve; a few gradient steps per fact; or "Joint OPT," optimizing all of a user's rows together, the recommended default beyond ~30 facts/user) sets the value written into those rows. The paper verifies the mechanism directly on the trained model rather than only arguing it architecturally (Section 4.3, Figures 7-8): writing a fact opens the gate at exactly its trigger position (from 0.02 to 0.99) while leaving every other position's gate near-zero; the residual-stream change at every non-trigger position, at every layer, is exactly 0.000 for all 16 test facts under both writing strategies, whereas a LoRA trained on the identical single fact shifts the residual at every position and every layer, including unrelated text ("The capital of France is") by a mean of 107; and writing the same facts into an early rather than late Engram layer drops recall from near-perfect to about a quarter, confirming the late-layer placement is functionally necessary rather than incidental. A per-user override table is served via a ~50-line server (EngramServer) that swaps in a user's small (row-index, row-vector) override map, runs the forward pass, and restores the originals, giving zero cross-user leakage by construction and throughput/latency that barely move as the tenant count triples (232 to 226 req/s from 30 to 100 users on the ablation-optimal model, at 88 KB/user).

Section 5 characterizes the bare per-user store before adding the shared skill. Within-user fact density has a ceiling: Joint OPT tracks a multi-fact LoRA baseline to within a few top-5 points and keeps top-5 recall above 90% out to ~300 facts/user at up to ~161x less storage, but degrades further out (35% top-1 at 1,000 facts). Three ablations triangulate the cause: scaling the dense backbone at fixed Engram capacity does not lift recall at 1,000 facts (and slightly hurts it); per-fact independent optimization (no shared table pressure) shows no ceiling to 1,000 facts; and a multi-fact-in-the-loss finetune only reaches the same asymptote faster. The remaining common factor — gradient interference among co-active rows within one user's table at a fixed inference-time optimization budget — is identified as the actual cause, ruling out backbone capacity, table/hash capacity, and pretraining as explanations. Against retrieval-based memory systems (simplified Mem0-like and MemMachine-like baselines implementing only their core retrieval step, not their full extraction/consolidation pipelines, on the stated grounds that the paper's facts are already structured), retrieval wins on verbatim-prefix queries but at a real context-token cost Engram does not pay (16-63 tokens vs. zero), while multi-trigger Engram insertion (writing a fact at multiple anticipated phrasings) wins by 22 points on paraphrased queries at zero context cost. On the published LOCOMO conversational-memory benchmark (10 conversations, ~800 single-hop QA pairs), Engram Joint OPT beats every retrieval baseline on token-F1, with the lead widening at larger Mini-Engram scale — but an LLM-judge rescoring (Qwen2.5-14B) flips the single-hop ranking in retrieval's favor by 0.05-0.10, because token-F1 over-credits a correct first answer token; training the whole answer with multi-token Joint OPT is reported to fix this and again overtake retrieval "on the larger models." Across LOCOMO's categories, Engram wins single-hop, multi-hop, and reasoning questions but loses open-domain questions, where the answer is a verbatim span of an unseen sentence a one-token nudge cannot reconstruct. A direct multi-hop chaining probe (two stored facts, a question requiring both) finds per-fact recall near-perfect (99.2%) but true chaining recall collapsing to 12.9% when the query doesn't happen to share surface words with the second fact's trigger (versus 90.6% when it does) — the store "matches words, it does not reason across them."

Section 6 introduces the paper's layered design: one shared LoRA trained once on cross-user in-context-reasoning examples from a held-out population (rendering each indirect QA as "Facts: ... Q: ... A: ..." completions), paired at inference with the test user's own Engram-row overrides and no facts in the prompt. Across a controlled six-condition comparison on 20 test users (base model; per-user LoRA; per-user Engram alone; naive per-user LoRA+Engram stack; shared LoRA alone; the layered design), the layered design matches per-user LoRA's direct recall (100% vs. 99%) while reaching 5.6x higher indirect-reasoning accuracy on average across three seeds (44% vs. 6% on the canonical seed), with contamination equal to the shared LoRA's alone (i.e., the per-user Engram adds zero additional damage on top), and not a single one of the 20 test users made worse than the untouched base model (versus 17 of 20 for per-user LoRA); the gap is reported with a paired-bootstrap 95% CI of [+31, +37] percentage points. Naively stacking a per-user LoRA with an Engram on top does not rescue the LoRA's reasoning damage, confirming the paper's hypothesis that the LoRA's contamination, not a missing store, is the actual bottleneck. Against retrieval as the knowledge base grows (adding distractor facts to reach 34 to 1,000 facts per user), the layered design's accuracy is invariant to knowledge-base size (its per-user table only ever holds that user's own facts), while retrieval-based conditions degrade monotonically as the candidate pool grows, crossing over and falling below the layered design once the knowledge base passes roughly 100 facts, even when the retrieval condition runs on an instruction-tuned model about 2.5x larger.

Authors' stated limitations (Section 8): the store shares per-user LoRA's inability to compose facts across triggers for genuine multi-hop reasoning; the within-user density ceiling persists at high fact counts; the method requires an Engram-pretrained base, which the author had to train from scratch at nano-scale since no production Engram weights are public; and a negative result is reported directly — training the shared skill on teacher-distilled reasoning traces reduced contamination but also lowered accuracy, because those traces assumed an explicit "thinking" step the paper's own scoring protocol does not read. No external funding is disclosed; the Acknowledgements name a GPU-hosting provider and disclose the use of AI coding tools during the research.

## Learnings

The core design principle — that a personalization system should store *content* (specific per-user facts) and *reasoning skill* (how to use facts in general) via architecturally different mechanisms, an addressed, content-keyed local edit for the former and a shared, globally-applied adapter for the latter, rather than folding both into one per-user weight delta — is a specific, transferable pattern for building any personalization system, directly informed by (and offering an engineering-verified instantiation of) complementary-learning-systems theory from neuroscience (a fast, local hippocampal trace paired with a slow, shared neocortex).

The mechanistic verification that a content-addressable memory-table edit changes literally zero residual-stream values outside its trigger position, at every layer, while a LoRA edit for the identical single fact perturbs every position at every layer including unrelated text (Figure 7), is a rare case of a locality claim that is directly and fully falsifiable on the actual trained model rather than only argued architecturally. This is a specific, reusable methodological bar for evaluating any proposed "local edit" mechanism: check the literal per-position, per-layer change, not just parameter count or aggregate held-out loss.

The density-ceiling triangulation method — systematically testing and ruling out three plausible causes (backbone capacity, table/hash capacity, and pretraining) for why per-user recall degrades as more facts are packed into one table, before concluding the actual cause is gradient interference among co-active rows at a fixed inference-time optimization budget — is a specific, transferable diagnostic technique for isolating the true bottleneck of any capacity or scaling ceiling, rather than assuming the most obvious culprit (usually "the model is too small") without directly testing it.

## Verification

This paper's verification practices are unusually rigorous for a method paper. It does not stop at architectural argument for locality; it directly measures the claim on the trained model (Figures 7-8) and reports the result held for every one of 16 test facts under both writing strategies. Its density-ceiling analysis triangulates the true cause via three separate ablations rather than accepting the first plausible explanation. Its headline layered-design result is reported with 3-seed means and a paired-bootstrap 95% confidence interval, not a single point estimate. It also transparently reports results that complicate its own story rather than only favorable ones: the multi-hop chaining collapse (Section 5.6), the open-domain LOCOMO loss (Table 2), the token-F1-versus-LLM-judge disagreement on LOCOMO single-hop (with the stated fix validated only "on the larger models," leaving its generality across all four trained scales somewhat open), and a negative result from a teacher-distillation variant (Section 8.3). Two limitations temper how far the specific numbers generalize. First, every experiment runs on self-trained "Mini-Engram" replicas at 178M-1.22B parameters, because no production-scale Engram-pretrained weights are publicly available; the paper is explicit about this constraint, but the specific multipliers reported (the ~33,000x contamination ratio, the 5.6x indirect-reasoning gain, the ~161x storage reduction) are demonstrated only at this nano-scale and are not claimed by the paper itself to hold unchanged at production scale. Second, the retrieval baselines (Mem0-like, MemMachine-like) are explicitly simplified to their core retrieval step rather than their full published extraction-and-consolidation pipelines, a disclosed choice justified by the paper's facts already being structured, but one that could favor the paper's own method somewhat relative to a comparison against the complete competing systems.

## Field context

The paper positions itself along two axes among personal-memory methods (Figure 2): whether a method pays context tokens at query time (in-context learning, retrieval, external memory systems) and whether it edits model weights globally (per-user LoRA, knowledge editing); User as Engram claims the previously-unoccupied corner of local weight edits at zero context cost. It explicitly engages the per-user-adapter literature (PRAG, DyPRAG, T2L) as the family whose "recall-without-reasoning" gap it reproduces and explains, and the retrieval literature (Mem0, MemMachine, LOCOMO/LongMemEval/BEAM/PersonaMem-v2 as benchmarks) as its comparison baselines. Within this corpus, the most substantive connection is [reviews/2606.02437.md](2606.02437.md) ("On the Scaling of PEFT: Towards Million Personal Models of Trillion Parameters"), which argues small per-instance LoRA adapters can serve as a scalable, isolated "local state" unit for personalization atop a shared foundation model (its "Scale Down" pillar). User as Engram's Section 3 findings sharply qualify that vision for the specific case of storing personal content in a LoRA: measured directly on a trained model, a per-user LoRA is not locally isolated (it changes behavior on unrelated text by roughly four orders of magnitude more than an addressed memory-table edit for the same fact), which directly challenges the implicit assumption that LoRA-style adapters alone deliver the isolation and composability that scalable per-user personalization requires, and offers a concrete architectural alternative (content-addressable, hash-keyed memory rows) that empirically achieves that isolation property where a LoRA does not. A secondary connection: [reviews/2606.13681.md](2606.13681.md) (EvoArena/EvoMem) and [reviews/2606.17162.md](2606.17162.md) (MemSlides), both reviewed earlier in this batch, argue for separating different functional types of agent memory (a patch history versus consolidated state; profile/working/tool memory) rather than using one undifferentiated store — User as Engram's content-versus-skill split implements a structurally similar principle one level deeper, at the level of model weights themselves rather than an external memory module wrapped around a frozen model.

## Critical discussion

The paper's central mechanistic claim is unusually well-supported: the locality of an Engram-row edit versus the globality of a LoRA edit is verified directly on the trained model, not merely inferred from architecture, and the density-ceiling analysis rules out alternative explanations through direct ablation rather than assuming the most obvious one. The layered content/skill separation's headline result is backed by a genuinely controlled six-condition comparison with reported seed-level uncertainty (3-seed means, bootstrap confidence intervals), and the paper is candid about every failure mode it finds (multi-hop chaining collapse, open-domain LOCOMO loss, a metric-dependent single-hop result, a negative distillation experiment) rather than presenting only favorable numbers.

Two limitations temper how far the specific reported magnitudes should be trusted beyond this paper's own setting. First, because no production-scale Engram-pretrained weights are public, every result is obtained on self-trained "Mini-Engram" replicas at 178M-1.22B parameters — a reasonable and disclosed workaround, but one that means the paper's most quotable numbers (the ~33,000x contamination ratio, the 5.6x indirect-reasoning multiplier) are nano-scale findings whose exact magnitude at production scale is not established here, even if the underlying mechanistic argument (addressed writes versus global function-bending) is architecture-general rather than scale-specific. Second, the retrieval baselines the paper compares against are explicitly simplified to their retrieval step alone rather than the full Mem0/MemMachine systems with their extraction and consolidation machinery, a choice the paper justifies but that narrows how directly "beats retrieval" should be read as "beats the complete published systems." A smaller, honestly-surfaced complication is that the LOCOMO single-hop win depends on the scoring metric (token-F1 favors Engram; an LLM judge favors retrieval on three of four tested scales before a multi-token training fix, whose success is reported only for "the larger models" rather than confirmed across every scale tested).

What remains solidly supported after these caveats: the core claim that an addressed, content-keyed memory-table write can be made provably and directly-verified local, while a same-fact LoRA edit is measurably global, is about as rigorously demonstrated as a claim of this kind can be within a single paper; and the layered design's practical payoff — matching per-user LoRA's direct recall while substantially improving indirect reasoning and eliminating cross-user contamination, with no user among the 20 tested made worse than the untouched base — is supported by a controlled, uncertainty-quantified experiment, even though its exact numeric size should be read as specific to the nano-scale replicas studied rather than a validated production-scale figure.
