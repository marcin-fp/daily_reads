Citation: Runpeng Dai, Kaili Huang, Changsung Kang, and Ciya Liao. It Takes Two to Match: Co-Evolving Generative Retriever with Reinforcement Learning. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00638v1

## What this paper is about

Search and recommendation retrieval usually works by having an LLM improve the query side (expanding, rewriting, or generating pseudo-documents) while a separate, frozen retriever still does the actual matching on the item side. This paper asks what happens if you train LLMs to generate the retrieval representation on both sides at once — a query-side LLM and an item-side LLM, each producing a compact keyword set, matched directly through an ordinary inverted index (so the resulting system stays compatible with existing keyword-based search infrastructure) — and let the two sides improve against each other rather than one chasing a fixed target.

## Extended summary

**Method.** CoGR trains two separate generators, G^q (query-side) and G^i (item-side), each producing a keyword set; an item is retrieved for a query whenever their keyword sets overlap (plus the query/item text itself), and BM25 over the generated keywords ranks the resulting candidates. Training has two phases. Phase 1 (SFT) bootstraps an aligned keyword space: item keywords are sampled from the base LLM, then each query's target keyword set is built by pooling the item-side keywords of that query's known-relevant items and keeping the most frequent ones — directly engineering keyword overlap between relevant query-item pairs before any RL begins, so RL starts from a policy with real recall rather than needing to discover overlap from scratch. Phase 2 (co-evolving RL) alternates GRPO updates between the two sides: query-side RL rewards a sampled keyword set by the retrieval F1 it achieves against the current frozen item-side index; item-side RL rewards a candidate keyword set for item i by a counterfactual marginal contribution — the change in aggregate query-side F1 caused by swapping only item i's keywords into an otherwise-frozen index, isolating that item's own causal contribution to retrieval quality (and cheap to compute, since queries whose retrieval outcome is unaffected drop out of the calculation). Each round rebuilds the opposite side's index from the latest policy, so the two keyword spaces progressively adapt to each other across five alternating rounds.

**Evaluation setup.** Two industrial-style datasets: an internal, de-identified APP Marketplace dataset (13,500 train / 1,500 validation queries, 39,600 apps, ~1,000 relevant items per query) and the public WANDS product-search benchmark (430 train / 50 validation queries, ~43,000 products, ~200 relevant items per query) — both explicitly chosen because they have dense, many-relevant-items-per-query annotations rather than the sparser single-relevant-document setting typical of many IR benchmarks. Ten baselines span sparse (BM25, SPLADE-v2), dense (DPR, ANCE, zero-shot and ANCE-fine-tuned Qwen3-Embedding-4B), and generative retrieval (DSI, DSI-QG, RIPOR, DeepRetrieval — the last a direct query-rewriting RL method closely related to CoGR's query-side-only ablation). Baseline cutoffs are tuned on the training split and evaluated on validation.

**Main results.** CoGR-4B achieves the best F1 on both datasets: 0.3963 on Internal (vs. 0.3575 for the strongest baseline, ANCE-Qwen4B, a 10.9% relative gain) and 0.6819 on WANDS (vs. 0.5012 for the same strongest baseline, a 36.1% relative gain). An item-frozen ablation (CoGR°, optimizing only the query side while keeping item keywords fixed after SFT) and DeepRetrieval (query-rewriting only) both substantially underperform full CoGR, supporting the claim that co-adapting both sides — not just the query side — is necessary. Training dynamics (Figure 3) show validation F1 rising from ~0.16 immediately post-SFT to ~0.40 after five co-evolving rounds, with the largest single gain in the first round.

**Ablations and analysis.** Replacing the counterfactual item-side reward with a symmetric "transposed F1" (treating each item as its own query against relevant queries), sharing one generator for both roles, or removing the SFT initialization each individually degrades F1 relative to the full recipe, though all three variants remain stable and reach reasonable performance on their own — the authors read this as evidence the overall co-evolving framework is robust to specific design choices even as each contributes incrementally. A vocabulary-dynamics analysis shows keywords becoming more specific over training (unigram share falling from 37% to 13%, three-plus-word phrases rising from 12% to 31%) and the query- and item-side vocabulary sizes converging after three to four rounds. A separate ablation on available context shows removing item descriptions (leaving only titles) hurts F1 (0.3963 → 0.3759), while adding existing search-engine results into the query-side prompt substantially helps (0.3963 → 0.4379), with the gain attributed mainly to resolving ambiguous, misspelled, or non-English queries before keyword generation.

## Learnings

The counterfactual, difference-based item-side reward (swap only one item's keywords into an otherwise-frozen index and measure the resulting change in aggregate query-side F1) is a specific, reusable technique for assigning credit to one component of a joint retrieval system when the natural objective (aggregate retrieval quality) is a property of the whole index, not of any single item — and the paper notes it is also computationally cheap, since queries whose outcome is unaffected by the swap drop out of the calculation, avoiding a full re-evaluation per candidate.

Bootstrapping the RL stage with an SFT phase that mechanically engineers keyword overlap between known-relevant query-item pairs (rather than leaving RL to discover any overlap from a cold start) is a transferable pattern for any two-sided or multi-agent RL setup built on a sparse, structurally-defined success signal (here, keyword-set intersection): giving the policy enough initial signal density to receive informative rewards before RL begins is a distinct and separately useful design choice from any properties of the RL algorithm itself.

The finding that co-evolving both sides clearly outperforms optimizing only the query side (the standard practice in most cited prior work) while a single generator playing both roles, or removing the marginal reward, or skipping SFT each still produce a "stable and reasonable" system is a useful characterization of where the framework's gains actually concentrate: the core co-evolving idea (two sides adapting to each other) does more work than any one specific implementation detail of it.

## Verification

The central reward signal (retrieval F1 against labeled query-item relevance) is a genuine, non-circular, human-labeled ground truth rather than an LLM-judged or self-graded score, which sidesteps the same-family-judge and judge-reliability concerns present in much of this batch's other RL-for-agents work. The ablation study is reasonably thorough, testing the reward formulation, the shared-vs-separate generator design, and the SFT initialization independently, and the co-evolving dynamics are shown as a training curve (Figure 3) rather than only a final-checkpoint number, which lets a reader see that the largest gain is front-loaded in the first round rather than assuming a smooth, unexplained improvement. What is not verified or reported anywhere in the extracted text: any confidence interval, variance estimate, or repeated-run/seed result for the main Table 2 comparisons, and no significance test accompanies the headline 10.9%/36.1% relative-improvement figures.

## Field context

CoGR extends three lines of retrieval work it explicitly distinguishes itself from: classical sparse/dense retrieval (BM25, SPLADE, DPR, ANCE), generative retrieval that replaces similarity search with autoregressive identifier generation (DSI, GENRE, SEAL, RIPOR, and related identifier-design variants), and the more recent line of LLM-and-RL-for-retrieval work (DeepRetrieval and related query-rewriting-via-RLVR methods) that the paper argues uniformly trains only the query side while still delegating final matching to a separate, frozen downstream retriever. Its stated contribution — training generators on both sides to co-adapt directly in the representation space used for matching, while preserving compatibility with existing inverted-index infrastructure — is, per its own Related Work, conceptually related to self-evolving/self-play LLM systems (cited: G-Zero, R-Zero) where interacting roles improve against each other's evolving behavior, though CoGR's two roles (query-side and item-side generators) are asymmetric and serve a fixed retrieval objective rather than open-ended self-play. No paper currently in this corpus addresses generative or co-evolving retrieval specifically, so this is a novel connection point within the corpus.

## Critical discussion

The paper's use of a genuine, labeled-relevance F1 objective as the RL reward — rather than an LLM judge or self-graded score — is a meaningful methodological strength relative to much of the surrounding literature on RL-trained generative agents, and the counterfactual item-side reward is a specific, well-motivated solution to a real credit-assignment problem (how to reward one item's keyword choice when the metric being optimized is a property of the whole retrieval system). The ablation coverage (reward formulation, shared-vs-separate generators, SFT initialization) is more thorough than a bare main-results table would suggest.

Two things temper how confidently the headline numbers should be read. First, the public, independently reproducible benchmark (WANDS) has only 50 validation queries, and the paper's most dramatic reported improvement (+36.1% relative F1) comes from exactly this small evaluation set; with no confidence interval, variance, or repeated-split result reported anywhere for either dataset, it is not possible from the paper alone to judge how much of this specific margin would survive a different validation split or evaluation run, and the more heavily-powered internal dataset (1,500 validation queries) is proprietary and not independently checkable by outside readers (`narrow-evaluation`). Second, and following directly from the first point, no statistical test or uncertainty estimate accompanies either headline relative-improvement figure, so the precise magnitude of CoGR's advantage over the strongest baseline (ANCE-Qwen4B in both cases) should be read as this specific pilot's outcome rather than as an established, tightly-bounded effect size (`missing-uncertainty`).

What remains well supported after these caveats: the mechanistic claims — that co-evolving both sides outperforms optimizing the query side alone (directly demonstrated via the item-frozen ablation and the DeepRetrieval comparison), that the counterfactual reward and SFT initialization each contribute incrementally (shown via controlled ablations that isolate one design choice at a time), and that the two keyword vocabularies genuinely become more specific and mutually aligned over training (shown via direct vocabulary statistics, not inferred from the F1 curve alone) — are each backed by a targeted, interpretable piece of evidence rather than resting solely on the top-line comparison table.
