Citation: Wu, S., Chen, Z., Liu, Y., Cui, H., Li, C., Yan, R. Your UnEmbedding Matrix is Secretly a Feature Lens for Text Embeddings. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07502v1

## What this paper is about

Large language models are surprisingly bad at one very simple job: producing a single vector that captures a sentence's meaning well enough to power semantic search or clustering. Prior fixes have mostly been prompt-engineering tricks (asking the model to "summarize in one word") that work inconsistently. This paper instead asks why LLM embeddings are bad in the first place, and finds a specific, mechanistic answer: when you project a raw LLM sentence embedding back onto the vocabulary (a standard interpretability trick called the Logit Lens), it lights up mostly on common, semantically empty words like "the" or "and" rather than words related to the actual sentence. The paper traces this to a specific slice of the model's own unembedding matrix — the linear layer that turns hidden states into next-token logits — and shows that simply projecting that slice out of the embedding, with no training or calibration data required, measurably improves embedding quality and, as a bonus, shrinks the embedding's dimensionality for free.

## Extended summary

The authors start from two established observations: LLM sentence embeddings are anisotropic (crowded into a narrow cone rather than spread through the embedding space), and, per the paper's own Logit Lens experiments across three model families (Qwen2.5-0.5B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3), those embeddings decode overwhelmingly to high-frequency, uninformative tokens regardless of input content. They hypothesize the narrow anisotropic cone corresponds to an "average" token — the frequency-weighted average embedding over the (undisclosed) pretraining corpus, following prior work's characterization — and reverse-engineer this average token analytically: using the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of the unembedding matrix's transpose, combined with empirical word-frequency statistics estimated from the RedPajama corpus (since the actual pretraining corpora are not public; the paper states, without showing supporting data, that alternative corpora give "identical results"), they compute an approximate "average token" hidden-state vector.

Applying Logit Spectroscopy (an existing tool that projects a representation onto individual right-singular-vector directions of a weight matrix) to this reverse-engineered average token, the paper measures how much each singular direction of the unembedding matrix's SVD contributes to boosting the logits of the 100 most frequent corpus tokens (a metric they call Delta-pi). They find Delta-pi is concentrated at the two ends of the singular-value spectrum — both the largest and the smallest singular values — rather than uniformly distributed, and term this the "edge spectrum" subspace; a control analysis (Appendix Figure 4) shows infrequent and randomly-sampled tokens are far less sensitive to this same subspace than frequent tokens are.

EmbedFilter is the resulting fix: a fixed linear transformation, built entirely from the unembedding matrix's already-trained weights with no additional training or calibration data, that projects any embedding onto only the "bulk" (mid-range singular value) subspace, discarding the edge-spectrum directions. Because the singular vectors are orthonormal, this projection is provably distance-preserving (proof given in Appendix B), so it doubles as a lossless-in-similarity dimensionality reduction — the embedding's dimension shrinks to 1/tau of the original, directly cutting index storage and speeding up similarity search by a corresponding factor. Applied on top of two prompt-based embedding-extraction baselines (PromptEOL, ECHO) across the three backbones on the MTEB benchmark (semantic similarity, classification, clustering, pair classification, reranking, retrieval, summarization — retrieval evaluated on an 8-dataset subset for compute reasons), EmbedFilter improves the average MTEB score in every one of the 6 tested backbone-baseline combinations, by up to 14.1% relative (ECHO + Qwen2.5-0.5B), and these gains largely persist even at an 8x dimensionality reduction (tau=8). The same improvement transfers to two more sophisticated prompting pipelines (MetaEOL, GenEOL) without modification. An ablation (Table 5, Qwen2.5-0.5B + PromptEOL, tau=2) directly tests whether the gain is just from having fewer dimensions: naive truncation and random-dimension-subsampling controls, which also halve the embedding size, both underperform the unfiltered baseline, ruling out dimensionality reduction alone as the explanation. The same ablation compares filtering the dominant (largest-singular-value), secondary (smallest-singular-value), and bulk subspaces individually: bulk-filtering (EmbedFilter) performs best, the reverse operation (keeping only the edge spectrum) performs worst, and an oracle "optimal" configuration that filters exactly the directions with the highest measured Delta-pi comes close to but does not exceed EmbedFilter's simpler, calibration-free design. Compared against BERT-whitening, a classical calibration-based anisotropy fix that requires a labeled NLI dataset, EmbedFilter outperforms it (54.57 vs. 53.04 average MTEB score on Qwen2.5-0.5B) without using any external calibration data, and the paper explicitly frames EmbedFilter as approximating a whitening-like effect for free within the model's own bulk spectral subspace. Dimensionality-reduction results (Table 4) show that with EmbedFilter, Llama-3.1-8B's embeddings compressed to 512 dimensions can outperform pre-LLM-era supervised baselines (SimCSE, coCondenser) that use 768 dimensions. ## Learnings

The core transferable technique is methodological rather than just the specific fix: applying an existing interpretability tool (Logit Spectroscopy) not to a real, observed activation but to an analytically reverse-engineered "probe" representation (here, a corpus-frequency-weighted average token derived via the unembedding matrix's pseudo-inverse) is a reusable way to isolate which weight-space subspace is responsible for a specific, undesirable systematic bias — in this case, over-representation of frequent tokens — without needing to run the model at all on real inputs to find the direction.

The specific mechanistic claim — that a weight matrix's extreme singular-value directions (both largest and smallest, not just one end) disproportionately encode frequency-driven, low-information structure, while the mid-range "bulk" spectrum carries more input-specific semantic content — is a concrete, falsifiable hypothesis about where frequency bias lives in a trained model's weights, distinct from the more common assumption (tested and rejected here via the ablation) that only the top singular directions matter.

The observation that a training-free, calibration-free linear fix (derived purely from weights the model already has) can beat a classical calibration-based baseline (whitening, which needs an external NLI dataset) is a useful data point for the broader "post-hoc embedding calibration" literature: it suggests some of what calibration methods learn from external data may already be recoverable directly from the model's own parameters, at least for the specific anisotropy/frequency-bias problem addressed here.

## Verification

The paper's strongest verification is its ablation design (Table 5): rather than only reporting that EmbedFilter improves scores, it isolates the specific mechanism by comparing against dimensionality-matched but non-informed controls (naive truncation, random dimension subsampling) that fail to reproduce the gain, directly ruling out "it's just fewer dimensions" as the explanation, and by comparing filtering the dominant, secondary, and bulk subspaces separately (with an oracle "optimal, highest-Delta-pi" configuration as an upper bound) to show the bulk-spectrum choice specifically, not any arbitrary subspace removal, is what drives the improvement. The head-to-head comparison against BERT-whitening under matched conditions is also a fair, direct test against a real established baseline rather than only against the paper's own prompt-engineering baselines. The main verification gap is the claim that "parallel experiments on alternative corpora produce identical results" for the reverse-engineered word-frequency proxy (used because the actual pretraining corpora of Qwen/Llama/Mistral are not public) — this specific robustness claim is stated but no supporting table, figure, or number for the alternative-corpus experiments appears anywhere in the paper or its appendices, so a reader cannot independently assess how sensitive the core "edge spectrum" finding is to this necessarily-approximate frequency estimate.

## Field context

This paper sits in the "LLMs as zero-shot embedding models" literature it reviews directly (PromptEOL, ECHO, MetaEOL, GenEOL, LLM2Vec) and specifically distinguishes its contribution from that line by seeking a mechanistic rather than heuristic explanation for LLMs' embedding weaknesses, building on established interpretability tools (Logit Lens, Logit Spectroscopy) and prior anisotropy diagnoses (BERT-whitening, the "narrow cone" observation) rather than proposing a new prompting template. No closely related work on text-embedding mechanistic interpretability, anisotropy, or logit-lens-style analysis appears elsewhere in this reviewed corpus, so this paper introduces a distinct sub-topic rather than extending a thread already covered in this batch.

## Critical discussion

The paper's core empirical claim is well supported by its own evidence: the ablation study directly and successfully rules out the most obvious alternative explanation (dimensionality reduction alone), the effect replicates consistently across three different model families and two different baseline prompting strategies with a wide range of filtering ratios, and the comparison against a real calibration-based baseline (whitening) is conducted under matched, fair conditions rather than cherry-picked.

Two aspects invite caution. First, the paper's abstract and framing describe the frequent-token bias as "a universal pattern inherent to LLMs" on the strength of three open-weight decoder-only model families spanning 0.5B-8B parameters — a real and consistent pattern within that sample, but a narrower evidentiary base than the word "universal" implies, since no encoder-based, mixture-of-experts, or larger frontier-scale model is tested (the latter being an understandable practical limitation, since interpretability access to unembedding-matrix weights requires an open-weight model, but one the paper's framing does not explicitly flag as a scope boundary). Second, the specific claim that the reverse-engineered average-token analysis is robust to the choice of frequency-estimation corpus is asserted without any shown supporting result, which matters because the entire "edge spectrum" discovery pipeline depends on this frequency estimate as its starting point; without the promised alternative-corpus data, a reader must take this particular robustness claim on faith rather than verify it, even though the paper's other empirical claims are generally well-substantiated. What remains solidly supported regardless of these two caveats: within the three tested model families, projecting LLM-derived text embeddings onto the mid-range ("bulk") singular-value subspace of the model's own unembedding matrix — a specific, weight-derived, training-free, calibration-free operation — measurably improves zero-shot MTEB performance over both prompt-engineering baselines alone and a classical calibration-based alternative, while simultaneously and provably preserving pairwise distances at reduced dimensionality.
