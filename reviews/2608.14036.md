Citation: Zhiyuan Jiang, Fangrui Huang, Hanwen Xing, Xander Wu, Yipeng Gao, Rui Cao, Mengdi Wang, Shilong Liu, and Yijiang Li. Demystifying Agent Skills: Why They Work-Until They Don't. arXiv preprint, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14036v1

## What this paper is about

Agent "skills" (Anthropic's format: a packaged, reusable procedure an LLM agent can load) are usually evaluated only by whether they raise aggregate task success — which tells you skills help but not why, or when they stop helping. This paper opens that black box: it runs controlled experiments isolating how the same prior experience behaves when packaged as a skill versus as raw workflow memory, builds a human-validated taxonomy of what a skill actually does inside a trajectory (stabilize a procedure? inject a missing fact? mislead the agent?), and separately studies what happens to skill retrieval and use as a skill library grows and gets more confusable.

## Extended summary

**Setup.** Two controlled study families. (1) Skill-vs-workflow-memory (RQ1-2): for each task, the authors collect real successful/failed trajectories, build a fixed-budget composition grid (from 5 successes/0 failures to 0/5), and distill the identical trajectory pool into either a Workflow Memory or a SKILL.md, evaluated on matched tasks under Terminal-Bench-2, SkillsBench, and Terminal-Bench-Pro, with Codex+GPT-5.3-Codex and Gemini CLI+Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview as the two agent-model pairings. A "no-hint" variant removes success/failure labels from the source trajectories before skill construction, isolating whether skills help via outcome signal or via procedural content. (2) Retrieval (RQ4): SkillsBench tasks paired with controlled candidate pools (size 5-100, random/similar/dissimilar distractors), evaluated three independent ways — embedding-based ranking (Arm 1), explicit agent selection without execution (Arm 2), and full-pool real execution with post-hoc skill-use parsing (Arm 3) — with outputs from Arms 1-2 never passed to Arm 3.

**Taxonomy construction.** From 8,135 normalized trial records (7,837 with transcripts), the authors open-code 240 sampled trajectories into 238 valid unique labels, consolidated into a 12-mode taxonomy under 3 categories (SC1 successful procedural anchoring, SC2 execution/verification failures, SC3 invocation/applicability failures). A human annotator independently checked all 238 labels against 3 supporting trajectories each (714 checks, all confirmed) and independently remapped the labels to the 12 canonical modes, agreeing with the LLM-assisted mapping at 95.8% exact match, Cohen's κ=0.952. The taxonomy is then applied via LLM judge to 528 paired (raw/workflow/skill) triples across the three benchmarks (1,584 arm-level assignments), the main dataset behind the paper's headline percentages.

**Key findings.** Skill beats Workflow Memory by +6.06 percentage points on oracle-status success (61.9% skill vs. 55.9% workflow vs. 59.1% raw), 95% bootstrap CI [+0.76, +11.36] — notable because both are built from the identical trajectory pool, so the gain is attributable to representation, not more data. Mechanism labels show skills work mainly via procedural_anchor (65.7% of skill-arm mechanism labels) rather than knowledge_injection (4.5%). Skills cut execution-layer failures (SC2: 23.5% skill vs. 37.3% raw vs. 33.3% workflow; environment-infrastructure failures specifically drop from 5.3%→0.2%), but introduce a new failure mode largely absent elsewhere: skill_guidance_misapplied_or_ignored appears in 10.0% of skill-arm cases vs. 0.8% (raw) and 0.4% (workflow), while workflow memory's distinctive failure is timeout_budget_exhaustion (10.6% vs. 1.7% raw, 4.4% skill) from carrying unfiltered exploration noise. Two lightweight non-skill baselines (an instruction-derived short plan, 47.7%; a workflow-derived test-first template, 59.2%) both underperform Workflow Memory (62.3%) and substantially underperform Skill (79.2%) on a matched 26-task subset, ruling out "any compact procedural hint" as the explanation for skill gains. Outcome-hint removal barely matters when the source pool is success-only but hurts sharply once failures are included (e.g., Gemini at 3s2f: 0.7462 with hints vs. 0.4000 without). Distilled skills built in Codex transfer to Gemini CLI, beating that framework's own raw baseline by up to +30 points depending on trajectory mixture. On retrieval: as pool size grows 5→100, embedding-retrieval precision falls 88.3%→76.9%, explicit agent-selection precision falls 70.0%→63.7%, but actual-use precision in real execution collapses much more sharply, 29.6%→3.3% — while downstream task success stays comparatively flat (36.4%→39.3%). Semantically similar distractors are the dominant stressor for offline identification (similar-pool Arm-1 precision falls to 53.4% at k=100 vs. 84-93% for random/dissimilar pools), while Arm-3 recall stays high (54-74% at k=100) even as precision collapses, meaning agents often use several candidates including the ground-truth skill without restricting to it, and downstream success is not a simple function of exact-match retrieval accuracy.

**Self-stated limitations.** The authors' own Limitations section: coverage is restricted to terminal/tool-use benchmarks (not web interaction or open-ended collaboration); only a limited number of agent-model configurations were tested; and the mechanism taxonomy is induced from a stratified open-coding sample covering roughly 3% of the normalized records, so rare behavioral modes may be underrepresented. Elsewhere (Appendix A.3) the authors also note the full 528-triple, 1,584-assignment dataset that produces the headline percentages remains LLM-assisted rather than exhaustively human-coded, distinct from the smaller upstream sample that was directly human-validated.

## Learnings

The paired-triple design (same task, same source trajectories, three representations: raw/workflow/skill, judged for both outcome and mechanism) is a reusable methodology for attributing an agent-augmentation's effect to a specific behavioral mechanism rather than only an aggregate success delta — directly transferable to studying other "compress prior experience into reusable form" designs beyond Anthropic-style skills.

The clearest actionable finding is the shape of the trade-off: skills reduce operational/infrastructure failures sharply but create a new failure mode (misapplying or over-generalizing guidance to a context it doesn't fit) that barely exists for raw execution or workflow memory. This means skill quality is not just "does it capture a working procedure" but "does it also signal the conditions under which it doesn't apply" — a design implication (skills should include applicability boundaries, not just steps) that follows directly from the taxonomy's SC3 category rather than being asserted a priori.

Retrieval precision and downstream task success are shown to be genuinely decoupled measurements, not two stages of one pipeline: exact ground-truth skill retrieval collapsing to single-digit precision at scale does not imply downstream failure, because agents draw partial procedural support from related non-ground-truth skills. This is a useful corrective against evaluating a skill library purely by top-1 retrieval accuracy.

## Verification

Central and unusually rigorous for a taxonomy-based qualitative-plus-quantitative study: the taxonomy's construction is independently human-validated at two separate stages (grounding of raw labels in trajectories, and mapping of labels to canonical modes), with concrete agreement statistics (95.8% exact, κ=0.952) rather than an assumed-reliable LLM judge. The headline representation effect (skill vs. workflow memory) is bootstrap-confidence-interval reported, not a bare point estimate. A dedicated control (two lightweight non-skill procedural baselines) directly tests and rejects the alternative explanation that any compact text hint would produce the same gain. The weaker link, which the authors flag themselves, is that this validation covers only the taxonomy's construction (the 238-label, 3%-sample open-coding stage); the full 528-triple dataset that generates every headline mechanism percentage in Section 5 is applied by an LLM judge without a matching independent human check at that scale.

## Field context

This paper sits inside the broader LLM-agent memory literature (episodic memory, workflow memory, procedural-memory management, agent memory surveys) and the newer skill-specific literature it cites (SkillsBench, SWE-Skills-Bench), but its distinguishing move is methodological: rather than adding another skill-construction method or another aggregate-success benchmark, it holds the underlying experience fixed and varies only its representation, then explains the resulting gap mechanistically. This is directly relevant, in a self-referential way, to how this corpus's own review pipeline operates: this repository's `.agents/skills/` directory (paper-review, evidence-chains, learning-synthesis, and others, per [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md)) is itself an instance of the "skill" abstraction this paper studies. No other paper in this corpus specifically evaluates skill mechanisms or retrieval degradation at scale, so this is a novel connection rather than a corroborating or contradicting one against prior corpus reviews.

## Critical discussion

The central representational claim (skill beats workflow memory when built from identical source trajectories) is well isolated by design and backed by a genuine control against the "any compact text helps" alternative explanation, which is a stronger evidentiary standard than most skill-evaluation papers meet. The taxonomy's construction is also unusually well validated by human spot-checking with reported agreement statistics rather than asserted.

Three things temper how far the paper's more granular percentage claims can be pushed. First, the fine-grained mechanism breakdown that most of Section 5's specific numbers rest on (procedural_anchor at 65.7%, SC2/SC3 percentages per arm, the skill_guidance_misapplied rate of 10.0%) comes from an LLM judge applying the taxonomy to the full 528-triple dataset, and only the taxonomy's construction on a much smaller 238-label/3%-sample subset was independently human-validated — the paper is transparent about this distinction in its own text, but the practical effect is that the specific percentages readers are most likely to quote are one step removed from direct human verification (`same-family-judge`). Second, the core controlled experiments use only two agent-model pairings for RQ1-3 (Codex+GPT-5.3-Codex, Gemini CLI+Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview), and RQ4 had to substitute GPT-5.4 for GPT-5.3-Codex mid-study due to API availability — handled with appropriate caveats by the authors (RQ4 results explicitly marked as not comparable across RQs), but the overall generalization claim still rests on a narrow set of scaffold/model combinations that the authors' own Limitations section acknowledges (`narrow-evaluation`). Third, the paper's single most citable number — the +6.06pp skill-over-workflow-memory gain — carries a 95% bootstrap CI of [+0.76, +11.36], a fifteen-fold span between its lower and upper bound; the main text states the point estimate prominently without foregrounding how much of that interval sits close to zero, which matters for anyone deciding how strongly to weight this specific comparison (`missing-uncertainty`).

What remains well supported after these caveats: skills measurably reduce a specific, identifiable class of operational failures (environment setup, output formatting, service lifecycle) relative to both raw execution and workflow memory, this is not explained away by a generic "compact procedural text" control, and skills introduce a real, distinct failure surface (misapplied or over-generalized guidance) that workflow memory and raw execution do not exhibit at the same rate. The retrieval finding that execution-time skill-use precision collapses far faster than downstream success is also a clean, direct empirical result independent of the taxonomy's validation limitations, since it is measured from parsed execution logs and verifier outcomes rather than LLM-judged mechanism labels.

## Relevance to us

This paper's findings map directly onto this repository's own skill-based operating model (`.agents/skills/`, symlinked between Cursor and Claude Code per [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md)). Two findings are worth carrying forward as design checks on our own skills: (1) skills work primarily through procedural anchoring rather than knowledge injection, which argues for keeping skills like `paper-review` and `evidence-chains` structured as compact, checkable procedures (as they already are, per SCHEMA.md/CLAIMS.md-style reference docs) rather than dense reference prose; (2) the paper's SC3 finding — that skills specifically introduce a misapplication failure mode (guidance followed mechanically outside its valid context) at a rate roughly 10-25x higher than raw or workflow-memory execution — is a concrete argument for keeping this corpus's skill descriptions in `AGENTS.md`'s routing table ("Use when...") explicit about applicability boundaries, and for continuing the existing practice of routing ambiguous cases back through the most specific matching skill rather than letting a nearby skill "mostly work." The retrieval-degradation finding (precision collapsing under semantically similar distractors as a pool grows) is a forward-looking caution rather than an immediate concern at this corpus's current skill-count (currently 10 skills, well below the k=50-100 range where the paper's collapse becomes severe), but is worth revisiting if the skill set grows substantially or skill names/descriptions start to overlap semantically (e.g., `learning-synthesis` vs. `perspectives` vs. `trend-analysis`, which the routing table already needs to disambiguate on scope/audience rather than topic).
