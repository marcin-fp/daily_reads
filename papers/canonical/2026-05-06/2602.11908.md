# **ABSTRACT** 

LLMs are widely used, yet they remain prone to factual errors that erode user trust and limit adoption in high-risk settings. One approach to mitigate this risk is to equip models with uncertainty estimation mechanisms that abstain when confidence is low. However, this binary “allor-nothing” approach is excessively restrictive in long-form settings, often discarding valuable information. We introduce **_Selective Abstraction (SA)_** , a framework that enables LLMs to trade specificity for reliability by selectively reducing the detail of uncertain content. We first formalize SA through the lenses of selective risk and coverage. We then propose **_Atom-wise Selective Abstraction_** , a claim-level instantiation that decomposes responses into atomic claims (short, self-contained statements each expressing a single fact) and replaces uncertain atoms with higher confidence, less specific abstractions. To evaluate this framework, we develop a novel end-to-end pipeline for openended generation that instantiates risk as factual correctness and measures coverage using an information-theoretic measure of retained information. Across six open-source models on the FactScore and LongFact-Objects benchmarks, atom-wise SA consistently outperforms existing baselines, improving the area under the risk-coverage curve (AURC) by up to 27.73% over claim removal, demonstrating that reducing specificity can boost accuracy and reliability while preserving most of their original meaning.

Despite these advances, LLMs remain prone to factual errors, generating content that appears fluent and coherent, but is factually incorrect or unsupported by underlying knowledge [18, 40, 16, 22]. As LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and law, factual mistakes can erode user trust and limit adoption. Ensuring trustworthy generation is therefore a central challenge for their reliable real-world use. 

A widely studied approach to this problem is uncertainty estimation, which aims to quantify a model’s confidence in its outputs. In classification, uncertainty estimates support selective prediction [7], where the model predicts only when confidence is sufficiently high and abstains otherwise. In text generation, a similar idea appears as _selective generation_ [34], which allows the model to abstain from answering when confidence is low. This approach is effective for short outputs such as question answering, where it is reasonable to treat the response as a single decision. However, selective generation does not scale well to long-form generation. Long responses often contain many factual claims with varying levels of uncertainty, 

sometimes within a single sentence. For example, the sentence “Amelia Earhart was an American aviator born on July 24, 1897” bundles multiple claims about profession, nationality, and date of birth. An LLM may be uncertain about the exact date while remaining confident about the month, the year, or any of the other details. In a long-form setting (e.g.: generating an entire biography, which may consist of dozens of sentences), abstaining from the entire generation is excessively restrictive, as it results in no content being generated at all. Even removing an entire sentence can discard valuable content. This highlights the need for a more fine-grained approach. 

In long-form generation, a common failure mode is a single overconfident detail within an otherwise reasonable answer. In February 2023, Alphabet lost roughly $100 billion in market value in the wake of Google’s Bard debut, as a high-profile demo response drew scrutiny for a factual mistake<sup>1</sup> . Asked about James Webb Space Telescope discoveries, Bard confidently claimed JWST took the “very first pictures” of an exoplanet, an assertion off by nearly two decades. Had Bard recognized uncertainty about whether JWST was “first” and instead generated “JWST has taken pictures of exoplanets” or “JWST has contributed important exoplanet discoveries”, the response would have remained informative while avoiding the hallucination. This incident illustrates a critical insight: not all information carries equal risk. Being overly specific in the wrong places can have dramatic consequences, while targeted abstraction, here removing just two words, maintains usefulness without sacrificing reliability. 

We introduce **_Selective Abstraction_** , a framework that enables LLMs to reduce the amount of information in longform generations when faced with uncertainty. Rather than relying on binary all-or-nothing decisions, Selective Abstraction retains informative content by reducing the specificity of uncertain parts, yielding outputs that are less specific but more reliable. 

**The key contributions of this paper are as follows:** 1. We introduce **Selective Abstraction** , a general framework for trading specificity for reliability in long-form text generation, formalized through selective risk and coverage. 

2. We propose **atom-wise selective abstraction** , a claim-level instantiation that selectively replaces uncertain claims with higher-confidence abstractions rather than removing them entirely. 

3. We develop an **end-to-end evaluation pipeline** for selective abstraction in open-ended factual generation, instantiating risk via factual correctness and coverage via an information-theoretic measure of retained content. 

4. We empirically demonstrate, across six open-source LLMs and two long-form factual benchmarks, that **atomwise selective abstraction consistently improves the**

# **2 Related Work** 

**Selective Prediction.** We view selective prediction as a starting point for formalizing Selective Abstraction. In classic selective prediction [7, 12], a classifier may abstain when uncertain. Formally, a selective model is a pair ( _f, g_ ), where _f_ is a classifier and _g_ is a selection function deciding whether to predict or abstain. A common choice defines _g_ via a confidence score _κ_ and threshold _θ_ : _gθ_ ( _x|κ, f_ ) = 1[ _κ_ ( _x,_ ˆ _y|f_ ) _> θ_ ], where _y_ ˆ = _f_ ( _x_ ). Thus, the model predicts only when its confidence exceeds _θ_ . Selective prediction induces a trade-off between _risk_ and _coverage_ . Risk is defined with respect to a given loss, e.g., the 0/1 loss, and is measured only over the samples that are predicted. Coverage is the fraction of samples that are not rejected. By rejecting uncertain samples, the selective model aims to reduce risk at the cost of lower coverage. _Hierarchical selective classification_ [14] extends selective classification to a hierarchical setting. Under uncertainty, the model can retreat to a less specific ancestor label rather than abstaining (e.g.: predicting “Dog” instead of “Golden Retriever”), thereby using the hierarchy to guide a reduction in the information content of predictions. This is conceptually aligned with our objective of trading specificity for reliability, rather than relying on a binary abstention decision. 

**Selective Generation.** Most existing work on uncertainty estimation for text generation focuses on tasks where the generated text is at most a few tokens long, such as machine translation, text summarization, and question answering (QA) [8, 24, 48]. Among these, QA is the most relevant to our setting. However, even in openended QA benchmarks, the generated answers are typically short, e.g. TriviaQA [19] and CoQA [33]. This allows for a binary decision: either accept or reject the model’s output. This approach, known as _selective generation_ [34], serves as the NLP analogue of selective classification [8, 10, 25, 42, 43]. While these tasks are considerably easier to evaluate, the uncertainty estimation methods developed for them do not generalize well to long-form text generation [50], which makes for a much more realistic use case for LLMs. 

**Evaluation of Long-form Generation.** In our work, quantifying _risk_ (Section 4.1) requires evaluating the reliability of the generated response. Throughout this paper, we use factual correctness as our notion of reliability. Most existing work in this research thread is focused on mitigating hallucinations in long-form text via fact-checking pipelines. These methods typically decom-

In practice, since evaluation is performed on a finite set of examples, we sweep over all distinct attainable thresholds _{θk}_<sup>_K_</sup> _k_ =1<sup>,inducedbytheconfidencescoresinthe</sup> evaluation set, which yields a finite sequence of operating points with coverages _{ϕk}_ (sorted by increasing order). If the minimum observed coverage is strictly positive, we add an anchor point at _ϕ_ = 0 with risk equal to the risk at the minimum observed coverage. This prevents methods that only attain a narrow, high-coverage range from appearing artificially better due to integrating over a smaller portion of [0 _,_ 1], and ensures AURC comparisons reflect performance over a common coverage domain. Lower AURC indicates a better risk-coverage trade-off because it corresponds to lower risk on average across the full coverage range.

# **7 Threshold Selection for Target Risk** 

Thus far, we have studied the trade-off between risk and coverage induced by atom-wise SA. However, practical deployment typically requires fixing a specific confidence threshold _θ_ , which calls for a principled method for choosing it. In this section, we propose an algorithm for choosing _θ_ to satisfy a user-specified target risk. Our approach is inspired by split conformal prediction [45], using a calibration set to select a confidence threshold that, with high probability, guarantees that the risk on unseen test samples does not exceed a desired target. The full algorithm and its theoretical guarantees are provided in Appendix H. Here, we describe the practical procedure and present empirical results. The algorithm takes as input a language model _M_ , a target risk level _α_ , and a calibration set of prompts _D_ cal = _{pi}_<sup>_n_</sup> _i_ =1<sup>.It outputs a</sup> 

confidence threshold _θ_<sup>ˆ</sup> such that the atom-wise selective abstraction achieves risk at most _α_ + _ϵ_ on new samples, with confidence at least 1 _− δ_ . Concretely, the algorithm first queries _M_ on each _pi_ to obtain an initial response _Ti_ , and decomposes it into atomic claims _A_ ( _Ti_ ), forming a consolidated calibration set of atoms, _Acal_ =<sup>�</sup> _i_<sup>_A_(</sup><sup>_Ti_).</sup> For each atom _ak ∈ Acal_ , the algorithm computes a critical threshold _θk_ , defined as the minimal confidence level at which the abstraction selected for _ak_ is correct. In particular, if the original atom _ak_ is already correct, then _θk_ = 0. Finally, the algorithm outputs _θ_<sup>ˆ</sup> , chosen as a quantile of the calibration thresholds. 

**Empirical Results.** We evaluate the proposed algorithm on a range of target risks using the models and datasets detailed in Section 5. We repeat each experiment (dataset + model combination) 100 times, sampling a fresh calibration set at each iteration. Each calibration set comprises 30% of the benchmark prompts. We report dataset-level results averaged over all models and repetitions (600 runs per dataset). Figure 4 shows that the algorithm balances the risk-coverage trade-off, with empirical risk consistently falling close to the target risk. We observe some variability, which is expected given the relatively small amount of calibration and test data, and we expect larger datasets to yield more stable results.

This work argues that reliability in long-form generation is not a binary choice between answering and abstaining, but a trade-off between specificity and correctness. Our empirical evaluation across six open-source LLMs and two open-ended factual benchmarks, shows that atom-wise selective abstraction consistently outperforms standard baselines, including redaction, inline abstraction, and self-revision. # **C Abstraction Generation and Confidence Elicitation: Additional Discussion** 

This appendix expands on Section 3 (Selective Abstraction), focusing on Stage 3 (Abstraction) in the end-to-end pipeline. In this stage, low-confidence atoms are abstracted into less specific alternatives in order to trade specificity for reliability. We clarify the motivation behind Step (2), which constructs a sequence of abstraction candidates for each atom and elicits confidence scores for these candidates conditioned on the atom’s original confidence. 

## **Why generate a sequence of abstractions?** 

Selective abstraction requires choosing a minimally abstracted variant that satisfies the confidence threshold. Generating a sequence of abstractions with gradually reduced specificity provides a discrete, ordered set of candidates for this selection. This formulation makes thresholding well-defined: rather than relying on a single unconstrained rewrite, the method can select the most specific abstraction whose confidence exceeds the threshold. In addition, eliciting confidences jointly for related abstractions encourages a coherent progression in confidence as information is reduced, aligning abstraction depth with reliability. 

## **Why condition abstraction confidence on the atom’s confidence?** 

Conditioning abstraction confidence elicitation on the atom’s original confidence serves two purposes. First, it anchors the abstraction confidences to the uncertainty of the underlying factual content, encouraging abstraction confidence to reflect reduced specificity rather than a complete re-evaluation of factual correctness. Second, this conditioning helps maintain comparability across atoms by evaluating each atom–abstraction group relative to its own baseline confidence, reducing drift caused by prompt-level or statement-level variability. Together, these effects support a stable relationship between abstraction depth and confidence, which is essential for consistent threshold-based selection. 

Additionally, assigning confidence scores within the same elicitation call may help relatively calibrate abstraction confidences across atoms by anchoring them to a common scale.

# **E On the Importance of Choosing a Confidence Scoring Function** 

The choice of the confidence scoring function _κ_ is critical, as it directly influences the quality of the downstream abstractions. The confidence function determines which atoms are selected for abstraction and the degree to which they are abstracted, thereby shaping the trade-off between information and reliability. 

## **E.1 Comparison Metrics** 

**RC Curves and AURC.** We first compare confidence scoring functions directly by the risk-coverage trade-offs they induce: a _κ_ that achieves a better trade-off (lower AURC) is preferable [7]. 

**Ranking (AUROC).** Ranking captures the extent to which the confidence score induces a partial order on samples, where correct samples are assigned higher confidence scores than incorrect samples [11]. In our setting, this property is central to selective abstraction: a _κ_ that ranks well will ensure that the atoms that are most likely to be incorrect are abstracted first, since they will be assigned the lowest confidence values. Conversely, poor ranking leads to inefficient abstraction, where correct atoms are unnecessarily abstracted while incorrect atoms are retained. 

We quantify ranking quality using the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), which measures the probability that a randomly chosen correct atom receives a higher confidence score than a randomly chosen incorrect atom. AUROC therefore reflects how well _κ_ separates correct from incorrect content across all possible thresholds. High AUROC implies that abstraction can be focused on genuinely risky atoms, yielding substantial risk reduction for a given loss of information.
