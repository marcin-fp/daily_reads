### WHEN SHOULD LLMS BE LESS SPECIFIC? SELECTIVE ABSTRACTION FOR RELIABLE LONG-FORM TEXT
### GENERATION
Shani Goren1∗ Ido Galil2∗ Ran El-Yaniv1,2 1Technion 2NVIDIA
shanigoren@cs.technion.ac.il
ABSTRACT
LLMs are widely used, yet they remain prone to factual errors that erode user trust and limit adoption in high-risk settings. One approach to mitigate this risk is to equip models with uncertainty estimation mechanisms that abstain when confidence is low. However, this binary “all-or-nothing” approach is excessively restrictive in long-form settings, often discarding valuable information. We introduce Selective Abstrac-tion (SA), a framework that enables LLMs to trade specificity for reliability by selectively reducing the detail of uncertain content. We first formalize SA through the lenses of selective risk and coverage. We then propose Atom-wise Selective Abstraction, a claim-level instantiation that decomposes responses into atomic claims (short, self-contained statements each expressing a single fact) and replaces uncertain atoms with higher confidence, less specific abstractions. To evaluate this framework, we develop a novel end-to-end pipeline for openended generation that instantiates risk as factual correctness and measures coverage using an information-theoretic measure of retained information. Across six open-source models on the FactScore and LongFact-Objects benchmarks, atom-wise SA consistently outperforms existing baselines, improving the area under the risk-coverage curve (AURC) by up to 27.73% over claim removal, demonstrating that reducing specificity can boost accuracy and reliability while preserving most of their original meaning.

Despite these advances, LLMs remain prone to factual errors, generating content that appears fluent and coherent, but is factually incorrect or unsupported by underlying knowledge [18, 40, 16, 22]. As LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and law, factual mistakes can erode user trust and limit adoption. Ensuring trustworthy generation is therefore a central challenge for their reliable real-world use. A widely studied approach to this problem is uncertainty estimation, which aims to quantify a model’s confidence in its outputs. In classification, uncertainty estimates support selective prediction [7], where the model predicts only when confidence is sufficiently high and abstains otherwise. In text generation, a similar idea appears as selective generation [34], which allows the model to abstain from answering when confidence is low. This approach is effective for short outputs such as question answering, where it is reasonable to treat the response as a single decision. Rather than relying on binary all-or-nothing decisions, Selective Abstraction retains informative content by reducing the specificity of uncertain parts, yielding outputs that are less specific but more reliable.

2 Related Work
Selective Prediction. We view selective prediction as a starting point for formalizing Selective Abstraction. In classic selective prediction [7, 12], a classifier may abstain when uncertain. Formally, a selective model is a pair (f, g), where f is a classifier and g is a selection function deciding whether to predict or abstain. A common choice defines g via a confidence score κ and threshold θ: gθ(x|κ, f) = 1[κ(x, ŷ|f) > θ], where ŷ = f(x). Thus, the model predicts only when its confidence exceeds θ. Selective prediction induces a trade-off between risk and coverage. Risk is defined with respect to a given loss, e.g., the 0/1 loss, and is measured only over the samples that are predicted. Coverage is the fraction of samples that are not rejected. By rejecting uncertain samples, the selective model aims to reduce risk at the cost of lower coverage. Hierarchical selective classification [14] extends selective classification to a hierarchical setting. Under uncertainty, the model can retreat to a less specific ancestor label rather than abstaining (e.g.: predicting “Dog” instead of “Golden Retriever”), thereby using the hierarchy to guide a reduction in the information content of predictions. This is conceptually aligned with our objective of trading specificity for reliability, rather than relying on a binary abstention decision.
Selective Generation. Most existing work on uncertainty estimation for text generation focuses on tasks where the generated text is at most a few tokens long, such as machine translation, text summarization, and question answering (QA) [8, 24, 48]. Among these, QA is the most relevant to our setting. However, even in openended QA benchmarks, the generated answers are typically short, e.g. TriviaQA [19] and CoQA [33]. This allows for a binary decision: either accept or reject the model’s output. This approach, known as selective generation [34], serves as the NLP analogue of selective classification [8, 10, 25, 42, 43]. While these tasks are considerably easier to evaluate, the uncertainty estimation methods developed for them do not generalize well to long-form text generation [50], which makes for a much more realistic use case for LLMs.
Evaluation of Long-form Generation. In our work, quantifying risk (Section 4.1) requires evaluating the reliability of the generated response. Throughout this paper, we use factual correctness as our notion of reliability. In this section, we formalize the selective abstraction framework at a conceptual level. Let M be a language model. Given a prompt p, denote the language model’s generated text response3:M(p) = T . 4 Evaluating Selective Abstraction
Evaluating selective abstraction requires quantifying both risk and information, which is inherently difficult in openended generation. In such settings, a ground truth may not exist, and even when it does, there is often no clear way to quantify how much information remains. We quantify the information encoded in an atomic claim by estimating how strongly it narrows down the set of compatible entities, that is, how much new information it contributes about an entity. For example, consider the claim “Alan Turing was born in London”. We view it as specifying a subject-independent condition, born in Lon-don, and thus as defining a subset of entities that satisfy this condition. Relative to a broad reference set, People, the claim restricts the compatible entities from all people to the smaller subset of people born in London. Practi-cally, this corresponds to asking how many people were born in London out of all people, and using that ratio as a proxy for how informative the claim is. Formally, each claim c has the form [entity][predicate]. We measure information by interpreting the predicate span as a subject-independent condition over entities of the appropriate type. 4.3 RC Curves and AURC
Selective abstraction induces a trade-off between reliability and specificity, measured by risk and coverage. We summarize this trade-off using Risk-Coverage (RC) curves [7], which plot the risk of retained content as a function of coverage. An example RC curve is shown in Figure 1 (right). Given a confidence scoring function κ(·) and a threshold θ, SA produces an abstracted response T ′(θ). For each θ, we compute its coverage ϕ(T ′(θ), T ) and risk R(T ′(θ)), as defined in the preceding subsections. Varying θ over the range of κ traces an RC curve. We summarize each RC curve using the area under the RC curve (AURC), defined as AURC =
∫ 1
0 R(ϕ) dϕ,
where R(ϕ) denotes the risk achieved at coverage ϕ. In practice, since evaluation is performed on a finite set of examples, we sweep over all distinct attainable thresholds {θk}Kk=1, induced by the confidence scores in the evaluation set, which yields a finite sequence of operating points with coverages {ϕk} (sorted by increasing order). If the minimum observed coverage is strictly positive, we add an anchor point at ϕ = 0 with risk equal to the risk at the minimum observed coverage. This prevents methods that only attain a narrow, high-coverage range from appearing artificially better due to integrating over a smaller portion of [0, 1], and ensures AURC comparisons reflect performance over a common coverage domain. Lower AURC indicates a better risk-coverage trade-off because it corresponds to lower risk on average across the full coverage range.

6 Results
Across all models and datasets, atom-wise selective abstraction consistently achieves a better risk-coverage trade-off than the competing approaches considered in this work. This advantage is evident in summary metrics, through lower AURC (Table 1), and is also visible in the risk-coverage curves (Figures 7 and 8 in Appendix G). These results suggest that gradual, atom-wise reduction is an effective and controllable mechanism for reducing information in a way that improves reliability while pre-serving coverage. Compared to the redaction baseline, atom-wise SA achieves substantially lower AURC (Table 1): the average improvement in AURC across models is 17.43% on FactScore and 13.77% on LongFact. The maximal
improvement we have measured was for gpt-oss-120b, where the improvement was 27.73% and 21.02% on FactScore and LongFact, respectively. The risk-coverage curves (Figures 7 and 8 in Appendix G) further confirm these gains: atom-wise SA yields a smooth trade-off that dominates redaction and the other baselines across the full coverage range, consistently achieving lower risk at the same coverage. We note that the relative improvements on LongFact are smaller than on FactScore because LongFact generations start at a lower initial risk at full coverage, leaving less headroom for improvement. One plausible reason FactScore starts at higher risk is that FactScore prompts, which ask for biographies, push LLMs toward generating highly specific factual details, whereas LongFact questions may often be answered in broader terms. Notably, the models with the largest AURC improvements are the same on both benchmarks, gpt-oss-120b and qwen3-235b-thinking, and the least improved model is also the same, llama-3.3-70b-instruct. This crossbenchmark agreement suggests that abstraction performance may be driven by model-specific properties.

7 Threshold Selection for Target Risk
Thus far, we have studied the trade-off between risk and coverage induced by atom-wise SA. However, practical deployment typically requires fixing a specific confidence threshold θ, which calls for a principled method for choosing it. In this section, we propose an algorithm for choosing θ to satisfy a user-specified target risk. Our approach is inspired by split conformal prediction [45], using a calibration set to select a confidence threshold that, with high probability, guarantees that the risk on unseen test samples does not exceed a desired target. The full algorithm and its theoretical guarantees are provided in Appendix H. Here, we describe the practical procedure and present empirical results. The algorithm takes as input a language modelM, a target risk level α, and a calibration set of prompts Dcal = {pi}ni=1. It outputs a
confidence threshold θ̂ such that the atom-wise selective abstraction achieves risk at most α+ ϵ on new samples, with confidence at least 1− δ. Concretely, the algorithm first queriesM on each pi to obtain an initial response Ti, and decomposes it into atomic claims A(Ti), forming a consolidated calibration set of atoms, Acal =
⋃ i A(Ti).
For each atom ak ∈ Acal, the algorithm computes a critical threshold θk, defined as the minimal confidence level at which the abstraction selected for ak is correct. In particular, if the original atom ak is already correct, then θk = 0. Finally, the algorithm outputs θ̂, chosen as a quantile of the calibration thresholds.
Empirical Results. We evaluate the proposed algorithm on a range of target risks using the models and datasets detailed in Section 5. We repeat each experiment (dataset + model combination) 100 times, sampling a fresh calibration set at each iteration. Each calibration set comprises 30% of the benchmark prompts. We report dataset-level results averaged over all models and repetitions (600 runs per dataset). Figure 4 shows that the algorithm balances the risk-coverage trade-off, with empirical risk consistently falling close to the target risk. We observe some variability, which is expected given the relatively small amount of calibration and test data, and we expect larger datasets to yield more stable results.
8 Concluding Remarks
This work argues that reliability in long-form generation is not a binary choice between answering and abstaining, but a trade-off between specificity and correctness. We introduce Selective Abstraction as a general framework for navigating this trade-off, and instantiate it through an atom-wise procedure that selectively reduces the information in uncertain claims. Our empirical evaluation across six open-source LLMs and two open-ended factual benchmarks, shows that atom-wise selective abstraction consistently outperforms standard baselines, including redaction, inline abstraction, and self-revision. A Atom-wise Selective Abstraction Examples
We provide qualitative examples of atom-wise selective abstraction (SA) across multiple models spanning both the FactScore and LongFact benchmarks. Each example shows the original response (θ = 0), and the output of atom-wise SA produced at different confidence thresholds θ.
Prompt: Can you explain the viral component of ALS Association’s “Ice Bucket Challenge” marketing campaign? model: Qwen3-30b-thinking dataset: LongFact θ = 0, Risk=54.72%, Coverage = 100%
The ALS Association created a campaign – the “Ice Bucket Challenge” – that took place in the 21st century and quickly became a classic example of success. The challenge was driven by psychological, strategic and other factors and relied on a simple requirement: dumping a bucket of ice water over one’s head. No special skills, equipment or cost were needed, and the act could be completed in under 30 seconds. C Abstraction Generation and Confidence Elicitation: Additional Discussion
This appendix expands on Section 3 (Selective Abstraction), focusing on Stage 3 (Abstraction) in the end-to-end pipeline. In this stage, low-confidence atoms are abstracted into less specific alternatives in order to trade specificity for reliability. We clarify the motivation behind Step (2), which constructs a sequence of abstraction candidates for each atom and elicits confidence scores for these candidates conditioned on the atom’s original confidence.
Why generate a sequence of abstractions? Selective abstraction requires choosing a minimally abstracted variant that satisfies the confidence threshold. Generating a sequence of abstractions with gradually reduced specificity provides a discrete, ordered set of candidates for this selection. This formulation makes thresholding well-defined: rather than relying on a single unconstrained rewrite, the method can select the most specific abstraction whose confidence exceeds the threshold. In addition, eliciting confidences jointly for related abstractions encourages a coherent progression in confidence as information is reduced, aligning abstraction depth with reliability.
Why condition abstraction confidence on the atom’s confidence? Conditioning abstraction confidence elicitation on the atom’s original confidence serves two purposes. First, it anchors the abstraction confidences to the uncertainty of the underlying factual content, encouraging abstraction confidence to reflect reduced specificity rather than a complete re-evaluation of factual correctness. Second, this conditioning helps maintain comparability across atoms by evaluating each atom–abstraction group relative to its own baseline confidence, reducing drift caused by prompt-level or statement-level variability. Together, these effects support a stable relationship between abstraction depth and confidence, which is essential for consistent threshold-based selection.
Additionally, assigning confidence scores within the same elicitation call may help relatively calibrate abstraction confidences across atoms by anchoring them to a common scale.

E On the Importance of Choosing a Confidence Scoring Function
The choice of the confidence scoring function κ is critical, as it directly influences the quality of the downstream abstractions. The confidence function determines which atoms are selected for abstraction and the degree to which they are abstracted, thereby shaping the trade-off between information and reliability.
E.1 Comparison Metrics
RC Curves and AURC. We first compare confidence scoring functions directly by the risk-coverage trade-offs they induce: a κ that achieves a better trade-off (lower AURC) is preferable [7].
Ranking (AUROC). Ranking captures the extent to which the confidence score induces a partial order on samples, where correct samples are assigned higher confidence scores than incorrect samples [11]. In our setting, this property is central to selective abstraction: a κ that ranks well will ensure that the atoms that are most likely to be incorrect are abstracted first, since they will be assigned the lowest confidence values. Conversely, poor ranking leads to inefficient abstraction, where correct atoms are unnecessarily abstracted while incorrect atoms are retained. We quantify ranking quality using the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), which measures the probability that a randomly chosen correct atom receives a higher confidence score than a randomly chosen incorrect atom. AUROC therefore reflects how well κ separates correct from incorrect content across all possible thresholds. High AUROC implies that abstraction can be focused on genuinely risky atoms, yielding substantial risk reduction for a given loss of information.
