\begin{frame}{The room we were in}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.7em}
    \item Four days of research talks by working physicists; one day of industry flash talks.
    \item Of \textbf{13 organisations} presenting on Industry Day, exactly \textbf{two} build AI for fundamental science: \textbf{Physical Superintelligence} and \textbf{us}.
    \item Of three VC firms present, only one said ``AI for science'' out loud.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{The market (AI for theoretical physics) might be less crowded than the noise suggests. The conference was worth the effort: this was five days of unfiltered access to our exact users.}
\end{frame}
\note{Don't linger. The point is: small category, we were in the room, everything that follows is first-hand.}

% ------------------------------------------- 3
\begin{frame}{Three sentences to take, if you don't want to read more}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1.0em}
    \item The room named the main gap: it is \lead{calibration, not capability}.
    \item \lead{Verifiers generalize where generators don't} -- so verification cost, not raw capability, is what determines which architecture can scale to harder problems.
    \item \lead{How we ship} matters as much as what we ship: one our direct competitor seem less advanced$^1$ but they already tapped \emph{into} the runtimes physicists already use.\\[24pt]
    {\footnotesize $^1$ Their "get-physics-done" project offers workflow orchestration with LLM-generic verification checks; has 900+ stars on github and is already used in workflows of people present at IAIFI.}
  \end{enumerate}
  \vfill
\end{frame}
\note{This is the spine of the talk. Say each sentence, then move on -- the next slides are the evidence.}

% ------------------------------------------- 4
\begin{frame}{A customer told us the output contract}
  MERL (Mitsubishi Electric's research lab), our ideal industry-type-of-client archetype, asked for, in order:
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0.5em}
    \item A \lead{verdict}: is this right?
    \item A \lead{physical explanation}$^1$, in the language they already use (engineers/physicists not mathematicians).
    \item A \lead{minimal runnable example}.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\footnotesize $^1$ Full formal derivation -- they feel it already exists and is overwhelming (they might be wrong here; however, it is their current perception when using Claude/Gemini/ChatGPT).}
  \takeaway{Formal verification in physics has limitation. Something closer to the way how physicsts work might be better. E.g.: verdict + confidence/intuition + minimal runnable example.}
\end{frame}
\note{Emphasise: we did not infer this. A customer said it, and a competitor shipped it.}

% ------------------------------------------- 5
\begin{frame}{Why ``calibration, not capability''}
  Three criticisms of current AI, raised repeatedly and independently:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.55em}
    \item Models are \textbf{too good at convincing the user} (so it is hard to verify the results).
    \item Models \textbf{comply} where a human collaborator would push back.
    \item On long-horizon work the failure is not (always) a wrong answer -- it is a \textbf{plausible shortcut at an intermediate step}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  And the community's own norm: they will accept \textbf{wider error bars} in exchange for fewer unvalidated assumptions -- and present that trade as the success.
  \takeaway{A system optimised to sound confident optimises for exactly what this audience distrusts.}
\end{frame}
\note{The wider-error-bars point usually lands hardest with engineers -- it inverts the usual demo instinct.}

% ------------------------------------------- 6
\begin{frame}{Finding 1 -- the work is in the representation, not the model}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.6em}
    \item Four plenary speakers in one day, independently: the \textbf{architectures were ordinary}; encoding the problem into a learnable object \textbf{was} the non-trivial research.
    \item There is \textbf{no procedure} for choosing among encodings, the choice measurably changes results, and practitioners feel it as a bottleneck.
    %\item Industry Day made the stakes concrete: for algebraic structure, the \textbf{type} of embedding (matrix vs.\ vector) determines what is learnable at all.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{Search over representations of a scientific object is a concrete, \emph{highly desirable} research direction -- and it lands directly on our mathematical-embedding stream.}
\end{frame}
\note{Flag this as the clearest open research seam we saw. Nobody owns it.}

% ------------------------------------------- 7
\begin{frame}{Finding 2 -- verifier}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \lead{Cheap verifier is ok}\par\vspace{0.3em}
      \small
      A frequently-wrong model can be still valuable.\par\vspace{0.3em}
      (depending what that model \emph{enables}).
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \lead{Doing it right is hard}\par\vspace{0.3em}
      \small
       E.g., What stays stable as assumptions vary, plus what was assumed.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{1.9em}
  The deepest learning for us: \textbf{verification generalises where generation does not.}
  \takeaway{Verification work buys capability, not just safety.}
\end{frame}
\note{Half a minute. This is the finding most likely to change what an engineer builds next week.}

% ------------------------------------------- 8
\begin{frame}{Finding 3 -- agents are ``very good at outcome, very bad at process''}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.55em}
    \item On 24--36 hour tasks, every branch offers a \textbf{harder correct route} and an \textbf{easier route that looks approximately right}. Their current experience is that the system takes the easier one.
    \item Amazon's responsible-AI lead mentioned something similar: a model that ``solved'' a task by \textbf{exploiting the harness}.
    \item Consequence: unattended run time is bounded by \textbf{how hard shortcuts are to find}. So verification tooling and autonomy duration are ultimately \textbf{the same problem}.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{Outcome-only evaluation is structurally blind to this.}
\end{frame}
\note{Tie back to slide 3, sentence 2: this is why verification is a capability lever.}

% ------------------------------------------- 9
\begin{frame}{Finding 4 -- diversity collapse}
  Arrived from \textbf{three independent directions in a single day}:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.4em}
    \item a scaled-up vision model suffering from mode-collapse;
    \item particle resampling erasing diversity;
    \item RL post-training collapsing a language model's answer distribution.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  It might impact our conjecturing system. We should monitor this and, desirably, mitigate this.
  \takeaway{Research arc on preserving output diversity is desirable.}
\end{frame}
\note{Strongest cross-paradigm signal of the week. Cheap to instrument, expensive to discover late.}

% ------------------------------------------ 10
\begin{frame}{Findings 5 \& 6 -- two constraints from the field}
  \small
  \lead{Base vs.\ Fine-tuned.}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.2em}
    \item Fine-tuned physics models differentiate \textbf{only} where the corpus sits outside base-model training distributions -- and we should know exactly where that boundary is.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \lead{Timeline.}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.2em}
    \item A large collaboration takes a \textbf{year} to adopt anything; agentic capability turns over in \textbf{months} (see the next Figure). Results must stay reproducible to \textbf{2041}.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{Functional requirement: ``can this result be regenerated in five years without us?''}
\end{frame}
\note{Two constraints, fast. The 2041 number is the one people remember.}
