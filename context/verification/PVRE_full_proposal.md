Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has produced large gains in mathematical and coding ability for language models, but physics reasoning has lagged behind, in part because physics lacks the large verified datasets and cheap step-level supervision that mathematics enjoys. We propose a system for reference-free, executable, step-level verification of physics derivations. Rather than judging reasoning steps with a learned model or comparing them to a gold solution, we check each step against physics itself: dimensional consistency, random numerical substitution, limiting-case behavior, and conservation and symmetry constraints. These checks are deterministic, cheap, and immune to reward hacking. We use them in three ways: as a label factory that produces process reward model (PRM) training data with no per-problem annotation cost (a fixed engineering cost per physical system family amortizes over unlimited generated instances), as a dense reward signal for reinforcement learning, and as an evaluation instrument. We call the resulting platform PVRE, Physics Verifiable Reward En-vironments: problems are generated synthetically from parameterized physical system families with difficulty as an explicit control knob, which keeps training in the learnable band and eliminates benchmark contamination. The central empirical question is whether high-precision but partial-coverage executable labels outperform the noisy but complete alternatives (Monte Carlo rollout labels and LLM judges) for training physics reasoners. The scoped goal is to lift openweight models of 7B to 32B parameters to frontier-level performance on compositional physics derivation tasks, a regime where current failures are execution failures rather than knowledge failures.
1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become the dominant recipe for improving the reasoning ability of large language models. When the correctness of a final answer can be checked automatically, for instance by an exact-match grader in mathematics or a unit test in code, a model can be trained with a clean binary signal, and this simple loop has produced striking gains (Guo et al., 2025; Lambert et al., 2024). The recipe works because the reward is trustworthy: unlike a learned reward model, an executable check cannot be flattered, and unlike a human annotator, it costs nothing per label.
Physics has not benefited from this recipe to nearly the same degree, and the reasons are structural. First, physics lacks the large question-and-answer corpora that mathematics accumulated
1
over decades of competition archives; less than one percent of the question pool used to train prominent RLVR systems touches STEM topics beyond mathematics, and coverage of physics specifically is thin (Prabhudesai et al., 2026). Second, and more fundamentally, physics reasoning fails in the middle of derivations, not just at the end. A model can select the right physical principle, make a single algebraic or conceptual slip three steps in, and produce a fluent, plausible, and wrong answer. Outcome-only rewards cannot see this, and they become nearly useless on hard problems where almost every rollout fails, because a uniform reward of zero carries no gradient.
The performance data confirm both that the problem is real and that there is headroom worth pursuing. On PHYBench, a set of 500 original physics problems, a leading frontier model reached 36.9 percent accuracy against 61.9 percent for human experts, and scores on PHYBench sit consistently below those on mathematics benchmarks of comparable prestige (Qiu et al., 2025). At the research end, PRL-Bench, built from recent Physical Review Letters papers centered on theoretical derivation, finds that even the strongest frontier models score below 50 out of 100, with failures dominated by conceptual and formulaic errors and by unstable long derivations (Miao et al., 2026). Perturbation studies sharpen the picture: when ABench-Physics rewrites a problem as a family of dynamic variants and demands that a model solve every variant, frontier scores fall sharply (Zhang et al., 2025), echoing similar fragility results in mathematics (Mirzadeh et al., 2024). Part of the frontier advantage on static leaderboards therefore reflects prior exposure rather than robust competence. This matters for us in two ways: it means evaluation must use fresh problem instances, and it means the gap between frontier and open-weight models on genuinely novel problems is smaller than leaderboards suggest, which makes closing it a realistic target.
Our institutional motivation is specific. FirstPrinciples builds AI systems for physics research, and open-weight models we can post-train, serve, and audit ourselves are strategically preferable to closed frontier models. The question this project answers is whether a carefully designed verification and reward pipeline can bring an open-weight model of modest size to frontier-level reliability on a well-defined class of physics derivation tasks.
1.2 Related work
Process reward models. Process reward models score individual reasoning steps rather than final answers. The founding result showed that step-level human annotation substantially outperforms outcome supervision for mathematical reasoning (Lightman et al., 2024), but human annotation does not scale. Math-Shepherd replaced human labels with Monte Carlo estimation, scoring a step by the empirical probability that rollouts continuing from it reach a correct final answer (Wang et al., 2024), and generative verifiers that reason before judging have pushed quality further (Khalifa et al., 2025). All of these approaches inherit a weakness relevant to us: Monte Carlo labels degenerate on hard problems, where continuation success probabilities approach zero for every step and the labels become uninformative, and learned judges remain vulnerable to the reward hacking that afflicts any learned reward signal under optimization pressure.
Process supervision for science. Two recent systems extend process supervision to scientific domains. Sci-PRM trains a tool-aware generative judge on a large corpus of chain-of-tool trajectories and shows gains both in best-of-N selection and as a dense RL reward (Zhao et al., 2026). DataPRM builds an environment-aware judge for agentic data analysis that can probe execution state to uncover silent errors (Qiu et al., 2026). Both are learned judges: verification is ultimately an LLM’s opinion, trained on annotated or sampled trajectories. On the evaluation side, PRISM-Physics decomposes competition physics solutions into causal DAGs and validates steps with a rule-based symbolic equivalence checker (Zhao et al., 2025). This is closest in spirit to our verifi-
2
cation layer, with one decisive difference: PRISM-Physics checks a candidate step for equivalence against a known reference solution, so it requires the gold derivation and serves evaluation only. It cannot label arbitrary trajectories on problems without curated solutions, which is exactly what a training pipeline needs.
Verifier-grounded training labels. FoVer demonstrates the mechanism we intend to exploit: it generates PRM training data from tasks that are formally verifiable (formal logic and theorem proving), and shows that PRMs trained on these exactly-labeled steps generalize to out-of-distribution reasoning (Kamoi et al., 2025). FoVer stops at domains where formal verification is native. Physics is semi-formal: units, approximations, regime assumptions, and physical arguments have no settled formal semantics, and formalization efforts such as PhysLean, while valuable, cover a narrow slice of the field and cannot serve as a high-throughput labeling function (Tooby-Smith, 2024). The open question is whether the FoVer mechanism survives the passage from formal logic to semi-formal physics.
Synthetic physics data and simulator rewards. Recent work generates physics training data at scale with correctness enforced by simulators or hybrid verification. One line uses physics simulators to generate and grade olympiad-style problems for RLVR (Prabhudesai et al., 2026). QuantumQA constructs a synthetic dataset with a hybrid protocol combining symbolic solvers, LLM-based semantic auditing, and human review, and trains with a reward model that mixes deterministic and judged signals (Qu et al., 2026). In both cases verification concerns the final outcome or serves as a dataset quality filter, with humans or LLM judges in the loop. Neither produces reference-free step-level labels, and neither runs the controlled comparison between label sources that would tell the field which supervision signal to prefer.
1.3 Problem statement
We state the problem in three parts. First, build the verifier. Construct a suite of reference-free, executable checks that can
adjudicate individual steps of a physics derivation without access to a gold solution: dimensional consistency, numerical consecutive-consistency under random substitution, limiting-case behavior of propagated expressions, conservation and symmetry constraints, and a registry of canonical physical relations. Each check either passes a step, fails it, or abstains, yielding ternary labels (verified correct, verified wrong, unadjudicated). The suite must be validated against derivations with known injected errors so that its precision, recall, and coverage are measured quantities rather than assumptions.
Second, use the verifier as a training signal. Generate problems from parameterized physical system families with difficulty as an explicit control parameter and with the full derivation graph known by construction. Sample solution trajectories from open-weight models, label every step with the verifier suite with no per-problem annotation cost, and use the labels in two ways: to train process reward models, and directly as dense rewards in reinforcement learning with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024).
Third, answer the scientific question. Executable labels are high-precision but partial: a wrong step can pass every invariant, and some steps cannot be adjudicated at all. Monte Carlo labels are complete but noisy, and collapse on hard problems. LLM-judge labels are complete but biased and hackable. Does precise-but-partial supervision beat noisy-but-complete supervision, at matched compute, for training physics reasoners? No prior work runs this comparison, and its answer is valuable in either direction.

The scoped ambition, consistent with the evidence on where open-weight models actually lose to frontier models, is to close the gap on tasks where failure is an execution failure (unreliable multistep symbolic manipulation and composition) rather than a knowledge failure (missing graduatelevel formalism). Reinforcement learning is understood to activate latent capability rather than inject new knowledge: RLVR-trained models rarely exceed the pass@k envelope of their base models (Yue et al., 2025), and the recent demonstration that RL reasoning gains on an 8B model fit in 13 trained parameters, while supervised fine-tuning requires updates orders of magnitude larger, makes the point sharply, since an update of that size cannot encode new domain content (Morris et al., 2026). This scoping is therefore a matter of principle, not merely of modesty. Our earlier internal results in quantum circuit synthesis point the same direction: roughly 95 percent of wrong answers in our Phase-0 experiments arose from multi-register composition errors rather than missing primitives, suggesting that compositional unreliability is the dominant, and trainable, failure mode.

2.1 Structured derivation schema
Free-form chain-of-thought is difficult to verify because claims are buried in prose. We therefore require models, during data generation and training, to emit solutions in a lightweight structured schema: a declaration of symbols with physical dimensions, a list of stated assumptions and regime constraints, and a sequence of steps, each consisting of an equation and a one-line justification tag identifying the step as an algebraic manipulation, a physics input, or an approximation. Models follow such schemas reliably with minimal prompting, and the schema collapses most of the extraction problem that makes free-form verification hard. For evaluation on external benchmarks, where solutions arrive free-form, a separate extractor maps prose steps into schema form, with the verifier abstaining whenever extraction confidence is low. We deliberately keep the schema-native pipeline for training and the extractor-based pipeline for evaluation, so that extraction errors cannot contaminate training labels.
2.2 The verifier suite
Each verifier is a small pure function with declared preconditions that abstains when they are unmet. The families, in increasing order of discriminative power:
1. Dimensional consistency. Every equation is checked for dimensional homogeneity using declared symbol dimensions. This check is cheap and has near-total coverage, but it is weak on its own: many conceptual errors are dimensionally clean. It serves as the first-pass filter and catches a nontrivial fraction of careless errors.
2. Numerical consecutive-consistency. For steps tagged as algebra, we draw random numerical assignments for all symbols, constrained to satisfy the stated assumptions, substitute into the parent equations and the child equation, and check that the child follows numerically across many draws. This is the workhorse. It verifies algebraic steps with near-deterministic precision, at millisecond cost, without any reference solution, and its cost does not grow with problem difficulty.
3. Limiting-case checks. Physical answers must behave correctly in known limits: a friction coefficient going to zero, an angle going to π/2, a mass ratio going to infinity. Each generated problem carries limit specifications, each a triple of a parameter limit, the reduced system it produces, and an executable oracle for that reduced system (a closed-form solution or a direct numerical integration). The check is purely numerical: evaluate the model’s propagated expression near the limit for random draws of the remaining symbols, evaluate the oracle at the same values, and compare within tolerance. No physics judgment happens at check time; the physics content was fixed at generation time, in code. Because limits probe the physics rather than the algebra, this family catches the conceptual errors that dimensional and numerical checks miss, and failures are localized to the offending step by blame assignment over the derivation dependency graph.
4. Conservation and symmetry constraints. On symbolic Lagrangian or Hamiltonian systems, conserved quantities are derived mechanically at generation time: cyclic coordinates yield their conjugate momenta by the Noether procedure, time independence yields the energy function, and declared dissipation terms yield an exact energy-balance identity rather than naive conservation. The check on a trajectory is then numerical bookkeeping: draw random assignments satisfying the stated assumptions, evaluate the model’s expressions for the relevant quantities before and after, and require the identity to hold within tolerance across
5
draws. Symmetry checks follow the same pattern with structured substitutions: invariance under exchange of identical subsystems, invariance of the answer under variation of a symbol it cannot physically depend on, and declared parity or time-reversal behavior. Every check reduces to evaluating expressions under transformed and untransformed numeric assignments and comparing.
5. Relation registry. A curated library of canonical physical relations (constitutive laws, standard forces, thermodynamic identities) against which steps tagged as physics inputs are template-matched. Registry coverage is inherently partial; we treat registry hits as bonus coverage, never as a requirement.
The output is a ternary label per step. A step verified correct by the checks applicable to its tag is labeled positive; a step failed by any applicable check, after blame assignment, is labeled negative; a step no check can adjudicate, such as a purely verbal argument, is labeled unadjudicated. We emphasize a semantic decision that the worked examples forced: labels are scoped to what a step claims. An algebraically valid substitution built on a wrong premise is labeled correct as algebra, and the negative label lands on the premise. This scoping is what makes blame assignment coherent.
Since consecutive steps of a derivation are generally not equivalent to one another, the relation these checks verify is entailment, whether the child follows from its parents, and the check semantics follow the step taxonomy. Two clarifications matter. First, deterministic here means the verdict procedure is exactly reproducible given a seed and contains no learned or judgment component; it does not mean formal proof. The checks are falsification-based, in the spirit of property-based testing: a pass means the claim survived N independent falsification attempts, and the errorinjection audit of Section 2.4 measures empirically how often injected errors of each class survive, which is what a pass is worth. Second, the semantics differ by step type. For exact manipulations, entailment means every assignment satisfying the parents satisfies the child; since the parents’ solution set has measure zero in the ambient space, admissible assignments are constructed rather than rejection-sampled, by fixing all but a few variables at random and numerically solving the parent system for the remainder before evaluating the child’s residual. Solve steps are the easy special case in which the child supplies the completion itself: the claimed root is substituted back into the parent, as in the worked example. For polynomial steps a fully symbolic upgrade exists at no architectural cost, since the child residual following from the parents is ideal membership, decidable by Gröbner basis reduction, which turns a not-falsified verdict into a proof. Approximation steps are handled by requiring the schema to state the claim precisely: the small parameter and the claimed order. The check is then either symbolic, a series expansion of the parent-minus-child difference confirming the leading surviving order, or numerical, a convergence-rate test that evaluates the residual along a geometric sequence in the small parameter and fits the log-log slope, the same procedure used to verify the order of a discretization scheme. The residual of an approximation is not required to vanish; it is required to vanish at the claimed rate. An undeclared approximation earns the unadjudicated label, which doubles as an incentive for models trained in the schema to make their approximations explicit. One asymmetry should be stated plainly: fail verdicts are certificates, since a nonzero residual at an admissible assignment is conclusive, while pass verdicts mean not falsified, with a measure-zero escape for a wrong step that agrees with its parents on all sampled draws. Generic errors do not live on measure-zero sets, and the error-injection matrix quantifies the residual risk rather than assuming it away.
Two properties of this design deserve emphasis for readers coming from the reward-modeling side. The labels are deterministic, so they cannot be hacked by the policy in the way learned reward models can under optimization pressure. And the labeling cost is independent of problem
6
difficulty and of policy quality, which is precisely where Monte Carlo labeling breaks: on problems where the policy rarely succeeds, continuation-based labels degenerate to uninformative zeros, while executable checks continue to adjudicate every algebra step and every limit.
The determinism rests on a strict role separation that we adopt as an architectural principle: language models may propose, but only executable oracles adjudicate. An LLM is permitted in proposal roles, for instance suggesting which limits are worth probing on an external free-form problem, because a wrong proposal can only cause an abstention or a wasted check, never a wrong label. Every pass or fail verdict is ultimately a numerical or symbolic comparison against something executable, and any claim for which no executable oracle exists is labeled unadjudicated. It is also worth stating precisely what reference-free means here. It means no gold derivation is required, so arbitrary trajectories can be labeled. It does not mean oracle-free with respect to the problem: on the training distribution the generator built the physical system and therefore legitimately knows its conserved quantities, its degenerate limits, and an independent numerical solution, and the verifiers consume this metadata. Dimensional and numerical-consistency checks are fully self-contained and carry their coverage to any problem; limiting-case, conservation, and symmetry checks consume generator-supplied, mechanically derived oracles, so they operate at full strength on the training distribution and with reduced, abstention-guarded coverage on external problems. This division of labor suits the project’s logic, because the training labels, where the oracle-backed families matter most as the catchers of conceptual errors, live entirely on the generated distribution.
2.3 Synthetic problem generation with difficulty control
Training problems are generated forward from parameterized physical system families. A generator samples a system configuration (for instance, a set of coupled oscillators with sampled masses, couplings, and constraints, or a quasi-static thermodynamic cycle with sampled process legs), derives the ground-truth solution symbolically or numerically with a computer algebra system (Meurer et al., 2017), records the full derivation graph, and produces a natural-language problem statement by templated generation followed by LLM naturalization and round-trip verification. Three properties are essential. Ground truth exists at the step level by construction, which is what allows us to measure verifier precision and recall without human annotation. Difficulty is a control parameter, exposed through knobs such as the number of coupled subsystems, perturbative order, constraint count, and cross-domain composition. And every instance is fresh, so evaluation on generated problems is contamination-free by construction, an important property given the evidence that static physics benchmarks partially measure prior exposure (Zhang et al., 2025; Mirzadeh et al., 2024).
Oracle emission is part of generation, not a separate annotation pass. The oracle-deriving code is written once per system family and runs automatically on every sampled instance: conserved quantities fall out of the Noether procedure on the symbolic Lagrangian, the numerical outcome oracle is the family’s integrator applied to the instance, and limit specifications are declared at the family level and instantiated per problem. The unit of the dataset is therefore not a question-and-answer pair but a problem package:

The model sees only the statement; the verifiers consume the rest. Limit oracles are frequently compositional: the zero-coupling limit of an N -oscillator instance is a set of independent single oscillators, itself a degenerate instance of the same family, so the reduced-system oracle is the family’s own solver. Families thereby reference one another, and each new family strengthens the limit checks available to the others, which is what makes the generator library a compounding platform asset rather than a one-off dataset.
This design also fixes the honest cost accounting. There is no per-problem annotation cost, but there is a fixed engineering cost per family, which we estimate at one to three researcher-weeks based on our prior experience building an equivalent generator for quantum arithmetic circuits, with the first family slowest because the package schema and the Noether machinery are built alongside it. That fixed cost then amortizes over unlimited instances, unlimited step labels, and the full difficulty range, whereas human step annotation costs per label indefinitely (Lightman et al., 2024) and Monte Carlo labeling costs compute per label and degrades exactly where we want to train. The per-family cost is the real bottleneck of the project and the reason the initial scope is deliberately narrow. For the same reason, the durable deliverable is the family-authoring framework rather than any single family: the package schema, the Noether machinery, the verifier registry, and the error-injection harness are shared across families, so marginal family cost falls from weeks toward days, and the harness makes machine-assisted family authoring safe, since generator code drafted by a language model is trusted only after it passes validation against known injected errors. Oracle authoring itself is a well-scoped task for domain physicists that requires no reinforcement learning expertise, which matches our staffing.
Difficulty control also gives us automatic curriculum. During training we estimate the policy’s pass rate per difficulty cell from the rollouts we are already sampling, and keep the training distribution pinned to the learnable band, annealing compositional depth upward as the policy improves. Stale training distributions are a common failure mode of RLVR runs, and a parametric generator is the clean fix.
The initial scope is two domains chosen to maximize compositional depth and symbolic verifiability: coupled mechanical systems (normal modes, constrained dynamics) and quasi-static thermodynamics (multi-leg cycles, coupled subsystem equilibration). Both are execution-limited rather than knowledge-limited for current open-weight models, a claim Experiment E1 tests directly before we commit. Quantum circuit synthesis, where we already have generators and verifiers from prior internal work, serves as a third domain for transfer checks.
2.4 Verifier validation by error injection
Before any verifier output is trusted as a training label, the suite is validated on synthetic derivations with controlled injected errors: sign flips, dropped factors, dimensional violations, wrong limits, illegitimate approximations, and wrong physics premises with correct downstream algebra. Because the generator knows the true derivation graph, every injected error has a known location and class, and we can report a full confusion matrix per verifier family per error class, together with step-level coverage (the fraction of substantive steps at least one verifier can adjudicate). No adjacent work reports such a table, because reference-based and learned-judge systems cannot produce one. This validation is the first deliverable of the project and its go/no-go gate.

2.5 From labels to models
The label factory samples K solutions per problem from several open-weight models under the structured schema, runs the verifier suite, and emits ternary step labels. At the intended scale of 5,000 to 10,000 problems with K between 8 and 16, this yields on the order of 105 labeled steps with no per-problem annotation cost, comparable to the scale at which prior PRM training data was constructed (Wang et al., 2024; Kamoi et al., 2025).
We consume the labels in two ways. First, we train process reward models on them, following standard discriminative and generative PRM recipes (Lightman et al., 2024; Khalifa et al., 2025), with the unadjudicated class handled explicitly rather than discarded. A process reward model is a separate model that judges rather than generates: given a problem and a solution prefix, it scores the latest step, and it is trained here as a per-step classifier on the verifier’s labels. The reason to train a learned judge when executable verifiers already exist is that the two occupy opposite ends of an exactness-versus-coverage trade: the verifier suite is exact but partial, abstaining on prose steps and losing its oracle-backed strength off the training distribution, while the PRM is the learned interpolation of the verifier, trained on exactly-labeled steps and then scoring every step, including unadjudicated ones and steps from families we never authored. That this interpolation genuinely extrapolates is the out-of-distribution result of Kamoi et al. (2025), and it is the entire reason a PRM appears in this pipeline. Second, we use verifier outputs directly as dense rewards in GRPO training (Shao et al., 2024), combining the outcome reward from the generator’s ground truth with step-level penalties for verified-wrong steps. Dense step penalties also make failed trajectories informative in a structured way: a derivation that is clean until its fourth step is penalized differently from one that is wrong from the start, so the gradient carries information about where things went wrong, not only that they did. Direct dense rewards and distilled PRM rewards are compared head-to-head; the former is unhackable but partial, the latter generalizes to unadjudicated steps but reintroduces a learned model into the loop, and under optimization pressure a policy can learn to flatter a learned judge in ways it cannot flatter a numerical residual check.
A note on formal methods, since the question arises naturally for physicist readers. Proof assistants provide sound verification, and physics formalization efforts exist (Tooby-Smith, 2024), but translating free-form model-generated derivations into formal statements is itself an unsolved research problem, and physics adds units, approximations, and regime assumptions that lack settled formal semantics. Our design deliberately occupies the middle of a soundness-versus-coverage axis: we trade formal soundness for the ability to adjudicate a large fraction of real derivation steps at near-deterministic precision and factory throughput. Formal verification of a narrow subset remains attractive future work.
2.6 What deploys: inference without oracles
A natural question, and one worth settling before the experiments are read, is what happens when the trained model meets a problem from a family we never authored, where no oracle metadata exists. The answer has three layers, and the distinction they rest on is that the oracle suite is a training-time instrument, not an inference-time dependency.
It helps to first separate four objects that the phrase “the model verifies” tends to blur. The shaped policy is what RL training guarantees: the verifier touches the model only through gradients, never as input, so training produces by definition a shifted sampling distribution in which derivations resembling those that passed become more probable. Internalized self-verification is a behavioral hypothesis about what that shifted distribution contains, discussed below; it is emergent, not installed. Tool-called verification is the deployment mode in which the model invokes
9
the portable checker at inference and reads its output in context, which is scaffolding, exact but external. And the PRM is a separate trained judge, described in Section 2.5. Our prior quantumbenchmark finding that iterative verifier feedback dominated all other interventions lived in the third channel, verifier output injected in context at inference; the headline bet of this project is that part of that signal can be moved into the weights through the first two, paid once at training time rather than at every inference, and the bare-policy versus tool-augmented comparison in E4 measures exactly how much moved.
The first and most important layer is that verification is amortized into the weights. This is the same logic as reinforcement learning on mathematics: no grader ships with the deployed model; the grader shaped the policy during training, and the shaped policy is what deploys (Guo et al., 2025). What the trained model carries into an unfamiliar family is not oracle access but the procedural discipline the oracles enforced, including algebraic hygiene, unit tracking, and constraint bookkeeping, together with the habit of self-checking. That habit has a concrete reinforcement mechanism even though we never reward it directly: trajectories in which the model back-substitutes its own roots or probes its own limits fail the verifiers less often, so they are differentially rewarded, and the behavior is amplified. Whether this internalization in fact survives the passage to unseen families is not assumed anywhere in this proposal; it is precisely the quantity the ring-1 and ring-2 evaluations of E4 measure.
The second layer is a structural observation about our own verifier suite: the self-verification skills that can transfer are exactly its oracle-free subset. Dimensional consistency needs only the declared symbols, and numerical consecutive-consistency needs only the model’s own equations; substituting random numbers into one’s own step and one’s own claimed root requires no knowledge of the underlying physical family. These two checks, the workhorses of the suite, are therefore executable by the model within its own reasoning on any problem whatsoever. The oracle-backed checks (limits, conservation, symmetry) do not port mechanically, but the model can approximate them in context, because knowing what a familiar limit should yield is base-model physics knowledge that is latently present; training has only to make consulting it habitual. The training signal is thus built from a superset of checks, and the deployable skill is the self-executable subset plus reinforced habits that point at the remainder.
The third layer concerns deployment modes that want explicit machine checking rather than internalized discipline. Two portable artifacts fall out of the pipeline at no extra cost. The reference-free verifier pair, dimensional and numerical-consistency, runs on schema-formatted output from any family with no oracle metadata, so a tool-integrated inference loop in which the model emits steps and the checker adjudicates the algebra works fully out of distribution. And the trained PRM is itself a portable learned verifier: it is trained on executable labels but generalizes to unadjudicated steps and unseen families, which is the out-of-distribution result already demonstrated for formallylabeled verifiers (Kamoi et al., 2025), and it supports best-of-N selection or step-level search at inference with nothing from the generator.
This layering imposes one discipline on our claims. The frontier-parity result of E4 is reported for the bare policy, with no tools and no PRM attached, so that the headline claim concerns internalized capability rather than scaffolding. The tool-augmented and PRM-guided inference modes are reported as ablations; they are also the modes most directly relevant to production use, where attaching the portable checker at inference is free additional reliability.

3 Planned experiments
3.1 E0: Verifier audit (go/no-go gate)
We build the error-injection validation set for the two workhorse verifier families (dimensional and numerical consecutive-consistency) in one domain, plus limiting-case checks, and measure precision, recall per error class, and coverage. In parallel we sample a few hundred free-form trajectories from open-weight models on PHYBench-style problems and measure what fraction of substantive steps the suite can adjudicate after extraction.
Expected outcome. Based on the design, we expect near-perfect precision for numerical consistency on algebra steps, high but imperfect recall for limiting-case checks on conceptual errors, and the open question is coverage. Success threshold: coverage of at least 50 percent of substantive steps with verifier precision above 90 percent. Kill threshold: coverage below roughly 30 percent, or precision that cannot be pushed above 90 percent, either of which breaks the precise-but-partial thesis. This experiment takes two to three weeks and decides whether the project proceeds.
3.2 E1: Failure-mode diagnostic and domain selection
We bucket the errors of candidate open-weight models (7B to 32B) on held-out physics problems into execution failures (algebraic slips, composition errors, dropped constraints) versus knowledge failures (missing formalism, wrong physical principle with no recovery path), following the errortaxonomy protocol from our earlier quantum work. Domains where execution failures dominate are selected for the generator.
Expected outcome. We expect execution failures to dominate in compositional mechanics and thermodynamics for models of this size, mirroring the roughly 95 percent composition-error share we measured in quantum circuit synthesis. If knowledge failures dominate everywhere, the RLVR premise weakens and the project pivots toward knowledge-injection methods, which is a different proposal.
3.3 E2: Label-source comparison for PRM training
The central controlled experiment. On identical trajectory sets, we produce step labels three ways: executable verifier labels, Monte Carlo continuation labels in the style of Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024), and LLM-judge labels from a strong open judge. We train matched PRMs on each and evaluate on a human-audited held-out subset of step labels and on best-of-N selection over fresh generated problems and external benchmarks, at matched total compute for label production.
Expected outcome. We expect executable labels to win on hard problems (where Monte Carlo degenerates) and to be at least competitive elsewhere, with the LLM judge showing the known biases and the worst robustness under distribution shift. A clean negative result, where noisy-but-complete labels win at matched cost, is publishable and would redirect our training stack; the experiment is designed so that either outcome is informative.
3.4 E3: Reinforcement learning with dense executable rewards
We run GRPO on the generator distribution under four arms: outcome-only reward; outcome plus dense executable step penalties; outcome plus distilled PRM reward; and outcome plus PRM reward with the PRM trained on executable labels. Curriculum (difficulty annealing on versus off) is ablated within the best arm. Reward-hacking incidence is monitored explicitly, since learnedreward arms are expected to show it and the executable-reward arm should not by construction.

Expected outcome. We expect dense process signals to outperform outcome-only training most strongly on the hardest difficulty cells, and curriculum to prevent the gradient starvation that outcome-only training suffers there. The comparison between direct executable rewards and distilled PRM rewards is genuinely open, and is the second scientific contribution of the paper. We add one behavioral probe: self-verification moves (back-substituting a root, testing units, probing a limit of one’s own answer) are never rewarded directly, but trajectories containing them should fail the verifiers less often and be differentially reinforced, so we count such moves in sampled traces before and after training and test whether they are causally load-bearing by ablating them, which distinguishes a policy that got better from a policy that got better by checking itself.
3.5 E4: Transfer and the frontier-parity claim
Transfer is not a side effect to hope for; it is the discriminating experiment between the two available explanations of any training gain. The elicitation view (Yue et al., 2025; Morris et al., 2026) predicts that training on our families improves domain-general procedural competence (long algebraic chains, constraint bookkeeping, limit discipline, unit hygiene), which must then surface on physics content the model was never trained on, since the base model already carries that content latently. The pattern-fitting view, made mechanistically concrete by studies of how RLVR can surface memorization shortcuts under weak reward signals (Yan et al., 2026), predicts gains confined to the trained families. Our two training families are deliberately content-disjoint but procedure-identical, which is what makes this distinction measurable.
We therefore evaluate in three rings (Figure 2). Ring 0: held-out fresh instances from the trained families, measuring in-distribution gain. Ring 1: untrained families within the trained domains, for instance rigid-body dynamics and heat-engine variants generated by families we author for evaluation only, measuring near transfer. Ring 2: external benchmarks spanning untrained domains (PHYBench (Qiu et al., 2025), ABench-Physics dynamic variants (Zhang et al., 2025), and the condensed-matter subset of CMPhysBench (Wang et al., 2025) as the farthest probe), never our own training distribution, with frontier models as untrained baselines under identical prompting. We report per-difficulty and per-error-class breakdowns in every ring, not just headline accuracy, and the transfer gradient across rings is the central figure of the paper. The precedent that verifier-labeled process supervision generalizes out of distribution in formal domains (Kamoi et al., 2025) is the reason to expect ring 2 to be nonflat.
Expected outcome. The target claim is that a 7B to 32B open-weight model trained with executable process supervision reaches parity with frontier models on the compositional derivation slice of the ring-2 benchmarks, evaluated as a bare policy with no tools or PRM attached, with tool-augmented and PRM-guided inference reported as ablations per Section 2.6. Partial transfer, meaning a clear positive gradient that falls short of parity, still supports the label-source contribution from E2 and E3, and a flat ring-1 result would itself be a significant finding about the limits of procedural elicitation, with direct consequences for how many families our internal roadmap requires.

The relation registry matches S1 against the coupled-oscillator equation-of-motion template: verified. S2 introduces a standard method with no equation to falsify: unadjudicated. S3 is checked by numerical consecutive-consistency: ten random draws of (m, k, kc, ω,A1, A2) consistent with the assumptions confirm that S3 follows from S1 and S2; verified. The limit check illustrates why redundancy matters: at kc → 0 the erroneous expression also reduces to k/m, so this particular limit passes despite the error, and had S4 been a physics input rather than checkable algebra, only the outcome oracle would have caught it. Step 5: labels and blame. Blame assignment over the dependency graph finds S1 through S3 verified and S4 the first failing node, so the fault localizes there. The emitted labels are S1 positive, S2 unadjudicated, S3 positive, S4 negative, final outcome negative.
Step 6: consumption. As PRM training data, the trajectory contributes one negative step in an otherwise correct prefix, exactly the hard-negative structure process reward models need. As a GRPO reward, the trajectory receives the outcome reward zero plus a step penalty −λ for the verified-wrong S4, so it is distinguished from a trajectory that is wrong from S1 onward, which outcome-only training cannot do. The same package also spawns the validation side of the pipeline: injecting the S4 error deliberately into the ground-truth derivation, along with sign flips, dropped factors, and wrong limits, produces the known-error instances on which the verifier confusion matrix of Section 2.4 is measured.
